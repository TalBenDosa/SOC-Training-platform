The two secrets this lesson turns on: krbtgt and replication

Assumes Kerberos basics; two facts every attack here depends on.

HACKTHESOCLesson 39DCSync and tickets6 / 65

The Kerberos recap this lesson builds on

  • TGTA Ticket-Granting Ticket, issued once at logon by the KDC (a role every DC plays) in the AS-REQ/AS-REP exchange.
  • TGSA service ticket, traded from the TGT per service in the TGS-REQ/TGS-REP exchange.
  • The KDCThe Key Distribution Center: the ticket-issuing service that every domain controller runs.
With the Kerberos flow assumed, the lesson turns on two specific secrets: the krbtgt master key, and DC-to-DC replication.
HACKTHESOCLesson 39DCSync and tickets7 / 65
In Kerberos the password hash IS the cryptographic key.

A ticket is encrypted and signed with an account's hash, so stealing the hash works as well as the password, and no password prompt ever enters the picture.

This is why hash theft, not password cracking, is the goal of every technique here. Hold the right hash and you can read or forge the matching tickets.
HACKTHESOCLesson 39DCSync and tickets8 / 65

The two secrets this lesson weaponises

krbtgt: the master key

Every TGT in the domain is signed and encrypted with the krbtgt account's password hash. The account never logs in. Control its hash and you can stamp your own wristbands for anyone.

Replication: the disguise

DCs keep an identical directory by periodically copying what changed, including password hashes. A normal, constant, trusted process between DCs.

Because replication is DC-to-DC by design, a replication request from anything that is not a DC is the anomaly detection keys on.

Replication being trusted is exactly what the first attack, DCSync, pretends to be. Whoever controls krbtgt controls the office that stamps every wristband.
HACKTHESOCLesson 39DCSync and tickets9 / 65

krbtgt hashSigns and encrypts every TGT the KDC issuesA forged TGT is signed by the same key, so it validates like any real one

Both abuses hide inside a trusted-by-design process, so detection keys on the identity behind the action, not the action itself.

Nothing downstream re-checks a TGT that carries a valid krbtgt signature, which is why losing this one hash is a domain-level event, not a single-account one.

DCSync: impersonating a domain controller

T1003.006: ask for replication and claim to be a DC.

HACKTHESOCLesson 39DCSync and tickets12 / 65
The attacker's workstation sends a replication request and claims to be a domain controller.

No code on the DC, no scraping LSASS. If the DC believes the request is authorised, it replicates the account's secrets, including password hashes, back to the caller.

DCSync abuses MS-DRSR, the protocol two real DCs use to replicate. AD checks permissions on the domain object, not where the request came from.
HACKTHESOCLesson 39DCSync and tickets13 / 65

Three extended rights decide whether the DC complies

  • DS-Replication-Get-ChangesRead normal, non-secret replicated attributes.
  • DS-Replication-Get-Changes-AllThe dangerous one: also read secrets like password hashes.
  • Get-Changes-In-Filtered-SetNeeded for filtered attribute sets, such as RODC scenarios.

By default only Domain Admins, Enterprise Admins, and DC computer accounts hold these.

An attacker reaches DCSync by compromising an account that has them, or by finding an ACL misconfiguration where an over-broad 'IT support' role was delegated them by mistake. BloodHound surfaces exactly that edge.
HACKTHESOCLesson 39DCSync and tickets14 / 65

Why DCSync is quieter than dumping LSASS

Scraping LSASS

Needs code running on the target, often trips EDR, and only yields the secrets cached on that one host right now.

DCSync replication

Runs from the attacker's own machine, touches no DC console, and can pull every account's hash in a single replication call.

On the network it looks like one more replication conversation, so the subject identity, not a payload, is the tell.

This is why there is no reliable network signature: the detection lives in the Event 4662 audit record on the DC, keyed on who asked.
HACKTHESOCLesson 39DCSync and tickets15 / 65

DCSync of krbtgt is the most damaging use: one replication call for that single object hands over the key that signs every TGT in the domain.
HACKTHESOCLesson 39DCSync and tickets16 / 65
On the wire it is a DC-to-DC conversation; the identity behind it gives it away.

It never signs in at a DC console nor reads LSASS, so the account and source host, not the traffic, are what mark a replication request as an attack.

Steal krbtgt once and mint wristbands forever. A single replication call can pull every account's hash in one pass.

The Golden Ticket: forging a TGT

T1558.001: build a TGT offline with the stolen krbtgt key.

HACKTHESOCLesson 39DCSync and tickets18 / 65
A forged TGT is byte-for-byte as trustworthy as a real one, with the KDC never involved.

A real TGT is trusted because it is signed with the krbtgt key. Hold that key and you produce your own, needing only the domain SID, the krbtgt key, and a target username.

The username can even be one that does not exist in AD, since nothing checks it at use time.
HACKTHESOCLesson 39DCSync and tickets19 / 65

What goes into a forged Golden Ticket

  • The krbtgt keyThe stolen master key that signs the TGT, so the KDC is never involved in issuing it.
  • Domain SIDIdentifies the domain; easy to read from any authenticated session.
  • A username and RIDAny name, even RID 500 (Administrator) or one that does not exist in AD.
  • Injected group RIDsRID 512 (Domain Admins) is asserted in the PAC, not looked up in AD, then used immediately.
Group memberships are asserted in the ticket's PAC, not looked up in AD. The domain SID is easy to get from any authenticated session.
HACKTHESOCLesson 39DCSync and tickets20 / 65

Normal TGT issuance versus a forged Golden Ticket

Same ticket on the wire; the difference is whether the KDC was ever involved.

Legitimate TGT

The KDC authenticates the account, looks up its real groups, signs the PAC, and issues a TGT with the policy lifetime, about 10 hours.

Forged Golden Ticket

Signed offline with the stolen krbtgt key, so the KDC never issued it: groups are asserted, the account is never verified, and the lifetime can be years.

A TGT lifetime far outside the ~10-hour policy is a strong tell, since no legitimate issuance produces one.

This contrast is the detection mindset: you cannot see the forging, so you watch for the artefacts it cannot avoid, above all an impossible lifetime.
HACKTHESOCLesson 39DCSync and tickets21 / 65

Why a Golden Ticket is 'game over'

  • Any service, any hostTrusted domain-wide, so any TGS request built on it succeeds.
  • Survives a password resetThe ticket never checks the impersonated account's current password.
  • Impersonates fake accountsDisabled or nonexistent: nothing in forging queries AD to confirm the account is real.
  • Arbitrary lifetime and SID HistoryDefault tools used a 10-year lifetime; it can smuggle privileged SIDs into the PAC.

The only fix: rotate the krbtgt password twice.

A multi-year lifetime, wildly outside the normal ~10-hour policy, is a strong tell. One rotation is not enough: AD keeps the previous key alive for replication, so a second retires it.
HACKTHESOCLesson 39DCSync and tickets22 / 65
You cannot revoke a Golden Ticket; you can only invalidate the key that signs it.

Because the ticket never re-contacts the KDC to be re-authorised, disabling the impersonated account does nothing. Rotating krbtgt twice is what breaks the signature.

Internalise this now: the response to a confirmed Golden Ticket is a krbtgt double rotation, covered in the defence section.

The Silver Ticket: forging a service ticket

T1558.002: a forged TGS, no krbtgt and no DC needed.

HACKTHESOCLesson 39DCSync and tickets24 / 65
A Silver Ticket is handed straight to the target service, which validates it with its own key.

The attacker steals or cracks one service or computer account's hash, then forges a TGS for that service offline. The DC never sees the transaction.

Every domain-joined computer has its own machine account hash, which is what a HOST or CIFS ticket is encrypted with. The most durable target is a human-managed service account whose password is rarely rotated.
HACKTHESOCLesson 39DCSync and tickets25 / 65

What a forged Silver Ticket targets

  • One service account's keyThe stolen or cracked hash of the service or computer account, not the krbtgt key.
  • A service classcifs for shares, host for remote management, mssqlsvc for SQL: the service the ticket is good for.
  • One target hostThe TGS is forged for a single service on a single server, e.g. fs01.
  • No DC contactThe service validates the ticket with its own key, so there is no DC-side request to log.
Because the DC is never contacted, there is no DC-side request to log, which is why Silver Tickets are stealthier than Golden Tickets.
HACKTHESOCLesson 39DCSync and tickets26 / 65

PropertyGolden TicketSilver Ticket

The invalidation row is the operational payoff: the response differs by ticket type, so classify which one you are facing before you act.
HACKTHESOCLesson 39DCSync and tickets27 / 65

Which Silver-ticket target lasts longest

A computer account

A domain member auto-rotates its machine-account password every 30 days by default, so a stolen machine hash goes stale on its own within a month.

A human service account

A human-managed service account behind an SPN, left unchanged for years, gives a forged Silver Ticket near-permanent life. A member with auto-rotation disabled is itself a finding.

gMSA accounts auto-rotate long passwords, which is why the defence section recommends them for service accounts.

The PAC inside every ticket

The structure that makes a forged ticket powerful, and how Microsoft hardened it.

HACKTHESOCLesson 39DCSync and tickets29 / 65
The ticket is a passport (identity); the PAC is the visa pages stapled inside (what you may do).

The PAC carries the user's SID, group memberships as RIDs, and SID History. A real DC fills it by looking up the account; a forger simply types in whatever groups they want.

The PAC (Privilege Attribute Certificate) is why a forged ticket asserting Domain Admins (RID 512) works even for an account in no such group.
HACKTHESOCLesson 39DCSync and tickets30 / 65

Why the PAC is the dangerous part of the ticket

  • It carries authorisationThe PAC, not the ticket wrapper, is what tells a service the holder's groups and privileges.
  • A real DC looks it upLegitimately the PAC is populated by querying the account object in AD.
  • A forger skips the lookupForging asserts the groups directly, so nothing confirms the account or membership is real.
This is the single idea behind every forged ticket: the privilege is claimed, not verified, and the krbtgt signature makes the claim look official.
HACKTHESOCLesson 39DCSync and tickets31 / 65

The PAC fields a forger simply types in

A real DC fills each by looking up the account; a Golden Ticket asserts them.

PAC fieldFilled on a real DC byForged value
User SIDLooking up the account objectAny RID, even 500 or one that does not exist
Group RIDsReading the account's real memberships512 (Domain Admins) asserted, never checked
SID HistoryMigration history, usually emptyPrivileged SIDs smuggled in to widen access
The lookup is what forging skips, which is exactly why the krbtgt key is catastrophic to lose: a valid signature over an invented PAC is indistinguishable at use time.
HACKTHESOCLesson 39DCSync and tickets32 / 65
The KDC signs the PAC, and the krbtgt key is the master key to that lock.

The signature was meant to stop tampering, but with the krbtgt key an attacker produces a valid KDC signature over any PAC they invent.

Historically two signatures: a server signature keyed by the service key, and a KDC signature keyed by krbtgt. This is why stealing krbtgt is catastrophic.

Beyond Golden and Silver: Diamond and Sapphire

Forgeries that start from a real, DC-issued ticket to erase the tells.

HACKTHESOCLesson 39DCSync and tickets35 / 65
A Golden Ticket is a counterfeit passport; a Diamond edits a genuine one; a Sapphire staples in a real VIP's visa pages.

Both Diamond and Sapphire start from something the office truly issued, so the 'it is not in our records' check no longer fires.

The classic Golden tells (orphan 4769, absurd lifetime, RC4) worked because it was forged offline. These variants defeat that.
HACKTHESOCLesson 39DCSync and tickets36 / 65

The forged-ticket family compared

TicketHow the PAC is obtainedKey evasion property
GoldenForged from scratch, offline, with krbtgtPowerful but leaves orphan-4769 / lifetime / RC4 tells
SilverForged offline with a service account keyNever contacts the DC at all (no 4768/4769)
DiamondEdit the PAC of a real TGT, re-sign with krbtgtHas a matching 4768 and realistic fields, looks DC-issued
SapphirePull a real privileged PAC via S4U2self+U2U (krbtgt)Most legitimate-looking: every element is genuine
Diamond and Sapphire buy stealth, not independence from krbtgt.
HACKTHESOCLesson 39DCSync and tickets37 / 65

Diamond versus Sapphire, how each dodges the tells

Diamond

Starts from a real TGT, so a genuine 4768 exists. The attacker alters the PAC and re-signs, giving realistic lifetimes and encryption that defeat the classic Golden heuristics.

Sapphire

Requests a real privileged account's PAC through legitimate Kerberos features (S4U2self + U2U), so every field is genuine and the request looks entirely normal.

Both still derive their trust from the krbtgt key, which is why krbtgt rotation remains the kill switch.
HACKTHESOCLesson 39DCSync and tickets38 / 65

What Diamond and Sapphire mean for defence

  • krbtgt is still the root of trustRotating krbtgt twice remains the definitive kill switch for Golden, Diamond, and Sapphire.
  • Shift to behavioural analysisThey ride legitimate 4768s, so lean on privilege, unexpected group SIDs, and host-mismatch anomalies.
  • PAC hardening counters itThe Nov-2022 extended signature raises the cost of Diamond's core move: editing an existing PAC.
Detection must move from 'is this ticket in our records?' to anomaly analysis, since Diamond and Sapphire ride on real ticket requests.

DCSync and ticket forging as one continuous incident.

HACKTHESOCLesson 39DCSync and tickets40 / 65

One incident, stage by stage, and the event each leaves

  1. 1DCSync the krbtgt hashReplicate krbtgt's secrets. On dc01: Event 4662 naming svc-helpdesk02, replication GUIDs, non-DC source.
  2. 2Forge a Golden TicketA TGT is produced offline with the stolen krbtgt key, Domain Admins in its PAC. No DC event: built in memory.

The scenario starts from a helpdesk account mistakenly delegated DS-Replication-Get-Changes-All, found via BloodHound recon.

Read this as a correlation story, not four isolated alerts: the 4662 is the earliest signal, the orphan 4769 is confirmation, the privileged access is the blast radius.

Catching step 1 stops everything downstream.

The 4662 is the earliest, highest-fidelity signal; the missing-4768-before-4769 is the confirmation; the privileged actions are the blast radius.

Detection: the events that betray forgery

4662 for DCSync, 4768/4769 for Golden, host logs for Silver.

HACKTHESOCLesson 39DCSync and tickets43 / 65
You can never see the forging itself; you watch for the artefacts it cannot avoid.

Forging happens offline, in attacker memory. Detection is built entirely from the events the forged ticket produces when it is used, and from the one replication call that stole the key.

Keep this frame: every indicator below is an artefact of use or of theft, not of the forge. That is why anomaly and correlation, not signatures, win here.
HACKTHESOCLesson 39DCSync and tickets44 / 65

DCSync: Event 4662 with the replication GUIDs

  • 1131f6aa is Get-Changes; 1131f6ad is Get-Changes-All
  • A non-DC subject invoking these is a high-fidelity DCSync signal
Requires object auditing (SACL) on the domain object, or 4662 never fires, so verify audit policy first. Correlate with flow data: MS-DRSR replication is DC-to-DC only, so a workstation source is anomalous on its own.

Benign replication versus a rogue 4662

Real DC replication

Subject is a DC computer account (ends in $) or a known Entra Connect sync account. It recurs on schedule, every few minutes, between the DCs.

Rogue DCSync

Subject is a user or workstation that is not a DC, exercising Get-Changes-All against the domain object, appearing out of nowhere with no replication history.

The discriminator is the subject identity plus whether the source is a real DC, not the GUIDs alone.

This benign-versus-malicious read is the core triage skill: the same GUIDs appear in both, so the account and source decide it.
HACKTHESOCLesson 39DCSync and tickets48 / 65

Golden Ticket tells in 4768 and 4769

  • Orphan 4769A service ticket request with no matching preceding 4768 for that logon session.
  • Anomalous lifetimesFar exceeding Kerberos policy: the classic multi-year artifact of default tooling.
  • Encryption downgradeRC4 (0x17) where AES is normal (0x12 AES256, 0x11 AES128).
  • Mismatched account fieldsUsernames that do not resolve in AD, or a SID that does not match the domain.

Silver Tickets bypass the DC, so pivot to the target host's own logs.

On the target: 4624 with Authentication Package Kerberos, plus 4672, with no matching 4768/4769 on the DC, indicates a service ticket the KDC never issued.

  • 0x17 is RC4, a downgrade where AES (0x12/0x11) is the norm
  • No preceding 4768 for this session on any DC is the orphan tell
Read this against the Golden tells: the encryption type and the missing TGT request are the two fields that move a single 4769 from routine to suspect. Confirm the AccountName resolves in AD.

Missing 4768 before a 4769 only means forgery when both would land on the same DC.

In a multi-DC domain, DC1 can issue the 4768 while DC2 serves the 4769. A per-DC query then shows an orphan 4769 for entirely legitimate traffic.

Correlate 4768/4769 across all DCs in one window, keyed on the logon session, before calling a missing 4768 a forgery. Lean on lifetime and encryption-type anomalies, which do not depend on which DC logged what.

Triage runbook for a suspected forged-ticket alert

  1. 1Confirm the 4662 sourceIs the subject a DC computer account or a known sync account? If not, treat it as live DCSync.
  2. 2Scope the pullCheck whether krbtgt or many accounts were replicated in one pass; that sets the blast radius.
  3. 3Correlate forwardHunt orphan 4769s, odd lifetimes, RC4, and host 4624/4672 with no DC ticket.
  4. 4Attribute the accessWhich principal and host held Get-Changes-All, and was it delegated by mistake?
  5. 5Escalate and containIsolate the source host, disable the account, open an IR ticket at high severity.
Run this top to bottom: the first step rules out benign replication, the middle steps size the incident, the last step hands a clean picture to IR.

Defence: protecting the trust root

Keep krbtgt and privileged hashes out of attacker hands.

HACKTHESOCLesson 39DCSync and tickets54 / 65

Four defensive levers

Protect and rotate krbtgt

Rotate on a schedule and twice after any suspected DA compromise. The double rotation is mandatory because AD retains the previous (N-1) key.

Administrative tiering

Tier 0 (DCs, AD) credentials never log on to Tier 2 workstations where they can be scraped from LSASS. This starves DCSync of the accounts it needs.

Harden credential exposure

Credential Guard isolates secrets; add sensitive accounts to Protected Users and mark them not-delegatable; prefer AES and disable RC4.

Audit replication rights

Review who holds Get-Changes-All; alert on any new grant. Use gMSA so service passwords auto-rotate, and watch for unexpected SID History.

Making RC4 usage itself an alert is a free win once AES is the norm. Restrict SeEnableDelegationPrivilege too.
HACKTHESOCLesson 39DCSync and tickets55 / 65

Why the krbtgt rotation is two resets, spaced apart

  1. 1First resetAD keeps the previous (N-1) key, so one reset leaves Golden Tickets valid against it.
  2. 2Wait for replicationLet the new key reach every DC. Confirm convergence, do not assume it.
  3. 3Wait out TGT lifetimeLet the max TGT lifetime elapse (default 10h) so in-flight legit tickets expire naturally.
  4. 4Second resetNow invalidate the retained N-1 key, and with it any forged ticket.
  5. 5ValidateConfirm replication health on all DCs; a burst of Kerberos failures means you rotated too fast.

Tooling such as New-KrbtgtKeys enforces this replication-aware spacing automatically.

The spacing is the whole point: reset twice back-to-back and you either break active sessions or, if you skip the wait, risk an inconsistent key across DCs.
HACKTHESOCLesson 39DCSync and tickets56 / 65

Responding to a confirmed krbtgt compromise

  • Rotate krbtgt twiceThe only definitive kill switch; space the two resets per the procedure so replication converges.
  • Reset exposed secretsReset any service and computer account whose hash the single DCSync pass could have pulled.
  • Scope the blast radiusAssume every account replicated in that pass is compromised; prioritise Tier 0 and service accounts.
  • Hunt for persistenceLook for forged tickets still in use, rogue nTDSDSA objects, and new replication-right grants.
Order matters: rotate the trust root first to stop new forgeries, then reset downstream secrets and hunt for what the attacker already planted.

Inside MS-DRSR: DCSync versus DCShadow

The exact RPC call, and a related technique that writes instead of reads.

HACKTHESOCLesson 39DCSync and tickets58 / 65

What a DCSync replication call does under the hood

  1. 1RPC bind to drsuapiBind to the replication interface (UUID e3514235-4b06-11d1-ab04-00c04fc2dcd2) on the DC.
  2. 2Call IDL_DRSGetNCChangesOpnum 3, naming the target object, e.g. CN=krbtgt,CN=Users,DC=corp,DC=local.
  3. 3Present the calling contextThe compromised account, e.g. svc-helpdesk02, holding Get-Changes-All rights.
  4. 4Receive replicated secretsThe DC returns replicated attributes, including unicodePwd, the NT hash.

IDL_DRSGetNCChanges is the exact call real DCs make every few minutes; only the caller's identity and permissions mark it as an attack.

A caller supplies an NC (naming context) and a USN watermark; the DC returns every change since, including secrets if rights allow. This is why Event 4662, not a network signature, is the reliable detection.
HACKTHESOCLesson 39DCSync and tickets59 / 65

Key takeaways

  1. 01A TGT is a domain-wide wristband, a TGS a single-service pass; the krbtgt hash signs every TGT, so stealing it owns the trust root.
  2. 02DCSync (T1003.006) abuses replication rights to pull hashes without touching LSASS, logging Event 4662 from a non-DC source.
  3. 03Golden Tickets (T1558.001) forge a TGT with the krbtgt key; only rotating krbtgt twice truly invalidates them.
  4. 04Silver Tickets (T1558.002) forge one service's TGS and never touch the DC, so detection shifts to 4624/4672 on the host.
  5. 05Diamond and Sapphire ride real 4768s to evade orphan-ticket tells, but krbtgt rotation still defeats them.
  6. 06Triage keys on the subject identity behind a 4662, then correlates 4768/4769 across all DCs before calling forgery.
