3am, an alert you have never seen, alone on shift. This is the moment playbooks exist for.

A playbook is the judgement of calm, rested people, written down so the hardest decisions are not made for the first time by the most tired person in the building.

Section 1 of 8

What a playbook is, and what it is not

Decision logic for a type of incident, defined by the fact that it branches.

HACKTHESOCLesson 35SOC playbooks6 / 57

Three documents beginners constantly confuse

DocumentScopeShapeAnswers
IR planWhole organisationGovernanceWho is allowed to decide?
PlaybookOne incident TYPEBranchingWhat should be decided?
RunbookOne taskLinearWhich buttons perform it?

Tells you what to click: runbook. What to decide: playbook. Who decides: IR plan.

NIST SP 800-61r2 calls the IR plan the foundation, with playbooks as the operational layer on top. If you write an if-statement into a runbook, you have a small playbook.
HACKTHESOCLesson 35SOC playbooks7 / 57

One alert, three layers of documents

Playbooks call runbooks. The playbook decides containment is warranted; the runbook tells you which buttons perform it.
HACKTHESOCLesson 35SOC playbooks8 / 57

"Not covered" means one of two escalations

Decision logic ran out

Nobody wrote down what to do next. The case goes to a more senior analyst or the playbook owner.

Authority ran out

You know exactly what should happen, you are just not allowed to approve it. It goes to the approver the IR plan names.

Playbooks also separate containment (stop the spread) from eradication (remove what was left), with different people allowed to do each.

The rest of the lesson keeps asking you to tell these two apart, because they go to different people.
HACKTHESOCLesson 35SOC playbooks9 / 57

Why writing it down beats improvising each time

  • ConsistencyTwo analysts on the same alert reach the same outcome, not two different guesses made under pressure.
  • CompletenessThe closure list catches the half-handled incident: the forwarding rule that nobody remembered to remove.
  • DefensibilityThe ticket records which version was followed, so a review can separate a bad step from a skipped one.
  • SpeedAt 3am you execute a decision made earlier by calm, rested people instead of inventing one from scratch.
Speed is the obvious benefit, but consistency, completeness and defensibility are the deeper reasons a SOC writes playbooks at all.

Section 2 of 8

Anatomy of a playbook: collect, decide, act

Know the sections so you can read any playbook fast, and spot a missing one.

HACKTHESOCLesson 35SOC playbooks11 / 57

Read the header first: it tells you how far to trust the rest

  • VersionFor example 4.2. Tickets record which version was followed, so a review can tell a bad playbook from a skipped step.
  • OwnerA named role such as SOC Lead or Detection Engineering, not a person who may leave. Deviation notes and edits go here.
  • Last reviewed dateReview intervals are often six or twelve months, plus after every incident. Well past it: contacts and console paths may be wrong.
  • Linked detections and runbooksThe alert rule names that route here, and the runbooks each step calls.
The header is the first thing to read on any playbook, before the first step.
HACKTHESOCLesson 35SOC playbooks12 / 57

Collect, decide, act: the spine under every section

Collect

Gather the evidence the playbook names before forming any conclusion: sign-ins, mailbox rules, MFA changes, consents. A conclusion formed first is a guess.

Decide

Walk the decision tree. Each branch reads a named field, so the same evidence sends every analyst to the same ending.

Act

Contain, eradicate, recover, within the authority the table grants you. Anything above your row is an escalation, not a solo move.

Every playbook section slots into one of these three acts; learn the spine and you can read any playbook fast.

Hold this three-act spine in mind for the rest of the section: the header, the collect list, the tree and the authority table each belong to one act.
HACKTHESOCLesson 35SOC playbooks13 / 57

The sections every good playbook has

  • Trigger and scopeThe detection rules that route here, and near-neighbours that do not.
  • Severity and SLAThe clock: first touch, containment, closure. It tells you when waiting becomes escalating.
  • Required dataEvidence gathered before you may conclude anything.
  • Containment and authorityWhat to do and who may do it. Blast radius differs by account.
  • Escalation criteriaNamed conditions and a named role, not 'escalate if it seems bad'.
  • Documentation and closureWhat must be recorded, and what must be true before you close.
The decision tree is the heart of the document. Closure conditions are why playbooks catch the incidents that get half-handled.

The decision tree: every question answers from a named field

A branch that says only 'if it looks suspicious' is not a branch, and it is where two analysts start reaching different outcomes.
HACKTHESOCLesson 35SOC playbooks17 / 57

Reading the tree: each question reads one named field

A branch is only a branch if it tests a field, not a feeling.

QuestionField it readsWhy it decides
Sign-in successful?ResultType = 0A failed sign-in is spray or noise, not a takeover
Was it the user?DeviceDetail, LocationA known device from a real location closes it benign
Persistence left?New-InboxRule, MFA, OAuthA new forward rule or MFA method confirms compromise
Scope exceeded?account count, roleMore than 3, or a privileged role, stops and escalates
This is the difference between a tree an auditor trusts and one two analysts walk differently: every node names the exact field it reads.
HACKTHESOCLesson 35SOC playbooks18 / 57

Who may act: the authority table in RACI form

  • R Responsible (does it), A Accountable (signs off), C Consulted, I Informed
  • Your row says C or blank: that is the authority flavor of escalation
The authority escalation is decided in advance here, so nobody negotiates it at 3am.
HACKTHESOCLesson 35SOC playbooks19 / 57
Collect before you decide. Decide before you act.

Containment is often irreversible and always visible to the adversary. You can always contain later; you can never un-contain and recover destroyed evidence.

Disable an account and the attacker burns access elsewhere where you cannot watch. Reimage a host and memory is gone. Under active ransomware the ordering compresses, but it does not invert.

Section 3 of 8

Executing one end to end: a compromised account

The compromised-account playbook mapped onto the NIST lifecycle phases.

HACKTHESOCLesson 35SOC playbooks21 / 57

The four NIST SP 800-61r2 phases at the keyboard

  1. 1PreparationLogging on, the rule existing, the playbook written, you having the permissions. Missing access is a preparation failure.
  2. 2Detection and AnalysisPull 7 days of sign-ins (IPAddress, Location, DeviceDetail, ResultType), MFA changes, inbox rules, OAuth consents.
  3. 3Containment, Eradication, RecoveryRevoke sessions, remove the forwarding rule, rogue consent and attacker MFA method, then verify normal service.
  4. 4Post-Incident ActivityThe phase most SOCs skip, and the only one that makes next month easier.
Rev 3 (April 2025) recasts IR around the six CSF 2.0 functions, but the four phases still map onto Detect, Respond and Recover and are the clearest way to learn. A forward-and-delete inbox rule is one of the strongest single indicators here.
HACKTHESOCLesson 35SOC playbooks22 / 57

There is no free containment action

Disable the account

Stops the attacker, but destroys visibility, tells them they are detected, and locks out a real person who may be mid-deadline.

Revoke sessions + reset

Usually the better first move: invalidates stolen tokens without necessarily announcing an incident response.

Isolate the host

Preserves memory and cuts C2, but stops the user working. In a hospital or factory that is not small.

Containment is always a trade. The playbook records in advance who may make that trade, at what scale.

An attacker who registers their own MFA method has turned a stolen password into durable access, which is why MFA changes are on the collect list.
HACKTHESOCLesson 35SOC playbooks23 / 57

The decision tree, branch by branch

  1. 1User confirms it was them?Yes and evidence agrees: close as benign true positive, document why.
  2. 2No, or unreachableNew inbox rule, new MFA method or new OAuth consent? If none: monitor, recheck in 24h.
  3. 3Yes: confirmed compromiseCheck scope: more than 3 accounts, a privileged role, or mail exfiltration?
  4. 4Scope exceededSTOP and escalate to the IR manager: scope exceeds this playbook.
  5. 5Within scopeRevoke sessions, reset, remove rule, revoke consent and rogue MFA, recover, close.
Walk the class through each branch with the same alert, showing how different evidence sends it to different endings.
HACKTHESOCLesson 35SOC playbooks24 / 57

What each containment action buys, and what it costs

The authority table says who; this is why the choice is never free.

ActionWhat it stopsWhat it costs you
Revoke sessions + resetInvalidates the stolen token nowLittle; the quiet, usual first move
Disable the accountAll attacker use of the identityVisibility, and it tips the attacker off
Isolate the hostCommand and control, lateral spreadThe user cannot work; it preserves memory
Revoke-and-reset is usually first because it is cheap and quiet. Disabling and isolating are heavier trades the authority table reserves for higher tiers.

Worked example: one case in the analyst's notes

Impossible travel for d.kaplan: Bucharest 14 minutes after Tel Aviv.

HACKTHESOCLesson 35SOC playbooks26 / 57

T+0 to T+6: open the ticket, then collect without concluding

Notice the analyst records what was found, not yet what it means. Nobody can travel between those cities in 14 minutes, so one sign-in is very likely not the user.
HACKTHESOCLesson 35SOC playbooks27 / 57

Reading that evidence before drawing any conclusion

Each line has an innocent reading; the context is what still alarms.

Line collectedInnocent readingWhy it still alarms
Sign-in from BucharestA real trip or a VPN exit node14 minutes after Tel Aviv: nobody travels that fast
Unmanaged new deviceA personal laptop the user ownsNever seen before, paired with a new country
New inbox ruleA tidy-inbox filter the user set upForwards externally, marks read, then deletes
This is the discipline of collect-before-decide: hold each innocent reading open until the fields together rule it out. The forwarding rule is what tips it to confirmed.
HACKTHESOCLesson 35SOC playbooks28 / 57

T+9 to T+15: decide, contain, eradicate

  • A conclusion formed before collection is a guess in a playbook's clothing
  • Tier 1 may revoke and reset a non-privileged account with no approval
Containment stopped the bleeding; eradication removed what the attacker actually left behind.
HACKTHESOCLesson 35SOC playbooks29 / 57
Containment stops the bleeding; eradication removes what the attacker left behind.

Revoking sessions ends the access. Removing the forwarding rule, any rogue OAuth consent and any attacker-registered MFA method is what stops them simply walking back in.

Analysts who stop at containment leave the persistence in place. The second forwarding rule or a lingering consent is how the same account is compromised again a week later.
HACKTHESOCLesson 35SOC playbooks30 / 57

T+40: close only when the closure conditions are met

Technical work ended at T+15, but closure required user contact and a written timeline. No deviation was needed because all five deviation conditions were false. That is what a working playbook looks like: not exciting, fully documented.

Section 5 of 8

When to deviate, and why silence is the failure

A playbook is a floor, not a ceiling.

HACKTHESOCLesson 35SOC playbooks32 / 57

Five moments when the next step is the wrong step

  • Evidence breaks an assumptionThe identity is a service principal with no mailbox and no human to phone. Half the steps are meaningless.
  • Scope is largerTwelve accounts sharing an indicator is a different incident, not one incident twelve times.
  • Blast radius exceeds authority'Disable the account', but it runs payroll integration for the whole company.
  • Different incident, same triggerSign-ins are legitimate; a departing employee is downloading the customer database. Insider case: legal and HR.
  • Next step destroys evidence'Reimage', but the malware is memory-resident and nobody has captured memory yet.
Playbooks encode what was true when written. Attackers change technique because defenders write things down, and environments change as businesses grow.
HACKTHESOCLesson 35SOC playbooks33 / 57
Deviation is legitimate. Silent deviation is not.

Write down what you did instead, write down why, and tell someone senior now, not at the end.

These three duties are mandatory the moment you step outside the playbook.
HACKTHESOCLesson 35SOC playbooks34 / 57

Two failure modes with one root

Off a cliff

Disables the payroll account at 3am because step seven said so, or reimages away the only evidence. Following a procedure that stopped matching reality is not diligence.

Silent freelancer

Does something smarter and tells nobody. Even if right, the SOC cannot reproduce it, the next analyst inherits an undocumented case, the playbook never gets fixed.

You owe the playbook your attention, and your colleagues your reasoning whenever you leave it.

Both treat the playbook as something to comply with, rather than a shared instrument to maintain.
HACKTHESOCLesson 35SOC playbooks35 / 57

The three duties the moment you step off the playbook

  1. 1Document what you did insteadRecord the action you took in place of the written step, in the ticket, as you take it, not from memory later.
  2. 2Document whyThe evidence or constraint that made the next step wrong: a service principal with no mailbox, a payroll dependency.
  3. 3Tell someone senior nowEscalate the deviation in the moment, so a second mind can catch a mistake while it is still cheap to fix.
The point of telling someone now, not at closure, is that a deviation under pressure is exactly where a wrong call is most likely and most correctable.

Section 6 of 8

How playbooks improve, and what SOAR should automate

Did the playbook hold? Every incident ends with that question.

HACKTHESOCLesson 35SOC playbooks37 / 57

The lifecycle loops back through lessons learned

  1. 1PreparationLogging, tooling, playbooks, access.
  2. 2Detection and AnalysisCollect evidence, form a conclusion.
  3. 3Containment, Eradication, RecoveryAct within authority.
  4. 4Post-Incident ActivityPlaybook confirmed, or amended with a new step, branch or escalation trigger, with an owner and a date.
An incident that neither confirms nor amends the playbook was a wasted incident. Check last-reviewed dates: a playbook untouched for two years is abandoned, not stable.
HACKTHESOCLesson 35SOC playbooks38 / 57

Automation is safe in proportion to reversibility

SOAR: Sentinel playbooks on Azure Logic Apps, Splunk SOAR, Cortex XSOAR.

Automate

If enrichment is wrong, the cost is a wasted API call.

  • Reputation lookups on IP, hash, domain
  • User's manager, department, 30 days of sign-ins
  • Ticket creation, dedup, assignment, notification
  • Evidence attachment

Keep human

Being wrong is expensive and someone must be accountable.

  • Irreversible containment
  • Anything with legal, HR or privacy weight
  • Judgement calls under ambiguity
Automate the gathering. Keep the deciding.
HACKTHESOCLesson 35SOC playbooks39 / 57
The real disaster: an automated action on a false positive, at machine speed, across the fleet.

A misfire on a legitimate deployment isolates every host that ran it: four thousand endpoints off the network in ninety seconds.

Guardrails: cap blast radius at N assets then pause, require human approval above that, make every action reversible and logged, and test against historical false positives first.
HACKTHESOCLesson 35SOC playbooks40 / 57

A Sentinel SOAR playbook with a human approval gate

  • Enrichment is automatic because a wrong lookup costs nothing
  • Privileged identities route to a human: the authority table says IR manager
  • A timeout does nothing, so silence never triggers an irreversible action
The gate records who approved, which keeps the accountability the IR plan requires. SOAR here means Sentinel playbooks built on Azure Logic Apps.
HACKTHESOCLesson 35SOC playbooks41 / 57

The guardrails that make SOAR containment safe

Each one blocks a specific way automation turns a false positive into an outage.

GuardrailWhat it prevents
Cap blast radius at N assets, then pauseA false positive isolating the whole fleet at machine speed
Human approval above the capAn irreversible action taken with nobody accountable
Every action reversible and loggedA mistake that cannot be undone or reconstructed later
Test against historical false positivesShipping a rule that would have misfired on last month's data
The approval gate in the previous slide is one of these guardrails; a mature SOAR deployment layers all four so no single misfire can cascade.
HACKTHESOCLesson 35SOC playbooks42 / 57
Read MTTR per playbook, not for the SOC as a whole.

If compromised-account cases close in 45 minutes and malware cases take six hours, the malware playbook is pointing at itself.

MTTR is the average from alert to verified closure. A slow playbook usually means an ambiguous branch, an evidence list too long for the severity, or vague escalation criteria, which turns 'this case type always takes forever' into a specific edit for the next review.

The playbook library and incident categories

Same anatomy template, filled in differently by category.

HACKTHESOCLesson 35SOC playbooks44 / 57

NIST SP 800-61 Rev 1 incident categories

These five are the Rev 1 categories; Rev 2 replaced them with attack vectors (email, web, impersonation, loss/theft).

  • Unauthorized AccessLogical or physical access without permission. The compromised-account playbook lives here.
  • Denial of ServicePrevents or degrades authorized use by exhausting resources.
  • Malicious CodeVirus, worm, trojan executes. Ransomware is the highest-stakes member today.
  • Inappropriate UsageAcceptable-use violations, shading into insider threat; pulls in HR and Legal.
  • Multiple ComponentSpans two or more categories: most serious modern intrusions.
Initial access often looks like unauthorized access, followed by malicious code for persistence, sometimes followed by denial of service.

Why a ransomware playbook is not a relabeled account playbook

Compromised accountRansomware

Collect before act?Yes, holds firmlyBends: isolate first if encryption is actively spreading

Notification clocks like GDPR's 72 hours start at actual knowledge, not case close. Deleting a dropper without learning what second stage it fetched leaves the real question open.

HACKTHESOCLesson 35SOC playbooks48 / 57

Same anatomy, three things changed

Unit of scope

A message across many mailboxes, not one identity, so the first collection step is a blast-radius query.

Ends in a handoff

A recipient who typed a password is a compromised-account problem: one phishing case can spawn several linked child cases.

Looser collect-before-act

Removing an email copy is cheap and easy to justify; disabling an identity is not.

Set beside the compromised-account playbook, the phishing playbook shows how much the same anatomy can change.

A scenario card, as a facilitator runs it

SP 800-84 elements: an objective, a realistic scenario, a facilitator with injects, and the step most organizations under-invest in, a written after-action report.

A document does not object when a phone number is wrong. A room trying to dial it does.

Key takeaways

  1. 01Runbook says what to click, playbook what to decide, IR plan who decides.
  2. 02Collect before you decide, decide before you act: containment is often irreversible and always visible.
  3. 03Deviate on the five triggers, but never silently: document what, why, and tell someone senior now.
  4. 04Read the header first, branch only on named fields; containment is a trade, and the authority table says who may make it.
  5. 05In SOAR, automate enrichment, gate containment behind a named approver with a do-nothing timeout, read MTTR per playbook.
  6. 06Phishing scopes by message across mailboxes and hands credential-entry users to the compromised-account playbook.

Check your understanding, question 1 of 3

A document titled 'Network Contain a Host in CrowdStrike Falcon' lists exact console steps in a fixed order, with no decision points or branches. What kind of document is it?

A runbook is the how-to for one task and is linear, with no branches because there is no judgement. A playbook branches across an incident type; an IR plan covers roles and authority, never console steps.

Twenty minutes into a single-user compromised-account playbook, you find eleven other accounts with the same suspicious OAuth consent granted in the same hour. What is the correct move?

Scope exceeding the playbook is a named deviation trigger. Twelve accounts sharing an indicator is a different incident; repeating the procedure loses the pattern, and disabling all twelve is irreversible action beyond Tier 1 authority.
