An attack category is a label for how an attack works. A threat actor is the person or group behind it.

Together they give the analyst a first guess about what an alert means, like triage at a hospital ER: a hypothesis, revised as tests come in.

The ER nurse sorts chest pain one way and a broken wrist another without a full diagnosis. Categories do the same job in a SOC.

Threat actors and what drives them

Motive predicts targets, patience and tooling.

Five actor types, five motives

An attacker who wants money picks whoever pays quickly. One who wants secrets stays for years.

Actor typeMain motiveTypical targetPatience
Nation-state / APT groupEspionage, strategic advantage, sabotageGovernments, defense, telecom, research, suppliersMonths to years
Financially motivated cybercrimeMoneyAny organization that can payDays to weeks
HacktivistPolitical or ideological messageVisible targets tied to a causeHours to days

Motive is the most useful single fact about an actor. Ask students which row they expect to see most often in a beginner queue.
HACKTHESOCLesson 127Attack categories, actors7 / 53

Crime has a supply chain; hacktivists and insiders differ

Initial access broker

A criminal who breaks in and sells the access to someone else. CISA: precursor malware operators "will often sell access to a network".

Double extortion

The buyer may steal data and threaten to publish it. Scattered Spider (G1015) impersonates IT help-desk staff to bypass MFA.

Hacktivists

Want attention, so they pick loud, public attacks. ENISA reports DDoS increasingly aimed at EU public administrations.

Insiders

CISA: anyone with authorized access who harms the organization by "intentional or unintentional acts". Perimeter tools see nothing wrong.

Ransomware gangs rarely do every step themselves. Scattered Spider is also tracked as UNC3944 and Octo Tempest. An insider can be the careless employee who emails a spreadsheet to a personal account, not only a malicious one.
HACKTHESOCLesson 127Attack categories, actors8 / 53

Three questions about any actor

  • MotiveWho benefits if this succeeds: money, secrets, publicity or revenge?
  • TargetSpecific (suggests APT or insider) or random (suggests an opportunist or a crime gang)?
  • PatienceOne noisy burst, or slow, careful activity?
These three questions turn an anonymous alert into a hypothesis about who is behind it.
HACKTHESOCLesson 127Attack categories, actors9 / 53

Worked example: one IP sweeping port 3389

One external IP tries ten internal hosts on port 3389, then a new range, all within an hour.

  • One IP, many hosts, port 3389, seconds apart: scanning for exposed remote desktop
  • Not a patient state actor: check whether any internal host answered
The category sets the urgency. Do not hunt for a long hidden campaign here; check whether anything answered.

Advanced Persistent Threat: what each word means

APT has a precise meaning, and each word does work.

HACKTHESOCLesson 127Attack categories, actors11 / 53

Advanced, Persistent, Threat: three words, three claims

APT is often used as a synonym for "scary hacker". It is not.

  • AdvancedResources beyond a lone hobbyist: custom malware, non-public vulnerabilities, funding, a specialist team.
  • PersistentWants to stay: plants several ways back in, avoids noise, returns after eviction. Dwell time is months.
  • ThreatA human adversary with intent and a goal, not just a piece of code.

A burglar grabs a television. An APT copies your keys and lives in the attic for a year.

Advanced does not mean every technique is sophisticated: many APT intrusions start with an ordinary phishing email. Dwell time is the days between first intrusion and detection.
HACKTHESOCLesson 127Attack categories, actors12 / 53

How MITRE ATT&CK names groups

Every vendor invents its own label, so each group has an ID, a primary name and aliases.

NameMITRE IDWhat to know
APT29G0016Aliases incl. NOBELIUM, Cozy Bear, Midnight Blizzard; attributed to Russia's SVR
APT32G0050Suspected Vietnam-based, focused on Southeast Asia, frequent strategic web compromises
menuPass (APT10)G0045Targeted managed IT service providers
UNC + numberMandiant labelUncategorized cluster, not yet confidently tied to a known group

Names differ between vendors, so always look up the MITRE ID.

APT29 is described as active since at least 2008, with the SVR attribution from US and UK government statements in April 2021. Targets include European and NATO government networks, research institutes and think tanks.
HACKTHESOCLesson 127Attack categories, actors13 / 53

APT activity is small anomalies that fit together

It rarely trips a loud alert.

  • Valid accountsUsed at odd hours or from impossible locations.
  • Admin toolsPowerShell or remote management used where they never were before.
  • BeaconingSlow, regular outbound connections to rarely seen domains.
  • Cloud persistenceNew mailbox rules, new OAuth application consents or new federation trusts.

Ask: one odd event or a chain? Scope backwards from the earliest related event. Suspected APT usually means escalate.

Beaconing is a periodic call-home. The key move is to look for a chain of events sharing a user, host or destination.
HACKTHESOCLesson 127Attack categories, actors14 / 53

Worked example: sign-in, then a forwarding rule

A service account signs in from a new country at 02:11. Eight minutes later it adds a mailbox rule.

A pattern of persistence and collection. It deserves escalation even though no malware alert fired.

Section 3 of 9

Denial of service and DDoS: attacks on availability

Nothing is stolen or changed. The service is simply unreachable.

HACKTHESOCLesson 127Attack categories, actors16 / 53

Three DDoS families, three things exhausted

DoS exhausts bandwidth, connection slots or processing power. Many machines attacking together make it DDoS.

FamilyWhat it exhaustsExample
VolumetricBandwidthUDP floods, reflection and amplification
ProtocolConnection tables, firewall and load balancer stateSYN flood
ApplicationServer work per requestThousands of HTTP requests for a heavy search page

DoS attacks availability, one leg of the CIA triad. Like a crowd blocking a shop entrance.

CISA groups DDoS into these three families. A SYN flood abuses the TCP handshake: the attacker never completes it, so half-open connections fill the server's table.
HACKTHESOCLesson 127Attack categories, actors17 / 53

Botnets, reflection and amplification

Botnet

A network of compromised devices obeying one controller. Mirai infected routers, cameras and recorders using 62 default login pairs.

  • Attacks above 620 Gbps in 2016

Reflection

The attacker sends requests with the victim's forged source address to a public server, which replies to the victim.

Amplification

The reply is far bigger than the request. The victim gets a lot from thousands of innocent servers.

  • DNS: 28 to 54
  • NTP: 556.9
  • Memcached: 10,000 to 51,000
The figures are CISA's bandwidth amplification factors. Memcached uses UDP and TCP port 11211. The attacker spends little bandwidth.
HACKTHESOCLesson 127Attack categories, actors18 / 53

What the SOC sees during a DDoS

  • Traffic jumpSudden rise in inbound packets or bits per second, often on one destination.
  • Many sourcesMany source IPs hitting one URL, or one IP sending millions of SYNs.
  • WAF and CDN alertsRate limit hits and bot challenges.
  • Reflector portsLarge inbound UDP from source ports 123, 53 or 11211.
  • User slownessCISA warns maintenance or faults cause the same symptoms, so confirm with traffic data.
Slowness alone proves nothing. Confirm with traffic data before declaring an attack.
HACKTHESOCLesson 127Attack categories, actors19 / 53

Worked example: SYNs without handshakes

  • Almost no ACKs completed out of millions of SYNs: handshakes are not finishing
  • 212,000 distinct sources: a protocol-level DDoS
  • First question: is the upstream provider or scrubbing service engaged?
The first question is not who, but whether the business impact is on this service only or on shared infrastructure.

Section 4 of 9

Man-in-the-middle: sitting inside the conversation

The attacker secretly sits between two parties and can read or alter their traffic.

HACKTHESOCLesson 127Attack categories, actors21 / 53

Four forms of man-in-the-middle

MITRE now calls it Adversary-in-the-Middle (T1557). Confidentiality (reading) and integrity (changing) are at risk.

FormHow the attacker gets in the middleATT&CK
ARP spoofingFalse ARP replies map the gateway IP to the attacker's MAC addressT1557.002
Rogue Wi-Fi / evil twinSame SSID as a real network, often with a stronger signalT1557.004
TLS interceptionA forged or downgraded certificate so encrypted traffic can be readUnder T1557
AiTM phishingA fake login page relays to the real one and steals the session after MFAPhishing and AiTM lessons
Think of a dishonest postal worker who opens, copies and reseals every letter. ARP is stateless and needs no authentication, so a device accepts a forged reply. In AiTM phishing the attacker keeps the session cookie, so MFA alone did not stop it.
HACKTHESOCLesson 127Attack categories, actors22 / 53
HTTPS helps but is not a cure: certificate errors are real evidence, not noise.

TLS stops an interceptor reading traffic unless the user clicks through a certificate warning or the attacker's certificate is trusted by the device.

Teach students never to dismiss a surge of certificate warnings as a user problem.
HACKTHESOCLesson 127Attack categories, actors23 / 53

What the SOC sees during a MITM attack

  • Wireless IPSRogue access point or duplicate SSID with an unexpected BSSID.
  • ARP monitoringMultiple IPs resolving to one MAC, or unsolicited (gratuitous) ARP replies.
  • Switch logsDynamic ARP Inspection drops, the control MITRE recommends along with DHCP snooping.
  • Certificates and sign-insSurges of certificate warnings, or one user signing in from two places moments apart.
BSSID is the access point's MAC address.
HACKTHESOCLesson 127Attack categories, actors24 / 53

Worked example: the gateway MAC flip-flops

  • A flip flop: the MAC claiming the gateway keeps changing between two values
  • First question: which MAC is the real router, and which switch port holds the other?
Two devices are fighting over the gateway address.

Zero-day versus n-day vulnerabilities

Unknown to the vendor, or already public: the difference changes the defense.

HACKTHESOCLesson 127Attack categories, actors26 / 53

Zero-day and n-day side by side

Zero-day: the vendor has had zero days to fix it. N-day: public, so defenders have had n days to act.

PropertyZero-dayN-day
Known to the vendorNoYes
Patch availableNoUsually yes
Who uses itWell-funded actors, mostlyEveryone, including opportunists
DefenseBehavior detection, hardening, rapid responsePatching, exposure management, signatures

A CVE identifier, such as CVE-2024-3094, is the public label for a known bug.

NIST defines a zero-day attack as one that exploits a previously unknown hardware, firmware or software vulnerability.
HACKTHESOCLesson 127Attack categories, actors27 / 53
Signatures match what someone has already seen. A new exploit has none.

What still shows up is behavior: a web server starting a command shell, a document reader writing an executable, a service account touching files it never touched.

This is why EDR tools watch process behavior. Antivirus and classic IDS rules match known patterns.
HACKTHESOCLesson 127Attack categories, actors28 / 53

Most real breaches use known bugs

  • KEV catalogCISA's list of CVEs with evidence of active exploitation.
  • CISA adviceUse it as an input to prioritization: "immediately address KEV catalog vulnerabilities".
  • Known to vendorsEvery entry is a bug the vendor already knows, so attacks mostly come through unpatched known flaws.
  • Headlines vs realityA missed patch on an internet-facing VPN is the likelier entry point than a zero-day.
  • Ransomware flagEach KEV entry also shows whether it is known to be used in ransomware campaigns.

Ask: is the product exposed, which version, is the CVE in KEV, did the host act oddly before the advisory date?

Advisories often arrive after the activity started, so hunt backwards from the advisory date.
HACKTHESOCLesson 127Attack categories, actors29 / 53

Worked example: a VPN daemon spawns a shell

  • A VPN daemon spawning a shell is suspicious with or without a CVE
  • Contain first, then check whether the product has a KEV entry
Behavior is the signal here, which is why it works even if the bug is a zero-day.

Supply-chain attacks: compromise the supplier

Reach many customers by compromising something they already trust.

HACKTHESOCLesson 127Attack categories, actors31 / 53

Four public supply-chain cases

ATT&CK: Supply Chain Compromise (T1195), with sub-techniques .001, .002 and .003 for dependencies, software and hardware.

CaseWhat was compromisedDetail
SolarWinds SUNBURSTThe vendor's buildTrojanized Orion DLL, signed with a valid certificate; linked to APT29
3CXA signed desktop appTraced to an earlier X_TRADER installer compromise; UNC4736, suspected North Korea
xz-utilsAn open-source libraryVersions 5.6.0 and 5.6.1 carried a backdoor, CVE-2024-3094
MSP accessA service providerA compromised MSP can open many customer networks at once; menuPass did this
Like poisoning flour at the mill instead of attacking each bakery. xz was noticed by engineer Andres Freund because sshd logins were slower. On some Linux systems xz was loaded into the SSH server. 3CX Desktop App 18.12.416 and earlier was affected, and Mandiant called it the first cascading software supply-chain case it had seen.
HACKTHESOCLesson 127Attack categories, actors32 / 53
The malicious file arrives from the vendor you trust, often validly signed.

Hash allow-lists and signature checks pass. Detection has to rely on behavior after install.

This is the central lesson of SUNBURST: passing the trust checks did not mean the software was safe.
HACKTHESOCLesson 127Attack categories, actors33 / 53

What the SOC sees after a poisoned update

  • First-ever connectionA trusted app contacting a new domain. For SUNBURST, CISA pointed to avsvmcloud[.]com subdomains.
  • Signed binary odditiesLoads an unexpected DLL or spawns a child process it never spawned.
  • Vendor or MSP accountsOdd-hour logins, new privileges, access to many customers in a short time.
  • Performance anomaliesSuch as the added sshd delay that exposed xz.

Ask: which version, what changed before and after the update, how many other hosts, what privileges?

Scope fleet-wide: the same version on other hosts shows how many customers or machines are exposed.
HACKTHESOCLesson 127Attack categories, actors34 / 53

Worked example: a management server calls a new domain

  • A management server querying a domain it never used before
  • Enough to isolate the host and check the installed version
No malware alert is needed. The first-seen domain from a trusted product is the lead.

Section 7 of 9

Watering-hole and drive-by attacks

Wait where the victims already drink: compromise a site they visit.

HACKTHESOCLesson 127Attack categories, actors36 / 53

How a watering hole works

Drive-by compromise (T1189) is the mechanism: infection by browsing, with no attachment to open.

  1. 1Pick a site the target usesIdentify a site the target community uses, for example an industry news portal.
  2. 2Compromise the siteOften via a known vulnerability, then add a small script or hidden iframe.
  3. 3Filter the visitorsThe script checks browser, plugins and location, and sends the exploit only to matching visitors.
  4. 4Run code on the endpointA vulnerable browser runs attacker code and downloads a payload, reaching client endpoints.
MITRE lists Drive-by Compromise as an Initial Access technique, with sources including compromised legitimate sites, public cloud storage scripts, malvertising and abused browser push notifications. Groups named there include APT28, APT32, Lazarus Group and Turla.
HACKTHESOCLesson 127Attack categories, actors37 / 53
The site is legitimate and likely on the allow list, so the perimeter waves it through.

The payload may be tailored, and an attacker may require a second visit before the exploit is sent, so the first visit looks clean.

Unlike an attack on a public web server, this reaches client endpoints, so it often leads to internal network access.
HACKTHESOCLesson 127Attack categories, actors38 / 53

What the SOC sees around a drive-by

MITRE's detection guidance correlates several layers.

  • New domain or changed scriptWeb or proxy requests to a never-before-seen domain, or a change in a known script.
  • Browser child processA browser spawning a script interpreter, or loading unsigned modules.
  • Temp writes then outboundFile writes in temp directories, then an outbound connection shortly after.
  • Later identity anomaliesSuch as unfamiliar token reuse.

Several victims from one team is the signature of a waterhole.

Ask which site was visited just before the odd process, whether other users in the group visited it, and whether the browser is patched.
HACKTHESOCLesson 127Attack categories, actors39 / 53

Worked example: a first-seen script, then encoded PowerShell

A browser launching an encoded PowerShell command two seconds after a first-seen script load is the pattern to escalate. Pull proxy logs for everyone who loaded that script.

Reading the comparison table

Availability

Availability is the target only in DoS and ransomware. All other rows threaten secrecy first.

Insiders

Insiders cut across all rows. Signal: unusual use of legitimate access, such as bulk downloads, access outside the role, USB or personal cloud uploads.

Direct or indirect

DDoS, n-day and zero-day hit the target head on. Supply-chain, watering-hole and MiTM arrive through something the target trusts or shares.

Category names describe behavior, not certainty: write the category with its evidence and update it as facts change.

An alert first labeled DDoS may turn out to be a marketing campaign that drove real users to the site. The spike proved only that something was happening; user agents, country mix and request patterns decide the rest.

Five habits to take away

  1. 1Name a categoryState the evidence behind it.
  2. 2Ask for scopeHow many hosts, users or customers show the same pattern?
  3. 3Ask for timeWhen did it start, and what changed just before?
  4. 4Escalate when it fitsA patient actor or a trusted supplier suggests escalation.
  5. 5Record the hypothesisUpdate it when new data arrives.
These habits apply to every category in this lesson.

Key takeaways

  1. 01Motive predicts targets and patience: ask who benefits, whether the target is specific, and how patient the behavior is.
  2. 02APT: Advanced (resources), Persistent (stays), Threat (a human with intent); it shows as small anomalies that fit together.
  3. 03DDoS attacks availability and MITM attacks confidentiality and integrity; each leaves distinct SOC signals.
  4. 04Most breaches use known bugs, and signed vendor software can be compromised, so watch behavior after install.
  5. 05Name a category, state the evidence, ask for scope and time, and update the hypothesis as facts change.

A quiet service account signs in from a new country, then creates a forwarding rule, with no malware alerts. Which part of the APT definition fits best?

Quiet access that stays, plus a forwarding rule that collects mail, is the persistence of a patient human adversary. Advanced is about resources, and nothing here shows custom malware. Scanners are noisy; distributed means many hosts.

Check your understanding, question 2 of 3

Why is patching known vulnerabilities often a better use of effort than trying to stop zero-days?

Catalogs like CISA KEV list bugs already exploited, each with a known fix, so patching closes the most used entry point. Zero-days can still be caught by behavior detection. Signature updates do not remove a vulnerability.

A vendor's application is validly signed and passes every hash check, yet contacts a brand-new domain right after an update. What is the best interpretation?

In SUNBURST and 3CX the malicious code arrived inside signed vendor software, so signature and hash checks passed. The new post-update behavior is the signal. Signed does not mean safe. An evil twin or DDoS does not fit one app's behavior.
