What C2 is and why attackers need it

The remote-control layer of every intrusion.

HACKTHESOCLesson 22C2 and exfiltration6 / 49

The implant lifecycle

A tiny stager fetches the full implant into memory, then the loop begins.

  1. 1Check inConnect and ask: any tasks for me?
  2. 2Receive taskingEnumerate, escalate, move, collect.
  3. 3Execute and returnRun the task, send output back.
  4. 4Sleep, repeatCommonly 30 to 300 seconds active; hours when staying quiet.
Cobalt Strike's implant is Beacon; Sliver, Mythic, Havoc and Brute Ratel ship their own; Qakbot and IcedID use simpler HTTP implants.
HACKTHESOCLesson 22C2 and exfiltration7 / 49

The check-in loop an attacker configures

  • Not a detection: it shows the behavior you are hunting for
  • The wait() is periodic. Human browsing is not.
Machines keep time far more precisely than humans. That precision is the attacker's greatest liability. TA0011.
HACKTHESOCLesson 22C2 and exfiltration8 / 49

Where C2 hides: protocols you already allow

HTTPS

Port 443 is nearly always open and the payload is encrypted. The favorite.

DNS

Almost never blocked and rarely inspected deeply.

Internal and SaaS

SMB or named-pipe peer beacons so one host talks out; ICMP; Slack, Discord, Google Drive APIs.

The question is never "what port?" but "does this host, this destination, this pattern, this volume make sense?"

Severing the channel is why containment playbooks prioritize network isolation.

Beaconing: the rhythm that gives C2 away

The single most reliable behavioral tell of an active channel.

HACKTHESOCLesson 22C2 and exfiltration10 / 49

Machines keep time; humans burst

Legitimate automation exists, but it is usually low-frequency, well-known and destined for vendor infrastructure you can allow-list.
HACKTHESOCLesson 22C2 and exfiltration11 / 49

Jitter blurs the rhythm, but cannot hide it

What jitter does

60-second sleep with 30% jitter lands check-ins between 42 and 78 seconds, defeating exact-period detections.

Why it fails

Intervals still cluster tightly. Coefficient of variation (stdev/mean) stays often below 0.2; human traffic is well above 1.

Add supporting signals: high connection count, tight payload sizes, a destination rare in your environment.

False positives: NTP, monitoring agents, CRL/OCSP checks (allow-list them), and pairs with too few samples (minimum-count threshold).
HACKTHESOCLesson 22C2 and exfiltration12 / 49

Beacon hunt via time-delta standard deviation

T1071 Application Layer Protocol. Allow-list NTP, monitoring and OCSP before alerting.

Worked example: a beacon in raw proxy logs

FINANCE-07 (10.14.22.108) made unusually many connections to one destination.

HACKTHESOCLesson 22C2 and exfiltration14 / 49

Six lines, one destination

Every line has src=10.14.22.108; it is dropped here for width. Ask the class to compute the deltas before the next slide.
HACKTHESOCLesson 22C2 and exfiltration15 / 49

Reading it step by step

  1. 1Deltas by hand62, 63, 58, 63, 58 s. Mean about 60.8, spread only 5 s: CV well under 0.2.
  2. 2Byte sizesbytes_out 485 to 493, bytes_in 1198 to 1255. The same small check-in every time.
  3. 3Context3 a.m., and 185.221.94.7 never appears in 90 days of this host's history.
  4. 4Confirm independentlyEDR: which process owns the connection? TLS: does the JA3 match known C2 tooling?

Rhythm, context and one independent source must agree before you call it a confirmed beacon.

Do not stop at 'regular, therefore malicious': a monitoring agent can look periodic too.

What TLS does not hide

  • JA3Hash of the Client Hello's ciphers, extensions and curves: fingerprints the TLS library, not the destination.
  • Known implantsCobalt Strike's default stager, Sliver and many Go implants have documented JA3s; match against abuse.ch SSLBL.
  • Rare in your estate99% of traffic uses a dozen JA3s; one host with a unique one is worth a look.
  • Certs and processSelf-signed or nonsense certs, fresh SNI domains, 443 from rundll32.exe or a temp-path binary.
Pair any TLS oddity with the originating process from EDR. A browser is expected; rundll32 is screaming.

Staging and exfiltration

A short pipeline: collect, stage, package, transfer. Each stage is detectable.

HACKTHESOCLesson 22C2 and exfiltration21 / 49

How data actually leaves

  1. 1Collection (TA0009)Copy shares, dump a database, harvest email, screen-grab.
  2. 2Staging (T1074)Fan-in: many file reads converging on writes in one folder, often temp or AppData.
  3. 3Archive (T1560)7z.exe, rar.exe or Compress-Archive, often with a password; large new archive.
  4. 4TransferOver C2, over an alternative protocol, or to cloud storage.
Office or a script spawning 7z is far more suspicious than a user double-clicking WinRAR. Attackers may rename the archive extension.

Detecting exfil: the four questions

You cannot inspect every byte, so ask the right statistical questions.

HACKTHESOCLesson 22C2 and exfiltration25 / 49

Ask these of flow and proxy data

  • Byte ratio inverted?Clients normally download. bytes_out / bytes_in over 10 with tens or hundreds of MB out is bulk egress.
  • Destination new?A finance host that never touched this IP suddenly uploading 4 GB is a near-certain incident.
  • Timing wrong?A 2 a.m. Saturday upload from a 9-to-5 weekday machine is worth waking up for.
  • DLP or CASB agree?Content-aware hits on the same host and window turn a suspicious flow into confirmed egress.

No single signal is decisive. Seniority is in the correlation.

A large upload could be a backup, a new destination a new vendor, off-hours a scheduled job. Together they stop being guesses.
HACKTHESOCLesson 22C2 and exfiltration26 / 49

Exfil hunt via byte ratio, volume and novelty

Then correlate the same src_ip and time window against DLP and CASB alerts. Always output pivot fields for the next analyst.

C2 frameworks and the malleable beacon

A huge share of intrusions run on a small number of toolkits.

HACKTHESOCLesson 22C2 and exfiltration28 / 49

The frameworks and their habits

Cobalt Strike

Legitimate red-team tool, now the most abused C2. Implant: Beacon. Deeply configurable.

Sliver, Mythic

Growing as attackers move away from heavily-signatured Cobalt Strike. Meterpreter still appears.

Malleable profiles

Reshape traffic to mimic a legitimate service, like a fake jQuery request. Camouflage of content.

Jitter hides a 60-second beacon short-term; over hours the regularity re-emerges statistically.

They differ in detail but share a shape: a beacon that calls home, receives tasks and returns results.

DNS tunnelling down port 53

The protocol almost never blocked and rarely inspected.

HACKTHESOCLesson 22C2 and exfiltration31 / 49
In aGVsbG8gd29ybGQ.attacker-domain.com, the subdomain is the stolen data.

The attacker runs the authoritative DNS server, so every query is delivered to them, and the answers carry commands back.

Slow, so used for stealthy C2 and low-and-slow exfil rather than gigabytes. Stealth is the point.
HACKTHESOCLesson 22C2 and exfiltration32 / 49

Tunnelled DNS looks nothing like human DNS

Human DNS

Normal name resolution.

  • Short, readable names like mail.google.com
  • Mostly A records
  • Lookups followed by a connection

Tunnel

The lookup itself is the payload.

  • Long, high-entropy labels
  • Thousands of queries to one domain
  • TXT, NULL or CNAME heavy
  • Lookups with no follow-on connection
DNS records intent; here the intent is the data.

Domain fronting and C2 over trusted services

Borrow someone else's reputation so traffic never looks out of place.

HACKTHESOCLesson 22C2 and exfiltration34 / 49

Two ways to hide behind trust

C2 over SaaS

Commands and results through Slack, Discord, Telegram, GitHub, Google Docs/Drive or a paste site. Just HTTPS to an allow-listed service.

Domain fronting / CDN

Visible SNI names an innocent CDN site; the real host inside the encrypted request is the attacker's. Curtailed, but domain borrowing continues the idea.

A raw IP on port 4444 trips a rare-destination rule. The domains your whole company uses all day give that rule nothing to grab.
HACKTHESOCLesson 22C2 and exfiltration35 / 49
"It went to a trusted domain" is not exoneration.

Fall back on behavior: metronomic rhythm, byte ratios and timing, JA3, and rarity for this host. Does this machine use GitHub's API at 3 AM every 90 seconds?

Attackers moved from hiding where they talk to hiding how they look while talking. The analyst's edge moved with them.

Key takeaways

  1. 01No C2 means no operation, so network isolation is the fastest way to neutralize a live intruder.
  2. 02Hunt beacons by the coefficient of variation of time deltas per src, dst, port; it stays low despite jitter.
  3. 03Fingerprint channels: JA3 and process for HTTPS, long high-entropy TXT-heavy queries for DNS tunnels.
  4. 04Exfil is a pipeline: staging, archiving, then T1041 over C2, T1048 alternative protocol, or T1567 cloud.
  5. 05Correlate inverted byte ratio, new destination, off-hours timing and DLP; no single signal convicts.
  6. 06A trusted destination is not exoneration: fall back on rhythm, byte ratio, JA3 and per-host rarity.
