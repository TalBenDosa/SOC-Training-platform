802.11 fundamentals: names, bands and channels

The radio link between a station and an access point, and the names that get confused.

HACKTHESOCLesson 106Enterprise wireless6 / 54

Four names that get confused

802.11 covers Layers 1 and 2 only: the radio link between a client (STA) and an AP.

TermMeaningExample
SSIDHuman-readable network name, up to 32 bytes, carried in beacons and probesCORP-WIFI
BSSIDMAC address of one AP radio for one SSID; identifies one BSS4c:77:6d:1a:02:b1
BSSOne AP radio plus the clients associated to itAP-B2-F3-014 on 5 GHz
ESSSeveral BSSs sharing one SSID, joined by the wired network, so clients can roamEvery AP that broadcasts CORP-WIFI
These confusions cause real investigative mistakes. Ask the class which of the four identifies a physical radio; the answer is the BSSID.
HACKTHESOCLesson 106Enterprise wireless7 / 54

Correlate by BSSID, never by SSID alone

  • SSID alone merges APsAn attacker AP that copies the name looks identical to the legitimate one.
  • BSSID identifies the radioThe BSSID, and the OUI (vendor prefix) inside it, identifies the physical radio.
  • One AP, many BSSIDsEach SSID on a radio has its own BSSID, and the 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz radios differ too.
  • A hidden SSID is no secretThe name still appears in probe requests and association frames, so any sniffer reads it.
The BSSID is usually the base MAC with the last bits incremented for each extra SSID. This is the foundation of evil twin detection in section 7.

Three bands, three trade-offs

BandSpectrumTypical widthsWhat to remember
2.4 GHz2.400 to 2.4835 GHz20 MHzChannels 1, 6, 11 only (US); best range, most interference
5 GHzAbout 5.15 to 5.85 GHz20, 40, 80, 160 MHzMany channels; DFS channels (about 52 to 144) vacate on radar
6 GHz5.925 to 7.125 GHz20 to 320 MHzWi-Fi 6E and 7 only; WPA3 (or OWE) is mandatory

Interference on 2.4 GHz comes from Bluetooth, microwave ovens and neighbours.

Generations map to bands: 802.11n (Wi-Fi 4) on 2.4 and 5, 802.11ac (Wi-Fi 5) on 5 only, 802.11ax (Wi-Fi 6, and 6E with 6 GHz) and 802.11be (Wi-Fi 7) on all three.
HACKTHESOCLesson 106Enterprise wireless9 / 54
Higher frequency means shorter range and weaker wall penetration, so a laptop at the edge of a floor may hear only the 2.4 GHz radio.

Wider channels give more throughput but fewer non-overlapping channels, so dense deployments use 20 or 40 MHz.

Questions to ask: which band and channel was the client on, is the BSSID in the AP inventory, and does the client hardware even support that band?

Half-duplex access and the connection steps

How stations share the air, and the sequence every client follows to join.

HACKTHESOCLesson 106Enterprise wireless11 / 54

CSMA/CA: listen, back off, send, wait for an ACK

A radio cannot transmit and listen on the same channel at once, so it avoids collisions instead of detecting them.

  1. 1Listen firstThe station senses the channel before it sends anything.
  2. 2Back off if busyWhen the medium is busy, wait a random backoff time.
  3. 3TransmitOptional RTS/CTS lets a station reserve the air.
  4. 4Expect an ACKEvery unicast frame must be acknowledged.
  5. 5Retry without an ACKNo ACK means a collision is assumed; the retry bit is set in the next frame.
This is the opposite of old wired Ethernet, which could detect collisions. CSMA/CA stands for carrier sense multiple access with collision avoidance.
HACKTHESOCLesson 106Enterprise wireless12 / 54

Airtime, not bandwidth, is the scarce resource

  • Slow clients cost everyoneOne client stuck at a low legacy rate occupies the channel longer and slows everybody.
  • High retry percentageIn AP statistics it means interference or poor signal.
  • Hidden node problemTwo clients hear the AP but not each other; RTS/CTS helps.
  • Management frames are openBeacon, probe, authentication, association, deauthentication and disassociation are unencrypted by default.
  • PMF (802.11w) closes the gapProtected Management Frames fix this for unicast management traffic.
Unencrypted management frames are the root of several attacks in section 7. Remember this slide when you reach deauth floods.
HACKTHESOCLesson 106Enterprise wireless13 / 54

The five-step connection sequence

Ask at which step the client stopped; the step tells you which team and which log to check.

  1. 11. ScanPassive: hear beacons (about every 102.4 ms). Active: Probe Request, then Probe Response.
  2. 22. 802.11 authenticationOpen System, 2 frames, no real security. With WPA3-SAE they carry commit and confirm.
  3. 33. AssociationRequest lists rates, capabilities, RSN element. Status 0 = success, plus an AID.
  4. 44. Security exchangeEnterprise: 802.1X/EAP with RADIUS. Then the 4-way handshake installs the keys.
  5. 55. Controlled port opensThe client sends DHCP and gets an IP in the WLAN's VLAN.

Step 2 proves nothing about identity: a client can be associated and still have no network access.

Terminology pitfall: real authentication happens in step 4, either EAP or knowledge of the PSK proven in the handshake.

Autonomous APs, lightweight APs and CAPWAP

Why enterprises split the work between thin APs and a central controller.

HACKTHESOCLesson 106Enterprise wireless16 / 54

Autonomous AP versus lightweight AP plus controller

Complete device

Autonomous AP

Carries its own configuration, runs its own security and bridges clients straight onto the wired VLAN. Ten APs means ten devices.

  • Configuration per device
  • Manual radio tuning
  • Weak, per-device roaming
  • Policy drifts between devices

Split-MAC

Lightweight AP + WLC

The LAP keeps real-time work: beacons, probe responses, ACKs, encryption and retransmissions. The WLC takes the rest.

  • Central configuration
  • Automatic radio tuning (RRM)
  • Controller-coordinated roaming
  • Built-in rogue detection

The trade-off: the controller is a critical dependency, while an autonomous AP failure hits only one AP.

The WLC handles client authentication and association management, security policy, WLAN-to-VLAN mapping, radio resource management and roaming. APs scan off-channel for rogues.
HACKTHESOCLesson 106Enterprise wireless17 / 54

CAPWAP (RFC 5415) builds two UDP tunnels

CAPWAP is the protocol between the AP and the controller.

TunnelUDP portCarriesProtection
Control5246Join, configuration, monitoring, firmwareDTLS, always
Data5247Client traffic encapsulated to the WLCDTLS optional

CAPWAP adds header overhead, so MTU and fragmentation problems cause strange partial failures.

Ports 5246 and 5247 are the numbers to block or permit on a firewall. Do not confuse them with RADIUS ports 1812 and 1813.
HACKTHESOCLesson 106Enterprise wireless18 / 54

How a lightweight AP joins a controller

  1. 1Get an IPThe AP obtains an address by DHCP.
  2. 2Discover a controllerDHCP option 43, a DNS name, a broadcast, or a stored list.
  3. 3Send a Discovery RequestPick a controller from the responses.
  4. 4Build the DTLS control tunnelThe AP uses its certificate.
  5. 5Join, download firmwareFirmware is downloaded only if needed.
  6. 6Receive configurationThe AP is configured by the WLC.

Will not join? Check option 43 or DNS, UDP 5246/5247 on firewalls, the clock, regulatory domain, AP license limit.

A wrong clock breaks certificate validity, a classic cause of a silent join failure. Work through the foot list in order when an AP will not join.

          A WLAN binds an SSID, a security method and a VLAN

          Vendors use different menus, but the content looks like this.

          • The WLAN decides which VLAN the clients land in
          • The AP group overrides CORP-WIFI to VLAN 140 in the lab
          Read the WLAN-to-VLAN mapping first: that single idea answers where a client lands. The AP group line shows how the same SSID can land elsewhere.
          HACKTHESOCLesson 106Enterprise wireless22 / 54

          AP groups and two classic VLAN pitfalls

          • AP groupA named set of APs that broadcast a chosen list of WLANs and can override the VLAN for a WLAN.
          • Same SSID, different subnetCORP-WIFI can land on a different VLAN and subnet in building B than in building A.
          • RF profilesPer-group radio settings, such as which bands are enabled and the minimum data rate.
          • VLAN missing from the trunkClients associate and authenticate, then get no DHCP lease.
          • RADIUS overrideA returned Tunnel-Private-Group-ID silently overrides the WLAN default VLAN.
          Ask: which WLAN and AP group did the client hit, and was the VLAN set by the WLAN default or by a RADIUS override?
          HACKTHESOCLesson 106Enterprise wireless23 / 54

          Three roaming types: the client decides, the network helps

          TypeSituationWhat happens
          Intra-controllerBoth APs joined to the same WLCThe WLC updates which AP serves the client; IP stays the same
          Inter-controller, Layer 2Different WLCs, same VLAN and subnet on bothClient entry moves to the new controller within a mobility group
          Inter-controller, Layer 3Different WLCs, different subnetsControllers tunnel traffic to the anchor controller so the client keeps its IP
          The Layer 3 case is the quiz scenario: the original controller stays the anchor and the foreign controller forwards traffic through the mobility tunnel.
          HACKTHESOCLesson 106Enterprise wireless24 / 54

          Fast roaming avoids a slow 802.1X re-authentication

          • PMK and opportunistic key cachingReuse the earlier authentication.
          • 802.11r (Fast Transition)Pre-computes keys for the target AP.
          • 802.11k and 802.11vLet the AP suggest candidate neighbors to the client.
          • Why it mattersA full 802.1X re-authentication on every roam takes hundreds of milliseconds and breaks voice calls.

          A burst of association events for one client MAC across many AP names in seconds is usually roaming, not an attack.

          This is the false-positive guard for deauth and association alerts: judge by reason codes and failures, not raw counts.

          Section 5 of 9

          Wireless security: WEP to WPA3 and the 4-way handshake

          Why each older design is broken, and what WPA3 changes.

          HACKTHESOCLesson 106Enterprise wireless26 / 54

          Why each older wireless protocol is dead or dying

          Radio crosses walls, so security must protect confidentiality, not only access.

          • OpenNo cipher: everything readable by anyone nearby. Acceptable only for guest with other controls.
          • WEPRC4 with a 24-bit IV. IVs repeat, so the key falls from tens of thousands of captured packets in minutes.
          • WPARC4 with TKIP. Transitional and deprecated.
          • WPA2AES-CCMP. Still widespread, and secure if the key is strong.
          • WPA3AES-GCMP, with SAE for Personal. Current, and PMF is required.
          An analyst should know why each is dead, not just that it is. WEP in particular fails because the short IV repeats.
          HACKTHESOCLesson 106Enterprise wireless27 / 54

          WPA3-Personal removes the offline guessing path

          FeatureWPA2-PersonalWPA3-Personal
          Key setupPSK turned into a key with PBKDF2SAE, a password-authenticated key exchange
          Offline guessing from capturesPossibleNot possible; each guess needs a live exchange
          Forward secrecyNo: a later-learned passphrase decrypts old capturesYes
          Management frame protectionOptionalMandatory
          Open networksNo protectionOWE (Enhanced Open) encrypts without a password

          Transition mode (WPA2 and WPA3 on one SSID) means a downgrade keeps WPA2 weaknesses in play.

          SAE stands for Simultaneous Authentication of Equals. Questions to ask: which security mode does the SSID use, is PMF required, and is a transition mode keeping WPA2 alive?
          HACKTHESOCLesson 106Enterprise wireless28 / 54

          The 4-way handshake builds fresh keys without sending the

          Both sides start from a shared Pairwise Master Key (PMK).

          • The SSID is part of the key derivation
          • The MIC in message 2 proves the client knew the PMK
          With Enterprise the PMK comes from the EAP exchange, and with WPA3 SAE produces it. The PTK is the per-session key; the GTK covers broadcast traffic.
          HACKTHESOCLesson 106Enterprise wireless29 / 54
          With a PSK, anyone who captures messages 1 and 2 can test passphrase guesses offline, because a correct guess reproduces the MIC.

          The password never crosses the air, yet this leak drives the attacks later in the lesson and is the reason WPA3 exists.

          Pause here. Section 7 builds directly on this: capture messages 1 and 2, then guess offline, with no trace on the network.

          The three roles in 802.1X

          The client and the RADIUS server speak EAP (RFC 3748), carried in EAPOL over the air and in RADIUS from the WLC.

          The client

          Supplicant

          The client software that presents the credentials.

            The AP or WLC

            Authenticator

            It relays and enforces. It does not decide.

              RADIUS

              Authentication server

              The RADIUS server decides whether the client is accepted or rejected.

                WLC and RADIUS share a secret. UDP 1812 authentication, 1813 accounting, 3799 CoA (disconnect or re-policy a live session).

                A shared secret mismatch makes the server discard requests with no user-visible reason. We return to this in the worked example.
                HACKTHESOCLesson 106Enterprise wireless33 / 54

                The 802.1X and RADIUS exchange, end to end

                • The WLC relays EAP to RADIUS; the server decides
                • Calling-Station-Id is the client MAC; Called-Station-Id is BSSID:SSID
                • Accept can carry a VLAN, which overrides the WLAN default
                These RADIUS attributes are the pivot keys you will use in logs. Because RADIUS carries the outcome and the user name, its logs are your best wireless attribution source.

                EAP methods: how the client proves identity

                MethodClient proves identity byNote
                PEAP-MSCHAPv2Username and password inside a TLS tunnelMost common; safe only if the client validates the server certificate
                EAP-TLSClient certificate and server certificate (mutual)Strongest; needs a PKI
                EAP-TTLSPassword inside a TLS tunnelSimilar to PEAP

                A client set to not validate the server certificate will hand its MSCHAPv2 exchange to an evil twin.

                Ask: which EAP method is in use, does the client check the server certificate, and which RADIUS server answered with what reject reason?

                Wireless attacks an analyst should recognize

                Many attacks never touch a firewall. Learn how each one appears in your data.

                HACKTHESOCLesson 106Enterprise wireless36 / 54

                Four wireless attacks and what each looks like

                AttackWhat the attacker doesWhat you observe
                Rogue APPlugs an unauthorized AP, often a cheap router, into a wall jackUnknown BSSID; same MAC on a wired switchport; consumer-vendor OUI
                Evil twinBroadcasts a copy of a real SSID with stronger signal; relays or harvests credentialsKnown SSID from a BSSID not in inventory, odd channel, different capabilities
                Deauth / disassocSends spoofed management frames that appear to come from the APBursts of deauth or disassoc, reason codes 1, 3 or 7, many clients at once
                PSK handshake captureCaptures messages 1 and 2 (or a PMKID) and guesses the passphrase offlineOften just a deauth burst and an instant reconnect; cracking leaves no trace
                The defender's view comes from the controller, monitor-mode APs and correlation with wired-side data. Ask: is the BSSID known, and is the radio also on the wired network?
                HACKTHESOCLesson 106Enterprise wireless37 / 54

                How the attacks chain together

                1. 1Deauth burstSpoofed management frames knock the client off the AP.
                2. 2Client reconnectsThe reconnect repeats the 4-way handshake.
                3. 3Attacker captures or steersRecords the handshake, or steers the client to an evil twin that wins on signal.
                4. 4Guess or harvestPSK guesses run offline; a fake RADIUS server targets PEAP-MSCHAPv2 exchanges.

                Against Enterprise, the targets are clients that do not validate the server certificate.

                This chain is the quiz scenario: deauth aimed at one client, then a quick reassociation on a PSK SSID without PMF.
                HACKTHESOCLesson 106Enterprise wireless38 / 54

                Detection ideas that work

                • Compare BSSIDs to inventoryAn SSID match with an unknown BSSID is the evil twin signature.
                • Correlate with the CAM tableMatch rogue radio MACs to wired data: a neighbour's AP is a nuisance, a plugged-in device is an incident.
                • Alert on deauth ratesUse a baseline, not single frames, since normal roaming creates a few.
                • A cracked PSK is invisibleTreat deauth then reconnect on a PSK SSID as a reason to rotate the key, not just log it.
                Ask: who is affected, and which credential could have been exposed? The CAM table check separates a nuisance from an incident.
                HACKTHESOCLesson 106Enterprise wireless39 / 54

                Mitigations to recommend, and a containment caution

                Mitigations

                Recommend

                Close the weaknesses the attacks rely on, and keep monitor-mode APs or a WIPS enabled.

                • Require PMF
                • Use WPA3, or Enterprise with EAP-TLS
                • Enforce server certificate validation
                • Long random PSK, 20+ characters, if needed

                Rogue containment

                Use with care

                Controller rogue containment sends deauths of its own. Use it only after legal and operational review.

                  Containment can disrupt a neighbour's legitimate network, which is why review comes first. A PSK of 20 or more characters is the fallback only if a PSK is unavoidable.

                    Four analyst pitfalls when reading wireless logs

                    • MAC randomizationPhones and laptops use a private MAC per SSID. Pivot through the RADIUS user name and DHCP hostname.
                    • Roaming noiseMany association events across AP names are normal; judge by reason codes and failures.
                    • Clock skewDiffering clocks on the WLC, RADIUS and DHCP break timelines; check time sources first.
                    • Sampling gapsA deauth flood seen by one monitor AP may not appear in client-side logs at all.
                    MAC randomization is the pitfall students hit most often: the MAC in the WLC may not match the one in an inventory.

                    Worked example: a client cannot get on the WLAN

                    Ticket: a new laptop shows CORP-WIFI but will not connect, and other staff are fine.

                    HACKTHESOCLesson 106Enterprise wireless46 / 54

                    Work down the sequence and stop at the first failure

                    The goal is to find the layer, not to guess.

                    1. 11. Does the client see the SSID?Check band support, the RF profile for the AP group, SSID spelling and the saved profile.
                    2. 22. Does association succeed?No WLC record: never reached an AP. Status 17: the AP is at its client limit.
                    3. 33. PSK or 802.1X?The security type decides what to check next.
                    4. 44. Is RADIUS reachable?Check UDP 1812 and 1813, the WLC as RADIUS client, the shared secret, the server.
                    5. 55. Authenticated, no address?Check the VLAN assigned, trunk allowance and DHCP scope.

                    A WLAN limited to 5 GHz is invisible to an old 2.4 GHz adapter.

                    Mention that a saved profile with a typo or an old security type fails silently. Move down the steps in order and stop at the first failure.

                    Steps 4 and 5: the RADIUS path and VLAN placement

                    From the WLC interface

                    Step 4: RADIUS reachable?

                    Confirm UDP 1812 and 1813 are permitted, the WLC is registered as a RADIUS client with the right secret, and the server is up.

                    • The WLC sees only timeouts

                    Key takeaways

                    1. 01Correlate wireless by BSSID, not SSID: an evil twin copies the name, and a hidden SSID still shows in probes and association frames.
                    2. 02Connection order: scan, 802.11 auth, association, security exchange, DHCP. Real authentication happens in the security step.
                    3. 03Lightweight APs keep real-time radio work; the WLC does policy and roaming. CAPWAP uses UDP 5246 (control, DTLS) and 5247 (data).
                    4. 04A WLAN binds SSID, security and VLAN; AP groups and RADIUS can override the VLAN, so ask which one placed the client.
                    5. 05WPA3 SAE blocks offline guessing and adds forward secrecy; on WPA2-Personal, deauth then reconnect can expose the handshake.
                    6. 06Name the last successful step and the first failing one; RADIUS logs are your best wireless attribution source.
