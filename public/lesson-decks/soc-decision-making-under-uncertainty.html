Section 1 of 11

The three doors: close, escalate, dig deeper

Every alert exits through one of three doors. Name the one you are aiming for.

HACKTHESOCLesson 24Decisions under uncertainty6 / 74

Three terms that carry the lesson

  • EscalationHanding a case to someone with more authority, context or mandate. A request, not a failure.
  • ConfidenceCalibrated: confirmed (observed), assessed (justifiable inference), unknown. Not the same as certainty.
  • Base rateHow often this alert type is really a problem. 2 real in 500 a month is about 1 in 250.
Base rate is why two identical-looking alerts from two different rules deserve different starting postures. A 1-in-2 rule deserves seriousness from the first look.
HACKTHESOCLesson 24Decisions under uncertainty7 / 74

The base rate sets your starting posture

RuleHistoryBase rateStarting posture
Rule A2 real in 500 fires a monthAbout 1 in 250Expect a false positive; need specific evidence to overturn
Rule BAbout half its fires are realAbout 1 in 2Take it seriously from the first look

Two identical-looking alerts from different rules deserve different starting postures.

The base rate is a prior, not a verdict. Specific evidence in front of you can and should move you.
HACKTHESOCLesson 24Decisions under uncertainty8 / 74

Two doors feel easy. The third is where the job lives

Close feels safe

Thin, boring evidence.

  • A scheduled task
  • A known admin tool
  • A login from a city the user visits monthly

Escalate feels safe

Loud, obvious evidence.

  • A confirmed malicious hash
  • An account creating admins at 3 a.m.
  • Beaconing to flagged infrastructure

Dig deeper

Genuine uncertainty. Treated as a free default, but every extra query is taken from the rest of the queue.

A SOC where everyone treats dig deeper as the safe default is a SOC where the urgent case sits unread for an hour.
HACKTHESOCLesson 24Decisions under uncertainty9 / 74

The three doors, and the bar for each

  • Digging deeper is not free: it costs every other alert in your queue
  • "Let me look around more" is not a question. Stop and pick a door.
Blowing through the budget without your questions getting sharper is the signal that dig deeper has quietly become stall.
HACKTHESOCLesson 24Decisions under uncertainty10 / 74

Timeboxing a dig, step by step

  1. 1Set the budgetFrom criticality and signal: about 5 minutes for a weak signal on a low-value asset.
  2. 2Name the questionWrite the specific question the next query will answer.
  3. 3Run it and re-askIs there now enough evidence to close or escalate?
  4. 4Check the trendAre your questions getting sharper? New evidence can justify extending.
  5. 5Budget blown, no progressDig deeper has become stall: escalate, or close with written caveats.
The timebox is not a hard stop. Its job is to make you notice when you have stopped making progress.
HACKTHESOCLesson 24Decisions under uncertainty11 / 74

Name the question before every query

Not a question

"Let me just look around a bit more." The phrasing that eats the most unaccounted-for time in a shift.

A bounded question

"Does this encoded command decode to a known deployment script, a benign admin tool, or unknown obfuscated content?" A two-minute check.

Example: encoded PowerShell from explorer.exe on a marketing laptop, Tuesday 2:15 p.m., vendor Medium.

Neither 'PowerShell is always noisy, close' nor forty minutes of reverse engineering is disciplined. Naming the question often reveals you already have enough.

Section 2 of 11

The three doors as a decision path

Dig deeper is a detour that always returns to the same question.

HACKTHESOCLesson 24Decisions under uncertainty13 / 74

Every alert routes through one question

  1. 1Alert lands in the queueAsk: is there enough evidence to decide now?
  2. 2Yes, expected activityClose, with a documented reason.
  3. 3Yes, concerning signalEscalate for more authority or context.
  4. 4No, genuine uncertaintyDig deeper, timeboxed and question-driven, then ask again.

Dig deeper is not a fourth door. It loops back until it resolves into close or escalate.

An analyst who cannot point to which arrow they are following has quietly stopped making a decision.

One bounded question turned an open-ended fishing trip into a clean exit through a door.

Signs you have quietly stopped deciding

  • You cannot say which arrow of the loop you are on right now
  • Your next query has no question attached, just "looking around"
  • The timebox has passed and your questions are no sharper
  • You are re-reading the same evidence hoping it resolves itself
Any one of these is the cue to pick a door: close with a reason, or escalate with what you have.

What counts as enough evidence

"I think this is fine" and "I can show you why" are different statements.

HACKTHESOCLesson 24Decisions under uncertainty17 / 74

The two bars are different heights

Wrongly close a true positive

The intrusion continues undetected for days or weeks and resurfaces as ransomware, a breach notification, or a call from a customer's security team.

Wrongly escalate a false pos.

A Tier 2 reviewer loses a few minutes and is mildly annoyed.

Require more evidence to close than to escalate. On the fence, tip toward escalation.

This is not permission to escalate everything; that moves the queue problem onto someone else and erodes trust in your judgment.
HACKTHESOCLesson 24Decisions under uncertainty18 / 74

The one-sentence test before you close

  1. 1Imagine the questionYour team lead asks: why did you close this?
  2. 2Answer in one sentenceIt must cite a specific field and value, not a feeling.
  3. 3You canYou have a finding. Write that sentence in the ticket.
  4. 4You cannotYou have a hunch. You do not yet have enough to close.
"I think this is fine" and "I can show you why this is fine" are different statements. Only the second lets you close.
HACKTHESOCLesson 24Decisions under uncertainty19 / 74

RDP to a finance server from an internal IP, outside business hours.

Analyst A

Closes: "RDP happens all the time, probably an admin." No evidence recorded.

Analyst B

Pulls 90 days of logon history: this account RDPs to this server every other Tuesday night, matching a documented backup check. Closes with the citation.

Same door. Only B's note survives a second look if this was the one time in fifty it was an attacker.

If B's check had shown the account never logs on at that hour, the same question would have pointed to escalate.

Finish the sentence before you close

  • Absence of obvious badness is not presence of a good explanation
  • Close reasons: change ticket, recurring task, inventoried tool, own scanner

Stuck between doors? Turn worry into a query

Vague, unanswerable

"Is this suspicious?" No single query can answer it.

Sharp, one pivot away

"Has this exact process-parent pair occurred on this host before, and how often?" Often collapses the ambiguity immediately.

Reframing a vague worry into a checkable question is the single highest-leverage habit an analyst can build.

Determining severity: an actual method

The vendor's label is a starting hypothesis; the vendor does not know your environment.

HACKTHESOCLesson 24Decisions under uncertainty24 / 74

What the vendor's severity engine cannot know

  • What the host isA disposable lab VM or your primary domain controller.
  • Whose account it isA shared service account or the CFO's.
  • What data lives thereNothing of consequence, or regulated personal and financial data.

So treat the label as a starting hypothesis, and recalculate it with your context.

The vendor computes severity with internal logic you rarely see, and without any knowledge of your environment.
HACKTHESOCLesson 24Decisions under uncertainty25 / 74

Four factors to walk every time

FactorLow endHigh end
Asset criticalityDisposable test VMDomain controller, backup server, internet-facing app, DB
Scope of impactOne host, one accountMultiple hosts or accounts, lateral movement
Certainty of detectionVague anomaly, no corroborationConfirmed malicious hash, known-bad IP, clear TTP
Data sensitivityNothing of consequenceRegulated PII, financials, trade secrets, customer records
Not a literal formula; four questions you deliberately walk through.

Marketing intern laptop

Photo upload to known cloud storage for a documented campaign, business hours. Low on all four. Stays Low-Medium.

Finance file server

Similar volume to a never-seen personal cloud account at 11 p.m. Critical asset, high data sensitivity. Real severity: High.

Recalculating every time guards against both severity inflation and severity fatigue.

Severity inflation and severity fatigue

Inflation

Every vendor "Critical" gets equal urgency. Unsustainable, and it breeds the complacency it was meant to prevent.

Fatigue

A reflexive discount on a rule that has cried wolf before. That is how a real Medium-labeled incident slips past.

A fresh four-factor pass every time protects against both.

They run in opposite directions across the same shift, which is why a fixed habit beats adjusting by feel.

Worked example: two hypotheses and a base rate

Reasoning through one alert the way you would at your desk.

HACKTHESOCLesson 24Decisions under uncertainty31 / 74

Four steps through one alert

  1. 1Start with the base rateHow often has this exact rule been right before?
  2. 2Weigh the specific evidenceEach finding, which hypothesis it favours, and how confident you are.
  3. 3Update away from the priorStrong case evidence should move you off the base rate.
  4. 4Decide and say itPick the door and state confirmed, assessed and unknown.
Students should be able to run these four steps on any alert in a few minutes.

The alert

  • H2 malicious: a phishing document that just executed a payload
  • Base rate: 4 true positives in 340 fires over 90 days, about 1 in 85
Starting posture leans H1, but a base rate is a prior, not a verdict.
HACKTHESOCLesson 24Decisions under uncertainty33 / 74

Reading this rule's base rate

What it says

4 true positives in 340 fires over 90 days: about 1 in 85. Higher than a generic "PowerShell ran" rule, because this one already requires the Word-to-PowerShell pairing.

What it does not say

That this alert is benign. A low prior means you need strong evidence to believe you found the exception, not that you ignore it once you have it.

Starting posture leans H1, the benign deployment script, but not so strongly that you skip the case evidence.

Section 6 of 11

Actions that change reality

Containment actions are interventions, and interventions can backfire.

HACKTHESOCLesson 24Decisions under uncertainty38 / 74

Each action has its own blast radius

Isolate a host

Can destroy volatile evidence, tip off a watching attacker who then accelerates elsewhere, or take down a hypervisor or shared jump box.

Disable an account

A shared service account can silently break jobs and integrations; a real employee wrongly locked out damages trust in the SOC.

Block an IP

IPs are often shared: a CDN edge, a corporate NAT gateway, shared hosting. Block a URL, domain or cert hash instead.

Ask what else lives at that address. If you are not confident, block something narrower.
HACKTHESOCLesson 24Decisions under uncertainty39 / 74

Isolating a host: when it helps and when it hurts

SituationEffect of isolation
Ransomware encrypting, live exfiltration, a hands-on attackerHelps: stops active damage now
Live-response collection not yet doneHurts: can destroy volatile evidence, especially if it triggers a reboot
Attacker watching their access in real timeHurts: they may see the session die and accelerate elsewhere
A hypervisor host or a shared jump boxHurts: takes down unrelated production work
Isolation is usually done through the EDR agent's containment feature. The question is never just "is it compromised" but "what else does cutting it off break?"
HACKTHESOCLesson 24Decisions under uncertainty40 / 74

None of the containment actions is free

Power off is highlighted because it destroys RAM with no undo.
HACKTHESOCLesson 24Decisions under uncertainty41 / 74

Disabling an account: two ways it backfires

Shared service account

Scheduled jobs, service logons and integrations break silently; the failure surfaces hours later in an unrelated system.

A real, working employee

Locked out on ambiguous evidence. If you were wrong, trust in the SOC's judgment is hard to rebuild.

Often lower blast radius than isolation, but never risk-free: check what depends on the account first.

Ask the asset or application owner before disabling anything that looks like a service account.

Blocking an IP: what else lives there?

If the IP isBlocking it breaksBlock this instead
A CDN edge nodeUnrelated sites routed through the same edgeThe specific URL or domain
A corporate NAT gatewayEvery legitimate user behind that egress pointThe specific session, account or host
A shared hosting serverHundreds of unrelated legitimate sitesThe domain, URL or certificate hash
IPs are shared infrastructure far more often than analysts assume. If you are not confident what lives there, go narrower.
HACKTHESOCLesson 24Decisions under uncertainty43 / 74

The pre-action checklist

  1. 1AuthorityIs this my mandate, or does it need sign-off from a lead, IR or asset owner?
  2. 2EvidenceHave I preserved memory, live session state and logs this action may destroy?
  3. 3Blast radius of the actionShared host, shared service account, CDN, NAT or shared hosting?
  4. 4Tip-off riskWill the adversary see it and accelerate? Is timing coordinated with the response?
An action correct on paper but taken without this checklist can create a second incident: an outage or a tipped-off adversary.

Escalation and communication

A decision to escalate is only as good as the message that carries it.

HACKTHESOCLesson 24Decisions under uncertainty50 / 74

Four answers before the reader has to ask

  • "Can someone check this out, seems weird" is not actionable
  • CONFIRMED, ASSESSED, UNKNOWN: say exactly where solid ground ends
A vague handoff wastes the twenty minutes of context you built and slows response exactly when speed matters.
HACKTHESOCLesson 24Decisions under uncertainty51 / 74

When to loop in people outside the SOC

  • Operations affectedA containment action will visibly disrupt the business: tell the asset owner.
  • An employee locked outA real person's access will be disrupted, and they will need to know why.
  • Possible notification dutyBring in legal or compliance early, not after the fact.

Too early creates noise; too late means they hear about their own incident from someone else.

An early, honest "we are still investigating" damages trust far less than silence.
HACKTHESOCLesson 24Decisions under uncertainty52 / 74

Calibrated uncertainty, not either extreme

Reflexive hedging

"Might possibly maybe be something." The reader cannot tell whether to act now or ignore it.

False confidence

Inferences stated as flat facts. Worse: decisions get built on more certainty than the evidence supports.

Calibrated

"I've confirmed X. Based on Y, I assess Z is likely, though I have not confirmed W." Reads as more competent.

Loop in owners outside the SOC when operations will be affected, an employee's access is disrupted, or a notification obligation may apply. Too early is noise; too late damages trust.
HACKTHESOCLesson 24Decisions under uncertainty53 / 74

Rewriting a hedge into calibrated language

Start from: "This might possibly maybe be something, not totally sure."

  1. 1ConfirmedWhat you observed: "powershell.exe spawned by winword.exe on WKS-2214."
  2. 2Assessed, and why"Likely a phishing payload, because the decoded command reaches an IP with no reputation."
  3. 3Unknown"Not yet confirmed whether execution succeeded."
  4. 4Need"Authorization to isolate, and a second review first."
Calibrated language reads as more competent than either hedging or false confidence, because the reader sees where solid ground ends.

Signal detection theory

From radar operators separating aircraft from noise: the math behind the base rate.

HACKTHESOCLesson 24Decisions under uncertainty55 / 74
An analyst facing a queue does the radar operator's job: decide if a blip is real or noise, fast, with a cost either way.

Signal detection theory came from mid-20th-century radar research, not security, and it maps onto triage almost perfectly.

The vocabulary on the next slides is standard in vendor docs, research papers and detection engineering.
HACKTHESOCLesson 24Decisions under uncertainty56 / 74

Four outcomes of any binary judgment

Really maliciousReally benign
You call it maliciousHit (true positive)False alarm (false positive): drives fatigue
You call it benignMiss (false negative): the one SOCs fearCorrect rejection (true negative)

Sensitivity = hits / (hits + misses). Specificity = correct rejections / (correct rejections + false alarms).

This vocabulary is standard across vendor docs, research papers and detection engineering.
HACKTHESOCLesson 24Decisions under uncertainty57 / 74

The criterion: tuning is always a trade-off

Loosen the threshold

Sensitivity up, but false alarms up too.

Tighten the threshold

False alarms down, but sensitivity down: more misses.

ROC curve

Hit rate against false-alarm rate at every threshold. Bows to top-left: strong. Hugs the diagonal: barely a coin flip.

When a rule is too noisy, ask: is it fundamentally weak, or tuned to too loose a criterion for our environment?
HACKTHESOCLesson 24Decisions under uncertainty58 / 74

Sensitivity and specificity, on real numbers

100,000 events a day, 5 malicious, 99,995 benign.

MetricValueWhat it means here
Sensitivity95%About 4.75 of the 5 real events are caught; about 0.25 is missed
Specificity99.9%About 99,895 benign events are correctly left alone
False-alarm rate0.1%About 100 benign events still fire an alert
Both headline numbers look excellent. The next slide shows what they add up to in your queue.
HACKTHESOCLesson 24Decisions under uncertainty59 / 74

Why a strong detector still floods the queue

  • About 19 of 20 alerts are noise: a fact about rare events, not a tool failure
  • Evidence sufficiency and severity recalculation are your compensation
Positive predictive value, not sensitivity or specificity alone, determines what your workday feels like.

Cognitive biases that distort SOC judgment

Unnamed biases are invisible, and "try harder" does not fix them.

Four biases and their structural fixes

BiasShows up asStructural mitigation
ConfirmationEarly "probably IT" feels satisfying, so you stop lookingGenerate at least one alternative before concluding (ACH)
AnchoringVendor "Low" pulls every later judgment toward low stakesRecalculate from the four factors before revisiting the label

  • The same two findings drove the escalation in the worked example

Automation bias: which comes first?

Tool first

Under time pressure, the EDR auto-classification, SIEM risk score or AI summary is accepted as the answer.

Evidence first

Read the raw process tree and logs, form your own view, then compare it with the tool's verdict as one input.

Research on human-AI triage finds analysts under time pressure lean on the tool more than the evidence justifies.

You cannot willpower your way past a bias you cannot feel. A process step can.

Write an alternative, recalculate before re-reading the label, read evidence before the verdict, treat each alert fresh, rotate off noisy queues.

Biases operate below conscious awareness by design, which is what makes them biases rather than ordinary mistakes.

Section 11 of 11

Analysis of competing hypotheses

Richards J. Heuer Jr., CIA: change the question to counter confirmation bias.

HACKTHESOCLesson 24Decisions under uncertainty66 / 74

Ask the opposite question

Unstructured

"Which hypothesis has the MOST evidence supporting it?" The question confirmation bias corrupts best.

ACH

"Which hypothesis has the LEAST evidence AGAINST it?" Forces disconfirmation-seeking.

Method: list hypotheses first, list all evidence, build the matrix, count inconsistencies, re-check shaky evidence.

From Heuer's book Psychology of Intelligence Analysis. The survivor of attempted demolition wins, not the one with most checkmarks.
HACKTHESOCLesson 24Decisions under uncertainty67 / 74

Heuer's method, in five steps

  1. 1List hypotheses firstBefore weighing evidence, so the first idea cannot anchor the rest.
  2. 2List all evidenceIncluding findings that seemed minor at first.
  3. 3Build the matrixMark each cell consistent, inconsistent or not applicable.
  4. 4Count inconsistenciesFewest inconsistencies wins, not most checkmarks.
  5. 5Re-check shaky evidenceIf one weak finding drives the verdict, look at it again.
The systematic pass is part of the power: it stops you reasoning from memory.

  2. 02Require more evidence to close than to escalate: a missed intrusion costs far more than a review.
  3. 03Recalculate severity from criticality, scope, certainty and data sensitivity; labels are hypotheses.
  4. 04Containment has its own blast radius: shared hosts, service accounts and CDN or NAT IPs break things.
  5. 05Escalate with observed, ruled out, hypothesis and need; say confirmed, assessed and unknown plainly.
  6. 06Low base rates make even strong detectors noisy; ACH and structural steps beat bias, not willpower.
