What a packet and a PCAP are

The basic unit of network data, and a recording of it.

HACKTHESOCLesson 76PCAP and Wireshark6 / 39

Packets travel, PCAPs record them

Packet

A small bundle carrying a piece of data plus addressing: where it came from, where it is going, which protocol it speaks.

PCAP

A packet capture saved to a file, .pcap or the newer .pcapng. A slice of network history you can replay packet by packet, long after the traffic is gone.

Like a video recorder capturing frames of what happened in a room.
HACKTHESOCLesson 76PCAP and Wireshark7 / 39

The tools, and where captures come from

Wireshark

Free graphical program to open a PCAP and explore every packet. Used to analyse.

tcpdump and tshark

Command-line relatives: tcpdump on Linux, tshark is Wireshark's CLI. Used to capture on servers.

Capture points

On a host, or at a chokepoint via a SPAN/mirror port or a network TAP. In a SOC you usually receive one from an IDS/IPS, a full-capture appliance or a responder.

A firewall log says a connection was allowed; the capture shows what was said, how often, and what it looks like.

To capture traffic you have to be able to see it. That is why placement matters.

Wireshark and the anatomy of a packet

Three panes, three zoom levels on the same data.

HACKTHESOCLesson 76PCAP and Wireshark9 / 39

The packet list already tells a story

Rows 1-2: the workstation looked up a domain. Rows 3-5: it opened TCP to the returned IP with the three-way handshake, SYN, SYN-ACK, ACK. Wireshark also colour-codes rows so anomalies stand out.
HACKTHESOCLesson 76PCAP and Wireshark10 / 39

The details pane unfolds a packet into layers

  1. 1Frame / EthernetLowest layer, with hardware (MAC) addresses.
  2. 2IPSource and destination IP: who, which machines.
  3. 3TCP or UDPPorts, what service, and for TCP the connection flags.
  4. 4ApplicationWhat was said: HTTP request, DNS query, TLS handshake.
Read top to bottom like nested folders. This is how networking really works: each packet wrapped in layers.
HACKTHESOCLesson 76PCAP and Wireshark11 / 39

Index, decoder, ground truth

Packet list (top)

One row per packet: No., Time, Source, Destination, Protocol, Length, Info. Your index for skimming candidates.

Packet details (middle)

Decodes one packet into its layers. Where you understand exactly what a packet is.

Packet bytes (bottom)

Raw hex and ASCII. Rarely needed, but shows raw content, including plaintext data in unencrypted protocols.

Most analysis lives in the list and details panes.

Section 3 of 9

Filtering: finding the needle

A few minutes of traffic can be millions of packets.

HACKTHESOCLesson 76PCAP and Wireshark13 / 39

Capture filters limit what you keep; display filters limit what you see

Capture filter

Decides what is recorded. Set before capturing, BPF syntax, e.g. host 10.0.0.5. Discards everything else permanently.

Display filter

Decides which already-captured packets are shown. Deletes nothing, change it freely. Different syntax, typed in the bar at the top.

In the SOC you mostly use display filters on a capture someone already collected.

Confusing the two is a classic beginner mistake. You can never get back what you did not capture.
HACKTHESOCLesson 76PCAP and Wireshark14 / 39

Display filters that cover most SOC work

FilterTypical SOC use
ip.addr == 10.0.0.5Isolate one host's entire conversation
ip.src / ip.dstSeparate outbound from inbound (exfil direction)
tcp.stream eq 7Isolate a single connection before Follow Stream
http.request.method == "POST"Spot data being uploaded or submitted
dns.qry.name contains "xyz"Find lookups of a specific or known-bad domain
tls.handshake.type == 1Client Hello: read SNI, fingerprint the client (JA3)
frame contains "password"Quick hunt for plaintext secrets in the clear

Start broad, then AND (&&) more conditions to narrow. Also || (or) and ! (not).

Others from the lesson's reference: tcp.flags.reset == 1 for refused ports, icmp for tunnelling and sweeps, arp for spoofing, tcp.analysis.retransmission for unstable links.

tshark and file extraction

PCAP analysis without the GUI.

HACKTHESOCLesson 76PCAP and Wireshark16 / 39

Same engine, same filters, in a terminal

  • -r reads a file
  • -Y applies the same display-filter syntax as the Wireshark bar
  • -T fields -e <field> extracts clean, scriptable output
For headless servers, automated pipelines, or captures too large to scroll. Learning the filter bar well pays off twice.
HACKTHESOCLesson 76PCAP and Wireshark17 / 39

An IOC-extraction pass over a capture

tshark -r capture.pcapng --export-objects http,./extracted_files/

  1. 1Export transferred filesFile > Export Objects reassembles files sent over HTTP, SMB, FTP, TFTP or IMF.
  2. 2Hash and checkSHA-256 each file, check against VirusTotal or your TIP.
  3. 3Pull every IP and domainStatistics > Conversations or -e dns.qry.name, then reputation feeds and passive DNS.
  4. 4Record confirmed IOCsHashes, IPs, domains, URLs in case notes, to search the rest of the environment.
This turns a single capture into shareable, actionable indicators rather than a one-off observation.

Section 5 of 9

Following the conversation and reading statistics

Zoom out, then zoom in.

HACKTHESOCLesson 76PCAP and Wireshark19 / 39

Two ways to step back from single packets

Follow > TCP Stream

Reassembles a whole conversation in order, each side colour-coded. Reveals full HTTP exchanges, plaintext commands, even credentials in the clear. TLS shows scrambled content.

Statistics menu

The shape of the traffic.

  • Protocol Hierarchy: unexpected protocols
  • Conversations: who talked most
  • Endpoints: unexpected participants
For encrypted traffic you fall back on metadata: who talked to whom, when, and how much.
HACKTHESOCLesson 76PCAP and Wireshark20 / 39

The zoom-out, zoom-in rhythm

  1. 1SummariseOpen Conversations, sort by bytes.
  2. 2Spot the outlierOne internal host sending an unusually large amount to an external address.
  3. 3Isolate itFilter to that conversation.
  4. 4Read itFollow the stream to see what it was.
Beginners who learn this rhythm get real answers without drowning in individual packets.

Section 6 of 9

Encrypted traffic: SNI, JA3, TLS metadata

You cannot read the payload, but the metadata still tells a story.

HACKTHESOCLesson 76PCAP and Wireshark22 / 39

Two signals from one encrypted session

Where it claims to go

SNI

Server Name Indication in the Client Hello, sent unencrypted because one IP can host many domains. Filter tls.handshake.type == 1, read tls.handshake.extensions_server_name.

What software built it

JA3 / JA3S

Hashes the Client Hello's TLS version, cipher suites and extensions, in order, to identify the client software. JA3S does the same for the server response.

Wireshark supports JA3 via a plugin; Zeek logs it automatically in ssl.log, which is why SOCs rely on Zeek at scale.
HACKTHESOCLesson 76PCAP and Wireshark23 / 39

Why JA3 is worth hunting

  • Not a browserMany malware families and C2 frameworks have a distinctive JA3 that matches no common browser.
  • Survives infrastructure changesA published bad JA3 can be hunted even when the IP or domain changes: it reflects the tool.
  • The escalation caseA JA3 tied to a known loader, reaching an SNI registered days ago, without decrypting one byte.
This extends the beaconing analysis from the worked example with two more concrete fields to pivot on.

What an analyst hunts for in a capture

Each attack pattern has a recognisable shape.

HACKTHESOCLesson 76PCAP and Wireshark29 / 39

Six traffic shapes worth a second look

  • C2 beaconingSmall, regular, repeating connections to one destination. Metronomic, not bursty.
  • Data exfiltrationUnusually large outbound transfer; sort Conversations by bytes.
  • Plaintext credentialsFTP, Telnet, plain HTTP: usernames and passwords in the stream.
  • DNS abuseLong random-looking names, floods to one domain, known-bad lookups.
  • ScanningBursts of SYN packets fanning out, few completed connections.
  • Unusual protocols or portsNon-standard ports used to evade simple controls.

Know what normal looks like, and hunt the deviation.

Ask one question: does this conversation look like a human doing normal work, or a machine doing something it should not?
HACKTHESOCLesson 76PCAP and Wireshark30 / 39

TCP flags and where you see them

FlagFieldWhere you see it
SYNtcp.flags.synFirst packet of every handshake; floods mean scanning or SYN-flood DoS
ACKtcp.flags.ackNearly every packet after the handshake
FINtcp.flags.finOrderly teardown; also used by stealthy FIN scans
RSTtcp.flags.resetClosed port answers a probe; bursts mean refused scan attempts
PSHtcp.flags.pushInteractive or streaming data
URGtcp.flags.urgRare normally; sometimes in crafted or scan packets
Reconnaissance tools abuse specific flag combinations, so recognising them turns a wall of packets into a story.
HACKTHESOCLesson 76PCAP and Wireshark31 / 39

Scan signatures share one shape

SYN (half-open)

Many SYNs to many ports or hosts, few handshakes complete. tcp.flags.syn == 1 && tcp.flags.ack == 0.

Connect scan

Full handshakes to many ports, then an immediate close. Noisier.

RST storm back

A burst of RSTs means most probed ports were closed: the source was scanning.

FIN / NULL / Xmas

Lone FIN, no flags, or FIN+PSH+URG to slip past simple filters.

One source, many destinations or ports, a short burst, few successful conversations.

Look for the fan-out in the packet list or in Statistics > Endpoints.

Zeek and NetFlow without the full PCAP

Full capture is expensive: a busy link can generate terabytes per day.

HACKTHESOCLesson 76PCAP and Wireshark33 / 39

Zeek pre-digests traffic into structured logs

Open-source network security monitor, formerly Bro. What most SIEMs ingest for network visibility.

  • conn.logEvery connection: source, destination, ports, duration, bytes.
  • dns.log and http.logEvery DNS query and answer; every HTTP method, URI, user agent, status code.
  • ssl.logEvery TLS handshake, including SNI and JA3/JA3S.
  • files.logEvery file Zeek extracted, with its hash.
No raw capture? Check whether Zeek logs exist for the same window; often better, since already structured and searchable. NetFlow (IPFIX is the vendor-neutral successor) keeps only flow metadata.

Key takeaways

  1. 01A PCAP is ground truth: a recording of what crossed the wire, read in Wireshark or captured with tcpdump/tshark.
  2. 02Wireshark's list is the index, the details pane decodes layers, the bytes pane is the raw truth.
  3. 03Capture filters limit what you keep; display filters limit what you see, and tshark uses the same syntax.
  4. 04Zoom out with Conversations, zoom in with filters and Follow Stream; encrypted beaconing shows in metadata.
  5. 05SNI names the destination in the clear and JA3 fingerprints the client software, no decryption needed.
  6. 06Without full PCAP, Zeek logs give searchable protocol detail and NetFlow gives long-range volume patterns.
