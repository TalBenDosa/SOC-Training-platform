HACKTHESOCLesson 03Reading a log4 / 45
A log is the security camera footage of a computer: it records everything, the boring and the alarming alike.

The camera does not stop the crime. It lets someone rewind later and see who walked in, what they touched, and when they left.

Systems write logs whether or not anyone is watching, which is exactly why they are so valuable after an incident.

Section 1 of 11

What a log line actually is

A record of one event: a diary the machine keeps about its own life.

HACKTHESOCLesson 03Reading a log6 / 45

Hunt for when, who and what before anything else

  • WHEN: 2026-07-04T08:14:22Z, the trailing Z means Zulu/UTC
  • WHO: jsmith on FIN-WKS-07 from 10.4.2.19
  • WHAT: event.action=user_login, result=success
Everything else in the line is supporting detail. With those three anchors you can read almost any log, even from a product you have never touched.
HACKTHESOCLesson 03Reading a log7 / 45

A log line is a bundle of fields

  • FieldA labeled value: a name and its data. user.name = jsmith is a field.
  • One event, one momentA log line is a collection of name-and-value pairs describing a single event at a single time.
  • Mostly normal99% of what a log captures is ordinary. Your job is to find the 1% that is not.
  • Attackers leave footageA stolen password still has to log in; malware still opens a connection. Both are log lines.
Respect the humble log line as the atomic unit of truth about what happened. The attacker cannot easily avoid leaving footage.

Where logs come from

Every layer sees the world from its own vantage point. Know which one can answer your question.

HACKTHESOCLesson 03Reading a log9 / 45

Five sources, five fields of view

SourceWhat it seesWhat it is blind to
Endpoint / EDRPrograms run, files touched, which process spawned whichMachines where the agent is not installed
Network / firewallThis address talked to that one, on this port, this many bytesWhich user or program made the connection

The firewall sees the envelope, not the person who wrote the letter. Trusting a clean log from a source that could never see the problem has let real intrusions slide past.

Only combined do they tell the story: jsmith logged in, opened Word, Word spawned PowerShell, and the host reached an unfamiliar external IP. No single source tells the whole story.

Raw vs parsed, structured vs unstructured

Why the same event looks different in two places.

HACKTHESOCLesson 03Reading a log12 / 45

Same event, three raw shapes, one vocabulary

Structured JSON is a gift: the splitting is already done. Unstructured text is readable by a human but a computer has to be taught where the username ends.
HACKTHESOCLesson 03Reading a log13 / 45

Recognize all three formats on sight

One firewall says src, another source_ip, a cloud log callerIpAddress. Normalization, also called parsing, rewrites them all into one field such as source.ip so you can search every source at once.
HACKTHESOCLesson 03Reading a log14 / 45
The parsed view is the convenient summary. The raw log is the ground truth.

Parsing can put a field in the wrong place or drop a value. Ask: is this what the machine reported, or what the parser decided?

Seasoned analysts pull up the raw log whenever something looks strange, usually because they were burned once.

Section 4 of 11

How to read an unfamiliar log

You are not memorizing products. You are applying a repeatable process.

HACKTHESOCLesson 03Reading a log16 / 45

Field-name families let you guess meaning on sight

  • source.* / destination.*The two ends of a connection: source.ip, destination.port.
  • user.*The human or account: user.name, user.domain, user.id.
  • host.* / device.*The machine: host.name, host.os.
  • event.*The action: event.action (the verb), event.outcome, event.category.
  • process.*A running program: process.name, process.pid, process.command_line.
  • Genuinely unknown fieldLook it up in the vendor's reference. Guess the common families; verify the exotic ones.
A wrong guess about a field's meaning is how analysts reach confident but false conclusions.
HACKTHESOCLesson 03Reading a log17 / 45

Worked example: a product you have never seen

  • Step 1, anchors: timeGenerated, DeviceName + account, ActionType
  • Step 2, intuition: DeviceName ~ host.name, InitiatingProcess ~ process.
  • Step 3, sentence: at 09:02 UTC akhan's mshta.exe connected to cdn-updates[.]info
If you can produce that plain-English sentence, you have read the log. Everything after is deciding whether the sentence is normal.

Section 5 of 11

Worked example: a real Windows 4624 logon

What actually sits behind the simplified login lines.

HACKTHESOCLesson 03Reading a log19 / 45

Event ID 4624: an account was successfully logged on

  • Subject is the computer account processing the logon, not the actor
  • New Logon is the person who actually logged on: jsmith
  • The timestamp sits outside this text block in Event Viewer
Written to the Security event log. The Event ID plays the role of event.action, but as a number instead of a word.
HACKTHESOCLesson 03Reading a log20 / 45

Three fields that reappear across Windows logs

Security ID (SID)

S-1-5-21-...-1001 is how Windows identifies the account internally. The name is just a label; sometimes you only have the SID.

Logon ID

0x3F5A2E1 is unique to this one session, not the account. It ties every later action in the session back to this logon.

Logon Type

How the logon happened: 2 interactive at the keyboard, 3 network (file share), 10 RemoteInteractive (Remote Desktop).

4624 Type 10 from an unfamiliar address tells a very different story than Type 2 at 8 AM from the user's own desk.

Telling Subject from New Logon, and knowing which is the actor you care about, is exactly the field-reading judgment this lesson builds.

From log to question: pivoting

A field is not just data. It is a doorway.

HACKTHESOCLesson 03Reading a log22 / 45

Every field raises a question

FieldThe questions it asks
TimestampNormal time for this activity? What happened right before and after?
source.ipWhose machine, internal or external, new or known? Its threat-intel reputation?
user.nameConsistent with their job? Should this account do this? Usual location? Compromised?
event.actionNormal for this user and host? Succeeded or failed? Many failures then success?
process.nameNormal to run here? Does process.command_line look legitimate or obfuscated? Parent?
To pivot is to take one value and find every other log that shares it: everything 10.4.2.19 did in 24 hours, every action jsmith took today.
HACKTHESOCLesson 03Reading a log23 / 45

Chaining pivots reveals impossible travel

No single line screamed attack; the story emerged from following the threads. Most pivots lead nowhere, which is the normal healthy outcome, but the reflex surfaces the rare case that matters.

Section 7 of 11

Timestamps, time zones, and why the clock lies

Investigation is about sequence, and sequence collapses if you misread the clock.

HACKTHESOCLesson 03Reading a log25 / 45

Convert to UTC and the true chain appears

A workstation stamped 09:03 US Eastern and a firewall stamped 14:03 UTC happened at the same instant. Sort the raw values and you wrongly drop the workstation from the incident.
HACKTHESOCLesson 03Reading a log26 / 45

Time traps that fool investigations

Rule: convert every timestamp to UTC before you correlate. UTC has no daylight saving and no offset.

  • Clock skewA host with a wrong clock misplaces its own events. NTP keeps clocks synchronized; obvious drift is a data-quality finding.
  • Event vs ingest timeWhen it happened is not when the SIEM received it. A delayed log is not a late attack.
  • Missing zone'09:03' is not a time until you know 09:03 where.
Most 'the events don't line up' confusion is not a mystery about the attack. It is a mistake about time.

Section 8 of 11

When logs lie: gaps, tampering, cleared logs

Attackers know you rely on logs, so they attack the logs.

HACKTHESOCLesson 03Reading a log28 / 45

Three ways attackers interfere with logging

Event ID 1102

Clearing logs

Windows logs that the security audit log was cleared: high signal, almost never legitimate. Linux equivalent: truncating /var/log or clearing shell history.

T1685, T1685.005

Disabling logging

Stopping the service, changing audit policy, unhooking EDR. Produces a gap. Defense Impairment: Disable or Modify Tools.

Rarer

Log injection

Forged or misleading entries inserted to send the investigation the wrong way.

Attackers cannot erase the fact that erasure happened, only its contents.

ATT&CK is the public catalog of attacker techniques. Disable or Modify Tools is T1685 (formerly T1562); Clear Windows Event Logs is T1685.005 (formerly T1070.001).
HACKTHESOCLesson 03Reading a log29 / 45
A gap is a signal, not a blank.

A server that logs every few seconds and goes silent for twenty minutes in your exact window is a lead. Corroborate with the firewall and DC that still saw the host.

Ask why the source stopped: logging disabled, host offline, or is the gap the shape of the attacker's activity? Absence of logs is never automatically absence of activity.

Retention and visibility

You cannot investigate what was never kept.

HACKTHESOCLesson 03Reading a log31 / 45

Two realities decide whether the log exists

Was it ever collected?

Visibility

An unmonitored server, a SaaS app nobody onboarded, a segment with no sensor. No skill recovers what was never recorded.

Is it still there?

Retention

Logs are kept for a finite window, often driven by compliance. Dwell time runs weeks or months, so the earliest evidence may already be deleted.

Verbose sources like flow data may be kept weeks; high-value security logs months or a year or more.
HACKTHESOCLesson 03Reading a log32 / 45

Turn the wall into a finding

  • Name the missing source'I could not confirm initial access because we do not collect logs from that VPN appliance.'
  • State the retention limit'The activity likely began earlier, but our flow logs only retain 14 days.'

The most important log is sometimes the one never collected, or the one deleted last Tuesday.

Both statements tell responders the true uncertainty and make the case for closing a gap or extending retention.

Syslog: facility, severity, CEF and LEEF

The plumbing that carries a huge share of the log lines you will ever read.

HACKTHESOCLesson 03Reading a log34 / 45

Syslog severity: lower number means more severe

SeverityLevelMeaning
0EmergencySystem is unusable
1AlertAction must be taken immediately
2CriticalCritical condition
3 / 4Error / WarningError condition / warning condition
5NoticeNormal but significant condition
6 / 7Informational / DebugInformational / debug-level message

Facility = which subsystem: kernel, mail, auth, print, or local0 to local7. PRI = facility x 8 + severity.

RFC 5424 defines a message as PRI, HEADER and MSG. Severity is a free triage signal: search every severity 0-2 event from any device in the last hour, across firewalls, Linux and printers.
HACKTHESOCLesson 03Reading a log35 / 45

Decode a raw syslog line and its CEF cousin

  • CEF (ArcSight): seven pipe-delimited header fields, always in order
  • Then an Extension of key=value pairs
  • LEEF is IBM's QRadar cousin of CEF, same idea, different header
Standards mean a SIEM can extract how severe and who reported it without a custom parser for every vendor.
HACKTHESOCLesson 03Reading a log36 / 45

Transport tells you how reliable a source is

UDP 514

Original syslog. Fast, connectionless, unreliable: a dropped packet is a permanently lost log line.

TCP

Guaranteed delivery. Most serious deployments use it for security logs.

RFC 5425

TLS syslog

Encrypted, so log traffic cannot be intercepted or spoofed in transit.

A UDP-only legacy appliance will have occasional silent gaps that are transport loss, not tampering. That distinction matters after the 'logs lie' section.

Section 11 of 11

Windows Event Log: channels, EVTX, WEF

Where Windows records live, and how they leave the host before an attacker can delete them.

HACKTHESOCLesson 03Reading a log38 / 45

Channels and the file underneath them

Windows Logs

Application, Security (4624, 4688), System, Setup, Forwarded Events.

Apps and Services Logs

Components register their own channels, e.g. Microsoft-Windows-Sysmon/Operational, separate from Security.

.evtx

Since Vista each channel is a binary XML-based file. Forensic tools parse it offline. A cleared log means the file's contents were wiped.

Exporting an event as XML shows the same fields Event Viewer renders as friendly text.
HACKTHESOCLesson 03Reading a log39 / 45

The 4624 inside the .evtx, and forwarding it off the host

Stored in C:WindowsSystem32winevtLogsSecurity.evtx, then forwarded with wecutil qc and wecutil cs subscription.xml.

This raw XML export is the literal structure inside the .evtx. WEF is agent-free: sources forward channels to a Windows Event Collector, authenticated with Kerberos in a domain or TLS. Once forwarded, a later 1102 on the host cannot touch the copy.
HACKTHESOCLesson 03Reading a log40 / 45

Windows Event Forwarding in the analyst's head

  • Source-initiatedMany endpoints, often via Group Policy, push to a collector that does not need every hostname.
  • Collector-initiatedThe WEC server pulls from an explicit list of source machines.
  • Render vs binaryEvents (Render) sends Event Viewer's text; Events (binary XML) is compact and closer to EVTX.
  • Local gap, central copyThin local log but full forwarded copy is strong evidence of local tampering.
  • Never onboardedAn unforwarded host is a visibility gap. The fix: extend the WEF subscription.
Forwarded events land in the Forwarded Events channel on the collector and typically flow on to the SIEM.

Key takeaways

  1. 01Read any log by first finding the three anchors: when, who, and what.
  2. 02Every source has a limited field of view; always ask what it is blind to.
  3. 03SIEMs normalize fields to one vocabulary, but the raw log is the ground truth.
  4. 04Investigation is pivoting: one field's value finds every log that shares it.
  5. 05Convert every timestamp to UTC before correlating; watch skew and ingest delay.
  6. 06A gap or a 1102 is a signal; name visibility and retention limits as findings.

Check your understanding, question 1 of 3

The firewall log shows a laptop's IP connecting to a suspicious destination, but your manager asks which user and which program on the laptop made the connection. Why can the firewall log alone not answer that?

Each source has a limited field of view. The firewall sees the network envelope (which IP talked to which IP), not the user or process behind it, which endpoint/EDR telemetry provides. Firewalls log allowed and blocked traffic too.

A firewall logs an outbound block at 14:03:11 UTC; the workstation logs the responsible process at 09:03:09 US Eastern (UTC-5). A colleague says the process started five hours earlier, so it is unrelated. Why are they wrong?

The colleague compared raw times across different zones, the classic error. Normalise everything to UTC before correlating: 09:03:09 EST is 14:03:09 UTC, two seconds before the 14:03:11 UTC block.

Check your understanding, question 3 of 3

A server that normally logs every few seconds went silent for the exact 20-minute window you are examining, and shortly after, Windows Event ID 1102 appears. How should you interpret this?

A steady source going quiet in exactly your window is a lead, and 1102 means the security audit log was cleared. Together they point to Defense Impairment (T1685, incl. T1685.005 Clear Windows Event Logs). Corroborate with sources the attacker did not control.
