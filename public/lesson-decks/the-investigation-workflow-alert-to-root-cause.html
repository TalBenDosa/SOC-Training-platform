The alert tells you where the attacker was noticed. The workflow finds where they came in, everywhere they went, and proves they are gone.

Method, not more knowledge, separates a fast, correct investigation from a slow or wrong one.

This lesson assumes students already read logs and write basic queries. Queries are KQL for Defender XDR and Sentinel, but the workflow is product-neutral: swap table and field names for SPL or any other SIEM.

Section 1 of 12

Alert intake and validation

Did the thing really happen, and is it malicious? Answered at the lowest possible cost.

HACKTHESOCLesson 25Investigation workflow6 / 55

The first two minutes

  1. 1Claim itAssign to yourself, set In progress. Open the incident, not the single alert: it groups correlated alerts.
  2. 2Read the detection, not the titleWhich rule fired, on which field values? The matched fields tell real theft from a backup tool.
  3. 3Open the raw evidenceCommand line, parent, account, logon session, destination. Write down every entity.
  4. 4Separate alert time from activity timeAlert time is when the rule matched. The first malicious activity is usually earlier. Note both.
The alert you were paged on is often not the earliest one in the incident. "Suspicious access to LSASS" can be credential theft, an EDR or backup product, or an admin's crash-dump utility.
HACKTHESOCLesson 25Investigation workflow7 / 55

Three tests settle most alerts

TestWhat you checkWhere
Is it explained?Change ticket, maintenance window, sanctioned scanner, pentest schedule, admin toolingTicketing, SOC wiki, the asset owner
Is it normal here?Devices running this parent and child pair in 30 days; first time for this device or userPrevalence query
Is it corroborated?A second, independent signal on the same entity within about two hoursAlertInfo, AlertEvidence, sign-ins, network
A pair seen on 400 devices for months with a change ticket is very different from a pair seen on one device for the first time today, next to a second alert on the same account.
HACKTHESOCLesson 25Investigation workflow8 / 55

Prevalence: how rare is this behaviour?

  • Never seen in 30 days, then twice in 13 minutes (14:09, 14:22)
  • Rare, unexplained and corroborated: true positive
  • The second row is your first scoping lead
A pair seen on 400 devices for months with a change ticket differs from one device, first time today, next to a second alert. Corroboration joins AlertEvidence to AlertInfo for the same device or account within two hours.
HACKTHESOCLesson 25Investigation workflow9 / 55

Use the verdict vocabulary precisely

Defender XDR classifications feed tuning and metrics.

True positive

Real malicious activity.

  • Multistage attack
  • Compromised account
  • Malware
  • Phishing

Informational, expected

Real activity, detection worked, but sanctioned.

  • Security test
  • Line-of-business application
  • Confirmed activity

False positive

The rule matched something that is not malicious.

  • Not malicious
  • Not enough data to validate

A pentest that really happened is not a false positive. Mark it expected activity so the rule is not tuned away.

Timebox the verdict, for example 15 minutes for a Medium. If you cannot decide, record undetermined, list the missing evidence and escalate. Then write a one-sentence verdict with host, UTC time, process, parent, account, ticket and prevalence.

Section 2 of 12

Scoping questions: who, what, when, where, how

Turn one confirmed data point into a bounded incident, every answer pointing at evidence.

HACKTHESOCLesson 25Investigation workflow11 / 55

Five questions, each with its evidence

QuestionWhat you needPrimary evidence
WhoEvery account: in the alert, logged on to the same hosts, created or used by the attackerDeviceLogonEvents, 4624 / 4648 / 4672 / 4720, SigninLogs
WhatEvery action: processes, files, persistence, data touchedDeviceProcessEvents, DeviceFileEvents, DeviceRegistryEvents, CloudAppEvents
WhenFirst malicious activity to now, in UTCEarliest confirmed timeline row, plus retention limits
WhereEvery host, mailbox, tenant and cloud resourceLogon and network pivots, IOC sweeps, AuditLogs
HowInitial access vector and each lateral techniqueEmail and click telemetry, logon types, remote-execution artifacts

Keep a running scope statement with a confidence word on every line. Two pivot rounds adding nothing, all sweeps run: scope is stable.

For every account in Who, ask the cloud questions too: new IP, ASN or user agent, replayable sessions, new MFA methods, OAuth consent, mailbox rules. An on-prem-only scope misses the footholds that survive reimaging.
HACKTHESOCLesson 25Investigation workflow12 / 55

Set the time window deliberately

Anchor on T0, the first confirmed malicious event. Search back at least 24 hours, forward to now.

Widen as you go

Every time you find an earlier event, widen the backward window again.

Retention trap

Retention is 30 days and the earliest event is on day 29? You found the edge of your data, not the start. Pull archived logs before claiming a start.

Ingestion trap

Cloud audit records can arrive minutes late. A query ending at "now" may miss the last few minutes.

Archived data means Sentinel long-term retention or the SIEM archive. Say in the notes that the start is unknown until it is checked.
HACKTHESOCLesson 25Investigation workflow13 / 55

The scope board, updated every round

  • A confidence word on every line
  • Anything new goes back through validation and gets its own pivots
  • Two rounds adding nothing, all sweeps run: scope is stable
The full board also has an open-action column, for example check svc_backup use after 14:22, or IOC sweep of 203.0.113.77 estate-wide.

The pivot cookbook

Which log answers which question, and the shared key that leads to the next source.

HACKTHESOCLesson 25Investigation workflow15 / 55

Endpoint questions and their tables

QuestionTable and key fieldsPivot to next source
Who logged on, and how?DeviceLogonEvents: AccountName, LogonType, RemoteIP, RemoteDeviceNameRemoteIP or RemoteDeviceName to the source host
Where else did this account log on?SecurityEvent 4624 / 4625 / 4648: TargetUserName, LogonType, IpAddressComputer to the next host
What ran, and who launched it?DeviceProcessEvents: ProcessCommandLine, InitiatingProcessId, SHA1ProcessId plus creation time to children

What did it talk to?DeviceNetworkEvents: RemoteIP, RemotePort, RemoteUrlRemoteIP to an estate-wide sweep
LogonType values: Interactive, RemoteInteractive, Network, Batch, Service. Fast investigators do not wonder where to look; they know which table answers which question.
HACKTHESOCLesson 25Investigation workflow16 / 55

Email, identity and tenant questions

QuestionTable and key fieldsPivot to next source
Which email delivered it?EmailEvents, EmailAttachmentInfo: NetworkMessageId, SenderFromAddress, SubjectNetworkMessageId to clicks and attachments
Who clicked the link?UrlClickEvents: Url, AccountUpn, ActionType, IsClickedThroughAccountUpn to sign-ins
Signed in from somewhere new?SigninLogs, AADNonInteractiveUserSignInLogs: IPAddress, SessionId, UserAgentIPAddress to every user it touched

Pivot on stable keys (NetworkMessageId, SessionId, SID, device ID). DeviceName is the FQDN, so use startswith or =~. Sweep on SHA1.

Keep the window around a pivot tight first, then widen, and check both directions: what the account did and who else used it. In several Defender tables SHA256 is often empty.

Section 4 of 12

Building the timeline and process trees

The investigation's working memory: one cited row per confirmed event, in UTC.

HACKTHESOCLesson 25Investigation workflow18 / 55

A claim without a citation is an assertion

Every row: UTC timestamp, entity, what happened, evidence pointer (ReportId, EventRecordID or NetworkMessageId), confidence word. Normalise to UTC first; firewalls and proxies often log local time.
HACKTHESOCLesson 25Investigation workflow19 / 55

Reconstructing a process tree correctly

  • Two keys, not oneMatch InitiatingProcessId to ProcessId and InitiatingProcessCreationTime to ProcessCreationTime. Windows reuses PIDs.
  • Walk upwardsexplorer.exe = user launched it; services.exe or PSEXESVC.exe = service or remote execution; wmiprvse.exe = WMI.
  • Walk downwardsChildren are where discovery, credential access and persistence show up.
  • Read command linesHidden-window PowerShell with an encoded command, two seconds after a ZIP opened, is a story.
  • Parent is a claimParent PID spoofing exists. Odd tree? Cross-check logon session, integrity level and timing.
In the Defender portal, the alert story and the device Timeline show the same tree. Use the portal to orient and the query to prove.
HACKTHESOCLesson 25Investigation workflow20 / 55

The reconstructed tree, and the gap it reveals

  • Join child to parent on PID plus creation time: Windows reuses PIDs
  • explorer.exe at the top means the user launched it
  • Silent gaps and effects without causes become the next pivots
WS-ACC-117, account CORP\d.levi, launched from Remittance_0610.lnk. Read command lines, not names, and treat the reported parent as a claim: parent PID spoofing exists. Use the portal to orient and the query to prove.

Section 5 of 12

Hypotheses: forming, testing, stopping

Hold at least two explanations and test each with a prediction that could prove it wrong.

HACKTHESOCLesson 25Investigation workflow22 / 55

Competing hypotheses for the LSASS alert

HypothesisIf true, we should seeIf false, we should see
H1: it.klein's credentials stolen and replayedHis logon comes from a host he does not normally use, after suspicious activity thereLogon from his usual admin workstation, nothing odd before it
H2: sanctioned troubleshootingA ticket, his usual workstation, a documented dump tool, the dump collected by ITNo ticket, unusual source, undocumented technique, output in a world-writable folder
H3: security or backup productA signed vendor binary as actor, same behaviour on many serversA Windows LOLBin as actor, on one server only
Result: the logon came from WS-ACC-117, which he had reached only once by RDP the previous afternoon; no ticket; rundll32.exe on one server only. H2 and H3 fail, H1 becomes the working hypothesis.
HACKTHESOCLesson 25Investigation workflow23 / 55

Three confidence words, and mean them

Confirmed

Directly shown by a record you can cite.

Probable

The best explanation of several records, nothing contradicting it, but no single record proves it.

Possible

Consistent with the evidence, not yet tested.

Guard against bias: look for evidence that would disprove your hypothesis. Stopping is a decision you write down, with its reason.

Absence needs care: no 4688 events may mean auditing is off. Stop when the answer changes no decision, all but one hypothesis failed, or the evidence does not exist and the gap is recorded.

Section 6 of 12

Root cause: the vector and patient zero

Where they came in and what weakness let them. Without both, eradication is guesswork.

HACKTHESOCLesson 25Investigation workflow25 / 55

Symptom, vector and root cause are different things

TermMeaningIn the worked case
SymptomWhat the alert sawLSASS memory access on SRV-FILE02
Initial access vectorHow the attacker first got code or a session inPhishing link to a ZIP with a shortcut file, opened by d.levi
Root causeThe weakness that let the vector succeed and spreadLNK runnable from archives; d.levi local admin; helpdesk RDP left a credential
A report that says root cause: credential dumping has described a symptom. Re-imaging SRV-FILE02 leaves the vector and the weaknesses in place.
HACKTHESOCLesson 25Investigation workflow26 / 55

Walking backwards to patient zero

Ask "what caused this?" one step back at a time, until the chain leaves your environment.

  • The delivery event lines up with the first process in minutes
  • Every recipient and clicker is a patient-zero candidate until checked
  • Patient zero: WS-ACC-117 / d.levi
Earliest seen is not always first: retention, clock skew and re-infection fool the ordering. A proxied sign-in page captures a session too, so check sign-ins right after the click. Finally ask which control should have stopped it.

Section 7 of 12

Blast radius, scope expansion, escalation

Expand in rings and know the full set before anyone touches containment.

HACKTHESOCLesson 25Investigation workflow28 / 55

Scope expands in three rings

Exposed identities

Ring 1

Every account with an interactive, RDP or service logon on a compromised host during the whole dwell window, plus accounts created or changed, plus service accounts.

  • 4720 user created
  • 4728 / 4732 / 4756 group adds

Cloud persistence

Ring 2

For each exposed cloud identity: sessions and tokens, MFA methods, OAuth grants, mailbox rules. Reimaging does nothing to these.

Estate-wide sweeps

Ring 3

Every indicator becomes a sweep: C2 IP and domain, file SHA1, task name, sender and subject, attacker sign-in IP across all users.

Any hit is a new host or identity that goes back through validation.

Ring 2 footholds: new IP or user agent on the same SessionId, "User registered security info", "Consent to application", and New-InboxRule rules that move payment or security mail to RSS Feeds. A service account on a dumped server is in scope even if unused.
HACKTHESOCLesson 25Investigation workflow29 / 55

Ring 2: where cloud footholds hide

FootholdWhere to lookWhat is suspicious
Replayed sessions and tokensSigninLogs, AADNonInteractiveUserSignInLogsNew IP or user agent on the same SessionId; ResultType 0 right after a click
New MFA methodsAuditLogs "User registered security info"A method added from an IP the user never used
OAuth grantsAuditLogs "Consent to application"; CloudAppEvents OAuthAppIdAn unfamiliar app with mail or file scopes
Mailbox rulesCloudAppEvents New-InboxRule, Set-InboxRule, UpdateInboxRulesDelete, move to RSS Feeds or Archive, or forward payment or security mail
AuditLogs also records "Add delegated permission grant". These are exactly the footholds that survive host reimaging.

Escalate on written triggers

TriggerWhy it changes the case
Privileged or service account compromisedReach is the whole domain or tenant, not one host
More than one host, or a server or DCCoordinated containment and business owners are needed

Escalation is a decision point, not failure. Escalate with the scope board, timeline and open questions, not a phone call that says it looks bad. Precursors: backup tampering, mass service stops, shadow copy deletion.

Section 8 of 12

Containment decisions and trade-offs

The first step that changes reality: it stops damage, but can destroy evidence and warn the attacker.

HACKTHESOCLesson 25Investigation workflow32 / 55

Decision point 2: what does each action destroy?

Contain at once only if damage is active (encryption, data leaving, DC or backups attacked). Otherwise scope first.

ActionStopsDestroys or revealsDo first
Isolate deviceNetwork, except Defender serviceReveals detection to the attackerCollect investigation package
Power off or re-imageEverythingMemory, live connections, diskNever a first move on key hosts
Disable accountNew interactive sign-insMay lock out a user or break servicesConfirm owner and dependencies
Password resetUse of the stolen passwordSessions and tokens may keep workingPair with session revocation
Revoke sessionsRefresh tokens, session cookiesUser signs in again everywhereSame moment as the reset
Block IOCKnown C2 or download siteTips off attacker, they switchBlock all indicators together
Remove rule or OAuth grantThe cloud footholdThe rule itself is evidenceExport the details first
Memory capture before isolation when the case needs it, for injected code or in-memory-only tooling.
HACKTHESOCLesson 25Investigation workflow33 / 55

Isolation and identity details that matter

  • Full isolationCuts all traffic except Defender, optionally allowing Outlook and Teams. Live response still works.
  • Selective isolationKeeps chosen processes or destinations reachable: servers, proxy setups. Tell the owner first.
  • Revoke sessionsRevoke-MgUserSignInSession resets signInSessionsValidFromDateTime. Takes a few minutes.
  • Access-token tailIssued tokens live 60 to 90 minutes by default, unless the app supports Continuous Access Evaluation.
  • Hybrid accountsDisabling on-prem AD reaches Entra ID only at next sync. Revoke in Entra directly too.
  • Service accountsRotation breaks every job using it. Coordinate inside the containment window.
Plan on a short tail of attacker activity after revocation and keep watching sign-ins. It is expected, not proof the new password leaked.
HACKTHESOCLesson 25Investigation workflow34 / 55

Decision point 3: one coordinated move

Piecemeal containment, one host this hour and the account tomorrow, is how attackers keep access. The attacker must lose every foothold at once.

Eradication, recovery and closing

Remove everything left behind, return systems to service, and prove both worked.

HACKTHESOCLesson 25Investigation workflow36 / 55

Every "What" row that created something is an eradication item

ArtifactEvidenceRemoval
Scheduled task4698, schtasks.exe in DeviceProcessEventsDelete, confirm with a live-response task listing
New service7045, 4697, Services key in DeviceRegistryEventsRemove the service and its binary
Run key or startup itemDeviceRegistryEvents on Run and RunOnceDelete the value
Dropped files and dumpsDeviceFileEvents FileCreatedCollect and hash as evidence, then remove
Accounts and group changes4720, 4728 / 4732 / 4756, Entra AuditLogsDisable or delete, revert memberships

Exposed credentialsRing 1 account listReset every one, including service accounts

Rebuild when the attacker had admin control or you are unsure; clean in place only when the footprint is fully mapped. Fix the root cause too.

The service key is HKLM\SYSTEM\CurrentControlSet\Services. The timeline is your eradication checklist.
HACKTHESOCLesson 25Investigation workflow37 / 55

Verification sweep at T+24h and T+72h

  • Expected after clean eradication: no rows
  • Failed attempts (ResultType 50126) go to the watchlist, not a failure
  • Also re-run the detection that fired
Watchlist reset accounts for 14 days: failed sign-ins mean they are still interested, a success means something was missed. Recover in stages and confirm with the owner that the business function works.

Evidence handling and case notes

How the next shift, the IR lead or an auditor can trust your conclusions.

HACKTHESOCLesson 25Investigation workflow39 / 55

Capture before you change anything

  • Investigation packageAutoruns, programs, connections, DNS cache, ARP, prefetch, processes, tasks, services, SMB sessions, temp folders. From the Action center.
  • MemoryWhen the case may need it, with your forensic team's approved tool through live response.
  • Cloud artifactsExport the inbox rule, OAuth grant and sign-in records before removing. Removal deletes the evidence.
  • Record each artifactWhat, source host, collector, UTC time, tool, SHA256. Stored in the SOC evidence location, not your desktop.
Collect investigation package is a Defender for Endpoint response action; it produces a ZIP.
HACKTHESOCLesson 25Investigation workflow40 / 55

ElementExample

What you did and whyPivoted from 4624 on SRV-FILE02 to source IP 10.20.14.37 to find the origin of the logon
Exact query text or saved linkPaste the KQL, with its time range

Keep facts, inferences (marked probable) and opinions visibly separate. A handoff must let the receiver act without calling you.

Notes are not paperwork added at the end; they are how you keep track of the case while you work it.
HACKTHESOCLesson 25Investigation workflow41 / 55

Facts, inferences and opinions stay separate

Fact

it.klein logged on to SRV-FILE02 from 10.20.14.37 at 14:19:03.

Inference

The attacker used it.klein's credential, taken from WS-ACC-117. Marked probable, supporting facts listed underneath.

Opinion

"This looks like a known crimeware kit." A separate labelled line, or nowhere.

Handoff: status and phase, scope board, timeline, actions taken and pending with owners, open questions, evidence location.

If the case lives only in your head, it ends when your shift does.

Section 11 of 12

Common investigative pitfalls

Failures of method, not knowledge, and they repeat reliably.

HACKTHESOCLesson 25Investigation workflow43 / 55

The big three

Tunnel vision

A story formed in five minutes bends every later fact. "Rogue admin" would have spent an hour on it.klein and never looked at WS-ACC-117.

  • Fix: hypotheses table first

Stopping at the first host

Isolating SRV-FILE02 alone leaves the beacon, the stolen credential and the mailbox session live.

  • Fix: backward pivot + sweep

Symptom as cause

"Credential dumping" is a symptom. The cause is the vector, the local admin right, the credential left behind.

  • Fix: ask what made this possible
Keep asking what made this possible until you reach something outside your environment or a control that failed.

The rest of the list

Mixed time zonesA beacon appears before the phishing emailNormalise to UTC, keep originals
Silence read as innocence"No 4688 events, so nothing ran"Check log source and audit policy first
PID-only joinsChildren attached to the wrong parentJoin on PID plus creation time

HACKTHESOCLesson 25Investigation workflow47 / 55

Intake to patient zero in twenty minutes

  1. 114:25 IntakeHigh LSASS alert on SRV-FILE02, it.klein. Prevalence shows a second hit at 14:09 on WS-ACC-117. True positive.
  2. 214:29 Backward pivotNetwork logon at 14:19:03 from WS-ACC-117 (d.levi). PSEXESVC installed seven seconds later.
  3. 314:33 Hypothesesit.klein RDP'd to WS-ACC-117 the day before to fix a printer. H2, H3 fail. H1 probable.
  4. 414:38 Process treeLNK -> hidden PowerShell 13:41, beacon, task 14:02, LSASS 14:09. H1 confirmed.
The 14:09 LSASS event raised a Medium alert that sat unassigned in the queue. d.levi being local admin is what made that access possible.

Root cause: one click, two footholds

  • EndpointEmail 13:36 to 14 recipients, click 13:39:31, ZIP 13:40:12 from remit-portal.example.net.
  • Cloud sessiond.levi sign-in 13:39:52 from 198.51.100.61, MFA satisfied; same SessionId non-interactive at 13:47.

Closed branches

r.cohen clicked at 13:44 but Safe Links blocked it. Attacker IP, C2 IP and task name appear nowhere else.

Exposed

Key takeaways

  1. 01Validate with a procedure: read what matched, separate alert and activity time, test explained, normal, corroborated.
  2. 02Scope with who, what, when, where, how, a confidence word per line, and pivots until two rounds add nothing.
  3. 03Know which table answers which question, and pivot on stable keys like NetworkMessageId and SessionId.
  4. 04Keep a cited UTC timeline, join process trees on PID plus creation time, and test competing hypotheses.
  5. 05Root cause is the vector plus the weakness, not the symptom. Expand scope in rings and escalate on triggers.
  6. 06Contain in one coordinated move, revoke with every reset, verify at 24 and 72 hours, close on evidence.

Check your understanding, question 1 of 3

A High alert fires on a server at 14:23. The suspicious process started at 14:22, and the incident groups an older Medium alert from 14:09 on a workstation. What is the starting point of your timeline?

Alert time is only when a rule matched. Anchor on the earliest confirmed event in the whole incident (14:09 or before) and search back at least 24 hours. Treating the grouped Medium as separate is how patient zero gets missed.

Check your understanding, question 3 of 3

You reset a compromised user's password and ran Revoke-MgUserSignInSession. Twenty minutes later a sign-in record still shows the attacker IP accessing mail. Most likely explanation?

Revocation kills refresh tokens and session cookies, but already-issued access tokens live until expiry unless the resource supports Continuous Access Evaluation. That is the expected token tail, so keep watching.
