An alert matters when you can say what it puts at risk, in the terms the business uses to decide.

Risk management is the continuous process of identifying what could harm information assets, estimating how likely and damaging it is, and deciding what to do.

Open with the goal of the lesson: not memorizing formulas, but being able to explain why one alert outranks another in business language.

Core vocabulary: asset, threat, vulnerability, risk

Most risk arguments come from one word being used for three different things. Fix the vocabulary first.

HACKTHESOCLesson 116Risk management for the SOC6 / 54

Seven terms, one precise meaning each

The CIA triad is only the lens here for what harm means.

TermPrecise meaningExample
AssetAnything of value that needs protection: data, systems, people, processes, reputationCustomer database, payroll server, an executive's laptop
ThreatA circumstance or event with the potential to harm an assetRansomware, theft, flood, a careless employee
Threat actorThe person or group that carries out or causes a threatA financially motivated gang, a malicious insider, a nation-state team
VulnerabilityA weakness a threat can exploitUnpatched VPN appliance, no disk encryption, weak password policy
LikelihoodProbability the threat exploits the vulnerability in a time frameOnce in ten years, or 4 on a 1 to 5 scale
ImpactMagnitude of harm if it happens$250,000 recovery cost, three days of downtime
RiskLikelihood and impact combined for one specific scenarioRisk = likelihood x impact
Go row by row and ask for a fresh example of each. Students most often blur threat, vulnerability and risk, so slow down on those three.
HACKTHESOCLesson 116Risk management for the SOC7 / 54
A risk is a scenario, not a word.

Ransomware is a threat. A risk is: a ransomware actor exploits an unpatched VPN appliance and encrypts the file servers, causing three days of downtime.

That one sentence contains an asset, a threat actor, a vulnerability and an impact. The most common beginner mistake is writing a threat such as phishing in the risk column.
HACKTHESOCLesson 116Risk management for the SOC8 / 54

The home analogy: storm, roof tile, furniture

Threat

The storm

The circumstance with the potential to cause harm.

    Vulnerability

    The broken roof tile

    The weakness the storm can get through. Fix the tile and the same storm is much less dangerous.

      Assets

      Furniture and photos

      What you stand to lose. Move the photos to a safe and the impact shrinks.

        Risk

        The ruined living room

        The chance this storm gets through that tile and ruins those things, multiplied by what it costs you.

          Remove the valuable furniture and there is nothing left to lose.

          Each lever on the slide maps to a security move: patching removes the weakness, backups and encryption shrink impact, and decommissioning removes the asset.
          HACKTHESOCLesson 116Risk management for the SOC9 / 54

          Inherent risk, residual risk, and the end state of treatment

          Risk is about the future and about uncertainty. Incidents are what happens when a risk materializes.

          • Inherent riskThe risk before any controls are applied: the raw exposure of the scenario.
          • Residual riskWhat remains after the controls you have actually implemented are taken into account.
          • Residual is never zeroA laptop with full-disk encryption still gets stolen; you still lose the hardware and the working day.
          • The correct end stateA residual score that a named person has consciously decided to live with.
          • The pitfallDeclaring a risk mitigated and closing it.
          We reuse the laptop example for the rest of the lesson. The point to land now is that treatment ends in an accepted residual score, not in a risk that has vanished.

          Section 2 of 9

          The risk management lifecycle

          A loop, not a document: identify, assess, treat, monitor.

          HACKTHESOCLesson 116Risk management for the SOC11 / 54

          Four activities, always a loop

          Every framework draws it slightly differently, but these four always appear.

          1. 1IdentifyBuild the asset inventory, ideally with owners, then the threats and vulnerabilities that apply. Output: risk scenarios.
          2. 2AssessEstimate likelihood and impact for each scenario, qualitatively or quantitatively, and prioritize them.
          3. 3TreatChoose mitigate, transfer, avoid or accept. Implement the controls and record the residual risk.
          4. 4MonitorCheck whether assumptions still hold: new vulnerabilities, changed systems, control failures, incidents. Feed it back.

          Inputs to Identify: inventories, vulnerability scans, threat intelligence, past incidents, audit findings, business owners.

          Stress the arrow from Monitor back to Identify. The SOC lives mostly at Monitor, which is why this loop matters to an analyst.
          HACKTHESOCLesson 116Risk management for the SOC12 / 54

          The same four-part loop sits under NIST guidance

          A concept-level mapping. You do not need to memorize step numbers.

          Our stepNIST SP 800-39 componentNIST RMF (SP 800-37) steps
          IdentifyFrame (set context, assumptions, tolerance)Prepare, Categorize
          AssessAssessSelect (partly), Assess
          TreatRespondSelect, Implement, Authorize
          MonitorMonitorMonitor
          The takeaway is recognition: when you meet Frame, Assess, Respond, Monitor in a framework document, you know it is the same loop.
          HACKTHESOCLesson 116Risk management for the SOC13 / 54

          Three tiers: organization, mission, system

          SP 800-39 applies its four components at three tiers.

          Strategy and appetite

          The organization

          Where leadership sets strategy and risk appetite.

            What must keep working

            Mission or business process

            The processes the business needs to keep working.

              Technical controls

              The information system

              The individual systems and their technical controls. The RMF is the procedure at this tier: prepare, categorize by impact, select controls from SP 800-53, implement, assess, authorize, monitor.

                Analysts mostly touch the system tier, but their evidence reaches the other two through the register and management reporting.
                HACKTHESOCLesson 116Risk management for the SOC14 / 54

                What triggers a new pass through the loop

                Assessments age quickly: a score that was right in January can be wrong in March.

                • A new system goes live
                • A merger adds networks
                • A critical vulnerability is published
                • An incident occurs
                • A regulation changes, or the annual review date arrives

                The SOC is mostly Monitor, but it also feeds Identify with new vulnerabilities and Assess with real incident data.

                Warn about the shelf document: an annual spreadsheet produced for an audit and never consulted. If the register does not change when incidents happen and systems change, it is not managing anything.

                Qualitative assessment: likelihood and impact matrices

                Ordered categories instead of money: fast, easy to explain, and consistent only if the anchors are written down.

                HACKTHESOCLesson 116Risk management for the SOC16 / 54

                Write the anchors down or scores drift between assessors

                Teams agree a 1 to 5 scale for each dimension and score against written anchors.

                ScoreLikelihood anchorImpact anchor
                1Rare: less than once in 10 yearsNegligible: under $1,000 or no visible effect
                2Unlikely: once in 3 to 10 yearsMinor: hours of disruption
                3Possible: about once in 1 to 3 yearsModerate: a day of disruption or limited data exposure
                4Likely: about once a yearMajor: several days, regulatory notification
                5Almost certain: several times a yearSevere: existential, large-scale breach, safety effect

                This is a typical set. Most organizations start here because it is fast and needs little data.

                Ask two students to score the same scenario without the anchors, then with them. The gap shows why anchors exist.
                HACKTHESOCLesson 116Risk management for the SOC17 / 54

                The 5x5 matrix: multiply to place the scenario

                Score = likelihood x impact. Colored green, yellow, orange and red, this is the heat map.

                • Ransomware at likelihood 4 x impact 5 scores 20: Critical
                • A lost USB stick at likelihood 4 x impact 1 scores 4: Low
                Each band maps to an action rule, so the score drives what happens next, not just a color on a slide.
                HACKTHESOCLesson 116Risk management for the SOC18 / 54

                Equal scores hide different shapes

                Both of these cells score 10 on the matrix.

                Score 10

                Likelihood 5, impact 2

                A steady drip of small losses.

                  Score 10

                  Likelihood 2, impact 5

                  A rare catastrophe.

                    Organizations often want to treat the high-impact cell more cautiously regardless of the product.

                    The product compresses two different questions into one number. Always read both axes, not just the score.
                    HACKTHESOCLesson 116Risk management for the SOC19 / 54

                    Four pitfalls you should be able to name

                    Strength: speed and a shared language between business and technical staff.

                    • Ordinal, not arithmeticA 20 is not twice as bad as a 10; the numbers only rank. Averaging scores across scenarios is meaningless.
                    • Subjectivity and anchoringTwo assessors can legitimately differ. Anchors, workshops and recorded reasoning reduce this but never remove it.
                    • False precisionA score of 12 looks scientific, but it is a judgment compressed into a number.
                    • No answer on spendIt cannot answer the budget holder's question: how much should we spend on this?
                    The last pitfall is the bridge to the next section. Money per year is what a budget holder needs.

                    Section 4 of 9

                    Quantitative assessment: SLE, ARO and ALE

                    Risk in money per year, so it can be compared with the cost of a control.

                    HACKTHESOCLesson 116Risk management for the SOC21 / 54

                    Five quantities turn a scenario into dollars per year

                    SymbolNameMeaning
                    AVAsset valueWhat the asset, or the scenario's exposed value, is worth
                    EFExposure factorPercentage of AV lost in one incident (0 to 1)
                    SLESingle loss expectancyExpected loss from one incident = AV x EF
                    AROAnnualized rate of occurrenceExpected number of incidents per year
                    ALEAnnualized loss expectancyExpected loss per year = SLE x ARO
                    Write the two formulas on the board: SLE = AV x EF, ALE = SLE x ARO. Everything else is an input.
                    HACKTHESOCLesson 116Risk management for the SOC22 / 54

                    Worked example setup: one stolen laptop

                    200 laptops, many with exported customer data. Records show about 6 lost or stolen per year.

                    • Hardware$2,000
                    • Replacement and staff time$500 for replacement, re-imaging and staff time.
                    • Breach response$7,500 for notification, legal review and credit monitoring when data was on the device.
                    • Asset value (AV)$10,000 for one incident.
                    • Exposure factor (EF)0.5: not every stolen laptop carries customer data, so about half the value is lost on average.
                    Ask where the AV figure came from. In real work it comes from incident history and finance, and you should say so.
                    HACKTHESOCLesson 116Risk management for the SOC23 / 54

                    The math: $30,000 per year if nothing changes

                    • ALE is a budget ceiling: $100,000 a year to prevent a $30,000 loss makes no sense
                    • A positive net value means the control pays for itself
                    Compute ALE before and after a control. The next section finishes this example with real control options.
                    HACKTHESOCLesson 116Risk management for the SOC24 / 54

                    Four ALE pitfalls

                    Mature teams use both methods: qualitative to triage the list, quantitative for the few large risks.

                    Inputs are estimates

                    AV, EF and ARO come from incident history, reports and judgment. Show ranges and state sources; FAIR does this formally.

                      ALE averages away disasters

                      A one-in-a-hundred-year event costing $5,000,000 has an ALE of only $50,000 per year.

                        Hard-to-price impacts

                        Reputation, trust and regulator attention are real, but models can quietly leave them out.

                          Forgetting ARO

                          Stopping at SLE. A large loss once a century can matter less than a small loss every week.

                            The $5,000,000 example hides that the organization could be ruined in a single bad year. Never read ALE as a promise about any one year.

                            Section 5 of 9

                            Risk treatment: four options and risk appetite

                            Four standard responses, judged against what leadership will tolerate.

                            HACKTHESOCLesson 116Risk management for the SOC26 / 54

                            Mitigate, transfer, avoid, accept

                            OptionWhat it meansExampleTypical when
                            Mitigate (reduce)Add or strengthen controls to lower likelihood or impactFull-disk encryption, patching, MFAControl cost is below the risk reduction it delivers
                            Transfer (share)Shift the financial consequence to a third partyCyber insurance, contractual liability clauses, managed serviceImpact is large but rare and hard to prevent entirely
                            AvoidStop the activity that creates the riskDecommission a legacy service, do not store card numbersRisk exceeds tolerance and the activity is not essential
                            AcceptKnowingly live with the risk and monitor itLow-value test system left unpatched for a weekWithin tolerance, or treatment costs more than the loss
                            Every row is a legitimate answer. The skill is matching the option to the situation in the last column.
                            HACKTHESOCLesson 116Risk management for the SOC27 / 54

                            Three points that separate practitioners from beginners

                            Not accountability

                            Transfer moves money

                            Insurance may pay recovery costs, but downtime, lost customers, regulatory duties and reputation damage stay with you. Insurers also exclude events and demand minimum controls.

                              Documented, not neglect

                              Accept is a decision

                              A legitimate, documented choice, not a failure. Silence is not acceptance: if nobody wrote it down and signed it, you simply have an unmanaged risk.

                                Pitfall

                                Not everything is mitigate

                                Treating every risk as mitigate burns budget on trivial risks and starves the important ones. Use transfer, avoid and accept deliberately.

                                  Ask: if a ransomware outage is covered by insurance, what do you still own? Downtime, lost customers, regulatory duties and reputation.
                                  HACKTHESOCLesson 116Risk management for the SOC28 / 54

                                  Appetite sets the mood, tolerance sets the limits

                                  Treatment decisions are judged against these limits.

                                  • Risk appetiteThe broad amount and type of risk leadership will pursue: low for data-protection risk, higher for product experimentation.
                                  • Risk toleranceTurns appetite into measurable limits. Three examples follow.
                                  • Limit on ALEAny single risk with ALE above $50,000 needs executive approval.
                                  • Limit on residual scoreAny residual risk scored High needs CISO sign-off.
                                  • Limit on acceptanceNo accepted risk may exceed 12 months without review.
                                  Appetite is a statement of attitude; tolerance is something you can check a decision against. Without tolerance limits, an analyst cannot tell if a residual score is acceptable.
                                  HACKTHESOCLesson 116Risk management for the SOC29 / 54

                                  Finishing the laptop example: mitigate, then accept the rest

                                  Starting point: ALE of $30,000 per year.

                                  OptionThe numbersVerdict
                                  Mitigate: encryption and remote wipeCost $4,000/yr. EF 0.25, SLE $2,500, ALE $2,500 x 6 = $15,000. Net $11,000/yrChosen: pays for itself
                                  Transfer: insurancePremium $14,000/yr, $2,000 deductible; insurer pays about $500 per incident, $3,000/yrPoor value
                                  Avoid: ban laptopsRemoves the riskBreaks the business need for mobile work
                                  Accept with no controlA $30,000 loss every yearExceeds the stated tolerance for this class of data

                                  Decision: mitigate, then formally accept the remaining $15,000 ALE, mostly cheap hardware replacement and inside tolerance.

                                  Net value for the chosen option is ($30,000 - $15,000) - $4,000 = $11,000 per year. With encryption, only hardware and labor ($2,500 of the $10,000) are lost.

                                  Section 6 of 9

                                  The risk register: the record of decisions

                                  The living table that answers: what could go wrong, who owns it, how bad, what are we doing, is it done?

                                  HACKTHESOCLesson 116Risk management for the SOC31 / 54

                                  What each register column is for

                                  • IDFor reference from tickets, audits and incident reports.
                                  • Risk scenarioA full sentence containing the asset, threat, vulnerability and consequence.
                                  • OwnerA named person with authority to spend and decide, not a team name.
                                  • Inherent and residual scoreThe score before controls, and the score after.
                                  • TreatmentMitigate, transfer, avoid or accept, plus the specific controls or actions.
                                  • Status and review dateOpen, in progress, accepted or closed, and when it is next reviewed.
                                  Tie ID back to your tickets: a case that cites R-021 tells the reader which business risk the alert touches.
                                  HACKTHESOCLesson 116Risk management for the SOC32 / 54

                                  Three register rows that teach three lessons

                                  RiskOwner and scoresTreatment and status
                                  R-014: Theft or loss of an unencrypted laptop exposes customer data, ALE $30,000IT Operations Manager. Inherent 12 High, residual 4 LowMitigate: encryption plus remote wipe. In progress, 80% rolled out, review 2027-01-15
                                  R-021: Ransomware actor exploits unpatched VPN appliance and halts file servicesHead of Infrastructure. Inherent 20 Critical, residual 8 MediumMitigate: 7-day patch SLA, MFA, offline backups. Open, monthly review
                                  R-027: Legacy HR application cannot be patched and holds employee recordsHR Director. Inherent 9 Medium, residual 9 MediumAccept until replacement project completes. Accepted by CISO until 2027-06-30
                                  R-014 uses likelihood 4, impact 3 before encryption and likelihood 4, impact 1 after. R-021 is the kind of entry an analyst meets when a VPN alert fires.
                                  HACKTHESOCLesson 116Risk management for the SOC33 / 54

                                  What the rows teach

                                  An honest register

                                  R-014: controls move scores

                                  Inherent and residual diverge because of a control. But the residual only becomes valid after the rollout finishes; until then the real residual is still High.

                                    Not neglect

                                    R-027: acceptance has terms

                                    An accepted risk with an end date and an approver. Acceptance without those is just neglect.

                                      Scoring residual risk as if controls already work is a classic register pitfall.

                                      Ask students what they would write for R-014's residual today, at 80 percent rollout. The honest answer is still High.
                                      HACKTHESOCLesson 116Risk management for the SOC34 / 54

                                      Good practice for a register people actually use

                                      • One accountable owner per riskShared ownership means no ownership.
                                      • Link to evidenceTickets, exception approvals, scan results, incident numbers.
                                      • Re-score when something changesEspecially after an incident.
                                      • Keep it short enough to readA register of 900 undifferentiated rows is not used by anyone.
                                      • Use it from the SOCAn alert touching an asset in a High or Critical entry means the business already expects this to hurt.
                                      Other pitfalls: writing threats instead of scenarios, no owner, no review date, and never closing or revisiting entries.

                                      Section 7 of 9

                                      Controls: how they reduce risk and where the SOC fits

                                      Classify any control on two independent axes: function and type.

                                      HACKTHESOCLesson 116Risk management for the SOC36 / 54

                                      By function: what the control does in time

                                      Lowers likelihood

                                      Preventive

                                      Stops the event from happening or succeeding.

                                        Lowers impact

                                        Detective

                                        Identifies that something is happening or has happened. It shortens the time the attacker has.

                                          Lowers impact and recovery

                                          Corrective

                                          Limits damage and restores normal operation.

                                            Directive, deterrent and compensating are extra labels. Compensating means a substitute when the ideal control is not feasible.

                                            Detective is highlighted because that is where the SOC lives. Detection does not stop an event, it shortens how long the attacker has.
                                            HACKTHESOCLesson 116Risk management for the SOC37 / 54

                                            Function by type: place any control on the grid

                                            Type: administrative = policies and training, technical = software and hardware, physical = locks and cameras.

                                            Function / TypeAdministrativeTechnicalPhysical
                                            PreventiveAcceptable use policy, security awareness trainingMFA, patching, disk encryptionDoor locks, laptop cable locks
                                            DetectivePeriodic access review, auditSIEM correlation rule, EDR alert, IDSCCTV, intrusion alarm
                                            CorrectiveIncident response plan, restore procedureEDR host isolation, reimage from backup, remote wipeSpare hardware, fire suppression
                                            Quiz the class by naming a control and asking for its row and column. Remote wipe is corrective and technical, a camera is detective and physical.
                                            HACKTHESOCLesson 116Risk management for the SOC38 / 54
                                            SOC detections are detective technical controls.

                                            A rule on one account signing in from two countries within an hour does not stop the first sign-in, but it reduces how long a compromised account is abused.

                                            The analyst executing the playbook that follows is a corrective administrative control. Together they turn a silent breach lasting weeks into an incident contained in hours, which reduces the exposure factor in ALE.
                                            HACKTHESOCLesson 116Risk management for the SOC39 / 54

                                            Layers help, but controls carry their own risks

                                            • Defense in depthEncryption (preventive), remote wipe (corrective) and an alert when a lost device reports in (detective) cover one scenario from three sides.
                                            • Coverage and effectivenessEvery control has a coverage (which assets?) and an effectiveness (does it work?).
                                            • Reduction on paper onlyA rule nobody watches at 3 a.m., a log source silent since last month, or an EDR agent missing from 15 percent of servers.
                                            • Detection needs responseA detective control only reduces impact if alerts are actioned.
                                            • Tuning is a risk decisionSuppressing a noisy rule changes a control. Record it.
                                            This is the slide to remember when someone asks you to silence a rule. Silencing it is not a convenience, it changes the organization's risk.

                                            Section 8 of 9

                                            How risk management connects to the SOC day job

                                            Detect and respond quickly to shrink impact, and let asset criticality set priority.

                                            HACKTHESOCLesson 116Risk management for the SOC41 / 54
                                            A SOC exists to shrink the impact side of risk.

                                            Faster detection and containment means a smaller exposure factor and a lower single loss expectancy. MTTD and MTTR measure how much risk the SOC removes.

                                            Preventive controls will not stop everything: attackers find gaps, people make mistakes, new vulnerabilities appear faster than patching. Pitfalls to name: treating all alerts as equal, and reporting alert counts instead of risk reduced.
                                            HACKTHESOCLesson 116Risk management for the SOC42 / 54

                                            Same alert, different asset, different risk

                                            Impact depends on the asset, so the same alert does not carry the same risk everywhere.

                                            AlertAssetWhy the risk differs
                                            Commodity malware blockedReception kiosk with no dataLow impact, isolated, easy to rebuild
                                            Commodity malware executedDomain controllerHigh impact, control of every account, wide blast radius
                                            Failed logins, 200 in a minutePublic marketing siteLow-value target, expected noise
                                            Failed logins, 200 in a minuteVPN gateway for the finance teamDirect path to critical systems
                                            Identical event, very different risk. This is the practical reason a SOC needs an asset inventory with criticality tags.
                                            HACKTHESOCLesson 116Risk management for the SOC43 / 54

                                            Five questions to ask on every alert

                                            Priority is roughly threat severity combined with asset criticality, exposure, data sensitivity and current threat context.

                                            1. 1What is this asset?Use the inventory and criticality tags. A missing inventory is itself a finding.
                                            2. 2Who owns it?A good inventory names an owner for each asset.
                                            3. 3What depends on it?The data or function that relies on it sets the impact.
                                            4. 4Is it in the register?A High or Critical entry means the business already expects this to hurt.
                                            5. 5What should have stopped this?Name the control that should have prevented or caught it.

                                            Also ask: is it internet-facing or internal, and is this vulnerability being exploited right now?

                                            Where no inventory with criticality exists, report that gap. It is a finding in its own right.
                                            HACKTHESOCLesson 116Risk management for the SOC44 / 54

                                            An incident is a risk that materialized

                                            It is the best data the organization will ever get, so it feeds back into the register.

                                            Likelihood

                                            The event now has a real occurrence, so update the ARO or likelihood score.

                                              Impact

                                              Actual costs and downtime replace estimates.

                                                Gaps

                                                The post-incident review shows which preventive or detective controls failed: new or re-scored entries.

                                                  Treatment

                                                  A risk that was accepted may no longer be acceptable.

                                                    Record root cause, affected asset and detection source in the case, and cite register IDs.

                                                    Also record when you close an alert as a false positive because a control or log source is mis-tuned. Leadership funds the SOC when it can say: here is what we detected, here is what it would have cost, here is the gap we found.

                                                    Frameworks at a glance: CSF, ISO 27001/27005, FAIR

                                                    They give structure, a common language with auditors and customers, and a checklist against forgetting something.

                                                    HACKTHESOCLesson 116Risk management for the SOC46 / 54

                                                    Five references and what each is best used for

                                                    FrameworkWhat it isCertifiableBest used for
                                                    NIST CSF 2.0Six functions: Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, RecoverNoDescribing and improving posture; talking to leadership
                                                    ISO/IEC 27001ISMS requirements, with mandatory risk assessment and treatmentYesDemonstrating a managed program to customers and regulators

                                                    ISMS stands for information security management system. Only ISO 27001 is certifiable, which is why customers ask for it.
                                                    HACKTHESOCLesson 116Risk management for the SOC47 / 54

                                                    CSF 2.0: the SOC lives in Detect and Respond

                                                    Govern sits on top; the other five follow the order of an incident.

                                                    1. 1GovernAdded in version 2.0: risk strategy, roles and policy sit at the top.
                                                    2. 2IdentifyAsset and risk understanding. The inputs to SOC work come from here.
                                                    3. 3ProtectControls, first in the order of an incident.
                                                    4. 4DetectWhere SOC work lives, together with Respond.
                                                    5. 5RespondWhere SOC work lives, together with Detect.
                                                    6. 6RecoverRestoring normal operation, the last function in incident order.

                                                    CSF uses Profiles (current and target) and Tiers to express maturity.

                                                    Map this back to the lifecycle slide: Identify here is where your detection inputs originate, and Detect and Respond are your daily job.
                                                    HACKTHESOCLesson 116Risk management for the SOC48 / 54

                                                    ISO 27001, ISO 27005 and FAIR in one view

                                                    Requirements

                                                    ISO/IEC 27001

                                                    Run a defined risk assessment process, select treatments, justify Annex A controls in the Statement of Applicability, and have management accept residual risk.

                                                      Guidance

                                                      ISO/IEC 27005

                                                      Explains methods for doing the assessment itself, in support of 27001.

                                                        Estimation model

                                                        FAIR

                                                        Not a control list. Splits risk into how often a loss occurs and how much it costs, uses calibrated ranges, and simulates a distribution of annual loss.

                                                          FAIR is the disciplined version of SLE and ALE: it addresses false precision and hidden uncertainty.

                                                          FAIR stands for Factor Analysis of Information Risk. A common pattern: ISO 27001 or the NIST RMF runs the program, CSF benchmarks it, matrices triage everything, FAIR-style quantification handles the few budget decisions.

                                                          Final checklist of five pitfalls

                                                          • Threat mistaken for riskWrite scenarios, not single words.
                                                          • Ignoring residual riskAlways score it and have it accepted.
                                                          • Treating every risk as mitigateWeigh cost, and use transfer, avoid and accept deliberately.
                                                          • A stale registerLetting it go stale after incidents and system changes.
                                                          • Detective controls as protectionCounting them as protection when no one acts on the alerts.
                                                          Use this as a closing self-check. If a student can explain why each one is wrong, they have the lesson.

                                                          Key takeaways

                                                          1. 01A risk is a scenario: asset, threat actor, vulnerability and impact. Ransomware alone is a threat, not a risk.
                                                          2. 02Risk management is a loop: identify, assess, treat, monitor. The SOC lives mostly in Monitor.
                                                          3. 03Qualitative scores (likelihood x impact) rank risks; quantitative ALE = SLE x ARO prices them in money per year.
                                                          4. 04Treat with mitigate, transfer, avoid or accept; acceptance needs an owner, an approver and a review date.
                                                          5. 05SOC detections are detective technical controls, and asset criticality sets alert priority.
                                                          6. 06Incidents are risks that materialized: feed real frequency and cost back into the register.

                                                          A file server holds data worth $80,000 to the business. A single incident is expected to destroy 25 percent of that value, and such incidents are expected about once every two years. What is the ALE?

                                                          SLE = $80,000 x 0.25 = $20,000. ARO is one incident every two years, 0.5 per year, so ALE = $20,000 x 0.5 = $10,000. The $20,000 figure stops at SLE and forgets frequency. The $40,000 and $2,500 figures come from no formula in the model.
