Report as afterthought

A chore banged out after the 'real work' is done. It produces vague, unusable reports that nobody can act on.

Report as the product

What separates a usable section from a weak one

Impact and scope

Be precise about confirmed versus suspected. Overstating causes panic; understating causes complacency.

Root cause

"Macro execution was not blocked by policy" is a root cause. Without it, the organization fixes symptoms and gets hit again.

Recommendations

"Block macro execution from internet-sourced Office files via GPO" beats "improve email security".

Every recommendation should tie back to something in the report. A recommendation with no basis in the findings is noise.
HACKTHESOCLesson 26Writing the incident report17 / 77

Recommendations come in three time horizons

  1. 1NowContain and eradicate. Example: reset r.alvarez's password across all systems.
  2. 2SoonClose the gap, patch, tune the detection. Example: block macros from internet-sourced Office files via GPO.
  3. 3LaterPolicy or architecture change. Example: a detection rule for winword.exe spawning powershell.exe.
  4. 4Every one tied to a findingA recommendation with no basis in the findings is noise. Each needs an owner.
Read the examples against the worked report in the next section: each recommendation answers a specific line in the root cause or timeline.

Section 4 of 10

Writing for executives and responders

Two readers with opposite needs, served in the same document.

HACKTHESOCLesson 26Writing the incident report24 / 77

One wants less, the other wants more

Reads for a decision

The executive

How bad? Notify anyone? Contained? What will it cost? Plain language, bottom line up front, no field names or command lines.

Reads for action

The responder

Every hostname, IP, hash, timestamp, Event ID and record number. 'A database server' is useless; SRV-DB02 (10.4.5.30) is actionable.

"Access to a system with customer records for three hours" means something to an executive. "GrantedAccess 0x1010" does not.

If you write only for one reader, you fail the other, and a report that fails either audience fails the incident.
HACKTHESOCLesson 26Writing the incident report25 / 77
Layer the report like a pyramid: plain conclusion on top, precise evidence below.

Each reader goes to their layer and stops. Never water down the detail, never bury the critical business fact.

This is layering, not compromise. The executive reads the summary and stops; the responder skims past it and dives in.
HACKTHESOCLesson 26Writing the incident report26 / 77

Building the layers, top to bottom

  1. 1Executive summaryPlain-language conclusion and status. The decision-maker reads it and stops.
  2. 2TimelineThe UTC, cited backbone that every other section refers back to.
  3. 3IOCs and technical findingsExact hostnames, IPs, hashes, Event IDs and record numbers.
  4. 4AppendicesThe deepest detail, such as long lists of affected users.
Never bury the critical business fact in the technical weeds, and never strip the technical evidence to keep it short.
HACKTHESOCLesson 26Writing the incident report27 / 77

Same fact, two registers

Same facts. The executive reads three sentences and decides; the responder reads the detail and acts.

Section 5 of 10

Evidence discipline: never invent, always cite

Every claim traces to a real artifact you observed. There is no third option.

HACKTHESOCLesson 26Writing the incident report30 / 77

Why fabrication is the cardinal sin

What a responder does

Blocks it, sweeps the estate for it, and hunts every other host that talked to it.

If the value was invented

The whole response chases a ghost while the real C2 keeps beaconing. Every downstream action rests on a false fact.

This platform's incident-report grader penalizes fabricated data: an IP, hostname or user that appears nowhere in the evidence.

Fabricated data does not just make a report inaccurate; it makes it harmful. That is why it is treated as a failure of the report's core purpose, not a style issue.
HACKTHESOCLesson 26Writing the incident report31 / 77

If a value is not in the evidence, it does not go in the report

  • Copy exact values: do not paraphrase, round or leave a placeholder
  • Cite the log source and identifier so anyone can pull the same record
When a responder reads that the C2 was 198.51.100.44, they block it, sweep for it and hunt other hosts that talked to it. An invented value sends all of that after a ghost; the grader penalizes fabrication.
HACKTHESOCLesson 26Writing the incident report32 / 77

Quote the real values, then cite where they live

  • Copy exactlyHostname WKS-4471, user svc_backup, IP 10.4.5.30, time 2026-07-02 11:47:20 UTC.
  • Do not round or paraphraseAnd never leave a placeholder you forget to fill in.
  • Cite source and identifier'Windows Security Event ID 4624, LogonType 3' or 'Sysmon Event ID 1, EventRecordID 88231'.
  • WhyAnyone can pull the same record and confirm it. A claim with a citation is evidence.
Copy-paste from the console beats retyping. Most 'fabrication' in junior reports is a misremembered value, not a lie.
HACKTHESOCLesson 26Writing the incident report33 / 77

Observation, inference and unknown are written differently

KindExampleHow to write it
ObservationThe EDR alert on LSASS accessState it with its citation: CONFIRMED
Inference"The attacker dumped credentials""LSASS accessed (EDR alert, T1003.001), consistent with credential dumping": LIKELY
UnknownWhether stolen credentials were used"Not confirmed whether the credentials were used": UNKNOWN or UNDER INVESTIGATION
Never launder an inference into a fact by stating it flatly. An honest 'not confirmed' is worth more than a confident guess that turns out wrong.
HACKTHESOCLesson 26Writing the incident report34 / 77

Likelihood and confidence are two different dials

Likelihood

How probable you judge the event to be. Expressed with a phrase from a fixed scale, such as 'likely'.

Confidence

How good the evidence behind that judgment is: high, moderate or low.

Together: "Likely (moderate confidence): the LSASS access at 10:05 UTC was credential dumping."

Moderate confidence here because the access mask 0x1010 matches common dumping tools, but no dump file was recovered. The reason is part of the claim.
HACKTHESOCLesson 26Writing the incident report35 / 77

Pin confidence words to one fixed scale

The scale most often borrowed: US intelligence estimative language (ICD 203).

PhraseProbability band
Almost no chance1-5%
Very unlikely5-20%
Unlikely20-45%
Roughly even chance45-55%
Likely55-80%
Very likely80-95%
Almost certainly95-99%

Likelihood is how probable the event is; confidence (high, moderate, low) is how good the evidence is.

'Possibly' and 'probably' mean different things to different readers. Pick one scale for the team, print it in the template footer, and use only its words.
HACKTHESOCLesson 26Writing the incident report36 / 77

Each claim: observation, judgment, reason for confidence

  • A reviewer can disagree with the judgment without re-running the case
  • Nobody mistakes a 'likely' for a 'confirmed'
Never launder an inference into a fact by stating it flatly. The reason for the confidence level is part of the claim.
HACKTHESOCLesson 26Writing the incident report37 / 77

Gaps are findings, not embarrassments

  • The temptationCould not find the initial access event, so write a plausible-sounding one.
  • Why it is backwardsA gap may mean a missing log source, a detection that did not fire, or covered tracks.
  • The honest finding"No evidence of the initial access vector was recovered. DNS logging was not enabled on the 10.4.5.0/24 segment."
  • The resultAn actionable recommendation to close a logging gap, instead of a false fact.
Report what you saw, report what you did not see, never report what you merely imagined.

HACKTHESOCLesson 26Writing the incident report40 / 77

Rewrite drill: vague to specific

  • Trigger words: some, various, certain, suspicious, with no specific after
  • Which server, what activity, when? Each question becomes a value
  • If a recommendation cannot be assigned and checked off, it is too vague
Run this as a live exercise: give students three vague sentences from old tickets and have them rewrite each with a value and a citation.
HACKTHESOCLesson 26Writing the incident report41 / 77

Missing impact leaves the key question unanswered

No impact statement

Every technical step is described, but nobody says what it means for the business. The decision-maker has to guess the severity.

Explicit impact

"A system holding customer PII was accessed" or "no sensitive data was reachable from the compromised host", marked confirmed or suspected.

Overstating impact causes panic; understating causes complacency. Precision about confirmed versus suspected is what keeps the business response proportionate.
HACKTHESOCLesson 26Writing the incident report42 / 77

A template that forces every reader's question

  • The evidence column in the timeline is mandatory
  • No evidence? Write "not observed" or "under investigation", never invent
A template does not make you a good writer, but it makes it very hard to omit something critical. In reporting, omissions are what hurt.

The report you write most: closure notes

The few lines that close an alert or case, written dozens of times a shift and read cold by others.

HACKTHESOCLesson 26Writing the incident report45 / 77

Four dispositions, each with a precise meaning

DispositionWhat it meansSentinel close label
True positive (TP)Real malicious or unauthorized activity; becomes or joins an incidentTrue Positive - suspicious activity
Benign true positive (BTP)Logic fired correctly on real activity that was authorized or expectedBenign Positive - suspicious but expected
False positive (FP)The alert does not describe what happened: wrong rule logic or wrong dataFalse Positive - incorrect alert logic / incorrect data
UndeterminedEvidence supports neither verdict, often a missing log sourceUndetermined
Map your team's categories onto whatever the tool offers and use them consistently: they feed false-positive rate per rule and time to close, which decide what gets tuned. An undetermined verdict is honest, and the missing source is a finding.
HACKTHESOCLesson 26Writing the incident report46 / 77

Choosing the disposition: three questions

  1. 1Did it happen as the alert says?No: wrong rule logic or wrong data. False positive.
  2. 2Yes: was it authorized or expected?Change window, scheduled scan, red-team exercise: benign true positive.
  3. 3Yes, and not authorizedTrue positive: becomes an incident or joins an existing one.
  4. 4Evidence supports neither?Undetermined. The missing log source is a finding of its own.
The order matters: question one separates FP from everything else, question two separates BTP from TP. Skipping question one is how real activity gets called an FP.
HACKTHESOCLesson 26Writing the incident report47 / 77

BTP versus FP is the split analysts most often get wrong

Fix the rule

False positive

A pattern that matches psexec anywhere in a file path instead of the process image, or a parser that put a value in the wrong field.

The rule is fine

Benign true positive

An admin running PsExec in a change window, a scheduled vulnerability scan, a red-team exercise. Needs an exception, a change record, or nothing.

Logging a BTP as an FP sends a detection engineer off to weaken a rule that works.

Ask: did the activity really happen as the alert says? If yes and it was authorized, it is a BTP, not an FP.
HACKTHESOCLesson 26Writing the incident report48 / 77

The true positive note: escalated, contained

Same rule as the next two notes. What makes this one a TP: no change record, no history of this use, and an open credential-access alert on the source host.
HACKTHESOCLesson 26Writing the incident report49 / 77

Five things every closure note contains

  1. 1What firedRule name, host, user, time in UTC.
  2. 2The dispositionTP, BTP, FP or undetermined, in the tool's own label.
  3. 3The deciding evidenceThe specific log and value, a change ticket, or confirmation via a verified channel.
  4. 4The action takenEscalated to an incident ID, contained, or none.
  5. 5Any follow-upA tuning request, an exception to add, a logging gap.

'Closed - FP' on its own fails all five, and the next analyst starts from zero.

The next analyst, the detection engineer tuning the rule and the QA reviewer sampling closed tickets all read the note cold.
HACKTHESOCLesson 26Writing the incident report50 / 77

One PsExec rule, closed two different ways

Both notes close the same rule. The BTP needs an exception, the FP needs the rule fixed: two very different follow-ups from one alert name.

Before it leaves your hands: review, redaction, TLP

A second analyst checks the evidence, unneeded personal data comes out, and a label says who may see it.

HACKTHESOCLesson 26Writing the incident report53 / 77

Three gates before a report leaves your hands

  1. 1Peer reviewA second analyst checks the draft against the cited logs.
  2. 2RedactionPersonal and sensitive data that readers do not need comes out.
  3. 3LabelAn explicit TLP label states who may see it.

The report can hold compromised credentials, internal IP ranges, employee names and where defenses failed.

Each gate protects someone: the responders from a wrong value, the employees from exposure, the organization from a leak.
HACKTHESOCLesson 26Writing the incident report54 / 77

Peer review checks the draft against the cited logs

Ideally by an analyst who did not work the case, and never from memory.

  • Action-driving valuesThe host to isolate, the account to disable, each IOC: spot-check them first in the log the report cites.
  • Confidence wordsEvery claim carries a word from the team's confidence scale.
  • Summary matches body'No data accessed' above an impact section saying 'under investigation' is the classic mismatch.
  • UTC and ownersAll timestamps are UTC, and every recommendation has an owner.

Reviewer name and review date go in the document-control block next to the version number.

The report can hold compromised credentials, internal IP ranges, employee names and a candid account of where defenses failed, so each pre-release step protects someone.
HACKTHESOCLesson 26Writing the incident report55 / 77
Redaction rule: keep only what a reader needs to act.

Reports collect personal data along the way: employee names, email addresses, the text of a lure sent to a named customer, a screenshot of someone's inbox.

The test for every personal detail: would a reader take a different action without it? If not, it comes out or goes to a controlled appendix.
HACKTHESOCLesson 26Writing the incident report56 / 77

Redact: keep only what a reader needs to act

  • People by account and role'r.alvarez, Finance AP clerk', not a home address or personal phone number.
  • Long user listsGo in a separately controlled appendix, not the body.
  • Mask unneeded valuesA card number shown as its last four digits.
  • Never paste secretsNo recovered credentials, session tokens or API keys, even revoked: record found, where, and when revoked.
  • Redact images properlyRemove the pixels and re-export; a black box on a PDF layer can be lifted off.
Reports collect personal data along the way: names, email addresses, the text of a lure sent to a named customer, a screenshot of someone's inbox.
HACKTHESOCLesson 26Writing the incident report57 / 77

Two redactions that are often done wrong

Wrong: secrets pasted in

A recovered credential, session token or API key copied into the report, even a revoked one.

Right: record the fact

Record that it was found, where, and when it was revoked. The value itself never appears.

Wrong: black box on a layer

A box drawn over a screenshot in a PDF can be lifted off by the reader.

Right: remove the pixels

Redact the image itself, then re-export the file.

Both mistakes leave the sensitive value in the document while making it look removed, which is worse than an obvious omission.

The TLP header goes on the first line

  • Never re-share at a more permissive label than it arrived with
  • If unsure, ask the originator; do not guess down to CLEAR
Wrong in either direction costs: CLEAR on a sensitive report leaks it; RED on a shareable write-up keeps the warning from peers who needed it.

Two opposite failures of cadence

False confidence

A preliminary notification dressed up as if it were the final word. Every early fact is subject to change.

Silence

No communication while you wait to produce a final-quality report, when stakeholders needed a two-sentence update hours ago.

Stakeholders left in silence for two days will assume the worst, or hear about the incident from someone else first.

What the early updates actually say

  • Preliminary: what we know, what we do not, when you hear next
  • Interim: what changed, so followers see progress at a glance
  • Both promise the next update time
Interim updates go out on a fixed cadence, once per shift or daily, or whenever a material fact emerges: a new host, containment achieved, severity revised.

Plain language, active voice, inverted pyramid

Habits from plain-language guidance and journalism that make accurate sentences readable.

HACKTHESOCLesson 26Writing the incident report68 / 77

Three sentence-level habits

  • Active voice"The attacker accessed LSASS memory" beats "unauthorized memory access activity was observed to have occurred".
  • One idea per sentenceState the vector. State the timestamp and evidence. State what was done. Three short sentences beat one dense one.
  • No jargon leakage"Lateral movement was effectuated via the utilization of SMB" is only longer than "the attacker moved laterally over SMB".
  • Inverted pyramidLead each paragraph with the conclusion, then the supporting detail, not the other way around.
Plain-language principles are formalized in U.S. federal guidance under the Plain Writing Act. Reserve technical precision for exact field names, Event IDs and command lines.

A nominalization turns a verb into a noun ('access' into 'access activity'), adding words without meaning. Technical precision belongs in field names, Event IDs and command lines, not in connecting prose.

  • One 60-word sentence carrying three ideas becomes three short sentences
  • "We found no use": plain, and not claiming more than the checks showed

Key takeaways

  1. 01The report is the product: others act on what you wrote, and it outlives the incident as memory and legal record.
  2. 02A complete report has executive summary, timeline, IOCs, MITRE map, impact and scope, root cause and recommendations.
  3. 03Layer the report: a plain, decision-focused summary on top, precise action-ready detail below.
  4. 04Every claim traces to a real artifact: quote exact values, cite the log, use one confidence scale, never invent.
  5. 05Closure notes: state the disposition precisely and never log a benign true positive as a false positive.
  6. 06Before release: peer review against the evidence, redact unneeded personal data and secrets, label with TLP.
