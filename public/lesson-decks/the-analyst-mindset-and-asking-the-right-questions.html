Two analysts, the same alert, opposite conclusions. The difference is almost never tooling. It is how they think.

Section 1 of 11

Hypothesis-driven, not checkbox-driven

Run queries chosen to confirm or kill an idea, not the same five every time.

HACKTHESOCLesson 23The analyst mindset6 / 44

A feeling versus a hypothesis

A feeling

"This looks suspicious." Makes no predictions, so no query can test it.

A hypothesis

"A phishing email led this user to run a malicious macro that established C2 to 203.0.113.5." Names actors, actions and artifacts, so it predicts evidence.

Hunt hardest for evidence that would prove it wrong. A theory that survives a real attempt to break it is far stronger.

If you predict a malicious email and the mail logs show nothing, that is enormously valuable: you just avoided a false conclusion.
HACKTHESOCLesson 23The analyst mindset7 / 44

Three hypotheses for one impossible-travel alert

  • B and C were tested and BROKEN: that is what makes A defensible
Holding three hypotheses is the built-in defense against tunnel vision. Propose, predict, test, let the data decide.

The six questions

Who, what, when, where, how and so-what: a framework that works on anything.

HACKTHESOCLesson 23The analyst mindset9 / 44

Five questions describe the event

  • WhoEvery identity and asset: user, service account, hosts, process, parent. "jsmith on FINANCE-07", not "a user".
  • WhatAn action on an object, stated before interpreting. If you need the word "attack", you jumped ahead.
  • WhenAn ordered timeline. Causality lives in ordering, and 3 a.m. versus working hours is context.
  • WhereSegment, host, geography, tenant. Gives blast radius and whether the location is itself anomalous.
  • HowMechanism mapped to ATT&CK: T1566 phishing, T1059 interpreter, T1021 remote services.
'Who' gives you the pivot points you will follow across every other log source.
HACKTHESOCLesson 23The analyst mindset10 / 44

So-what: build the impact chain

Keep asking "and then what could they do?"

  1. 1Malicious macro runsSo what?
  2. 2C2 establishedSo what?
  3. 3Credential theftSo what?
  4. 4Lateral move to a DCSo what?
  5. 5Domain-wide ransomwareNow the business risk is clear.

Lead with the so-what chain when you escalate: it is the language leadership acts on.

An analyst who answers five questions but not the sixth has described an event without explaining why anyone should care.
HACKTHESOCLesson 23The analyst mindset11 / 44

Six lines filled in is an investigation

Point out where each answer lives. So-what often comes from the system owner or a change ticket, not a log.
HACKTHESOCLesson 23The analyst mindset12 / 44

All six, applied to one EDR alert

If you can fill all six lines, you have an investigation, not just an alert.

Section 3 of 11

Fact vs assumption

The worst SOC errors come from confidently reporting what was never observed.

HACKTHESOCLesson 23The analyst mindset14 / 44

Every statement is one or the other

Fact

What telemetry directly recorded. "Sysmon Event ID 3: powershell.exe on FINANCE-07 connected to 203.0.113.5:443 at 09:06:04." You can point to the line.

Assumption

A conclusion drawn but not observed. "The attacker used that connection to exfiltrate data." Maybe likely, but not what the log said.

For every important sentence ask: which log line proves this?

Labeling an inference as fact can trigger an unnecessary breach notification, accuse an employee, or send IR chasing a ghost.
HACKTHESOCLesson 23The analyst mindset15 / 44

The two-column discipline

  • Every reported IOC must trace to a left-column observation
  • Right-column only means a hunting lead, not evidence
Inventing an IOC is the cardinal sin: it gets blocked, hunted and shared with other teams, all based on nothing.

Baselining: know normal first

"Anomaly" has no meaning without a baseline.

HACKTHESOCLesson 23The analyst mindset17 / 44

Three scopes of normal

ScopeWhat defines normal
UserWorking hours, days, time zone; usual cities, devices, network; apps, shares and data volume
Host (role-based)Services, peers, whether it browses. A DC does not browse; workstations do not SMB each other
EnvironmentTotal auth volume, login geography, usual egress destinations, daily and weekly cycles
A finance clerk at 3 a.m. from a new country pulling 10 GB from a code repository: every deviation matters only because you knew the baseline.
HACKTHESOCLesson 23The analyst mindset18 / 44

Learn normal, then score deviations

Step 1 runs over 30 days and saves a lookup; step 2 runs live. You can only score deviations because step 1 defined normal.
HACKTHESOCLesson 23The analyst mindset19 / 44

Baselines come from data and from people

Data

Historical telemetry: typical and 95th-percentile login hours, usual destinations, byte volumes. UEBA automates per-entity profiles.

People

Tribal knowledge: which service accounts touch admin shares, the nightly batch job that looks like staging, Tuesday's vendor scanner. Write it down.

The worst false positives come from not knowing the benign explanation; the worst misses from not knowing what should never happen.

Cognitive biases and when to escalate

Errors that feel like clear thinking from the inside.

HACKTHESOCLesson 23The analyst mindset21 / 44

Three biases and their antidotes

BiasWhat it isAntidote
Confirmation biasWeighting evidence that fits your belief, dismissing what contradicts itRun a query that could break your theory
AnchoringOver-relying on the first information, like the alert titleAsk what you would conclude seeing it fresh
Alert fatigueAttention eroded by a flood of mostly-false alertsTreat each as possibly real, take breaks, tune noisy rules
After the two-hundredth benign VPN 'impossible travel', muscle memory says close, and that is when the real one slips through.

Self-check and escalation, in one card

The failure mode is not escalating too often. It is sitting on a genuine incident for hours out of pride, trying to be certain.

Here is what I know, here is what I don't, and here is why I need you: that honesty is what senior analysts respect most.

Section 6 of 11

Analysis of competing hypotheses

A structured technique from intelligence analysis that defeats confirmation bias.

HACKTHESOCLesson 23The analyst mindset25 / 44

ACH in four moves

  1. 1Enumerate hypothesesLate-night login: compromised, working late, admin script, misconfigured service.
  2. 2Matrix the evidenceFor every piece of evidence, consistent or inconsistent with each hypothesis?
  3. 3Seek disconfirmationA valid password fits all four and is worth little. Inconsistency is what moves you.
  4. 4Survivor winsYou eliminate the others rather than proving a favourite right.

Always hold at least two explanations and hunt for what would kill each.

ACH forces 'probably the pentest' or 'probably a service account' to survive disconfirmation instead of being assumed, and makes reasoning reviewable.

Every artifact is a key into another data set.

Section 9 of 11

Knowing when you are done

Closure, confidence, and case notes someone else can trust.

HACKTHESOCLesson 23The analyst mindset33 / 44

Three parts of a professional close

Reasoned closure

State it against the hypotheses: "H1 eliminated because X; H2 supported by Y and Z." What happened, scope, disposition.

Explicit confidence

High, medium or low, plus what would change your mind. "Looks fine" is not a finding.

Notes for a stranger

Written as you go, so a colleague can pick it up cold and the reasoning survives review, audit or legal process.

Some close too early, others never close. Neither serves the SOC.

Example finding: 'Assessed benign with medium confidence; would revise if the same source reappears or EDR later flags the process.'

Structured frameworks: OODA and the Diamond

One structures the tempo of an investigation, the other how you describe it.

HACKTHESOCLesson 23The analyst mindset35 / 44

The OODA loop, John Boyd

  1. 1ObserveGather raw telemetry: the alert fires, you pull the logs.
  2. 2OrientInterpret against baselines and frameworks. Hypotheses and six questions live here.
  3. 3DecideChoose the next action: which query, whether to escalate.
  4. 4ActRun it or isolate the host, producing new observations. Loop.

The attacker runs their own loop. Whoever cycles faster gains the advantage.

A SOC that takes six hours to act hands the attacker five hours and fifty-nine minutes of unopposed cycles. Each pivot in the trace was one OODA cycle.

Thinking in probabilities: the base rate

Why a highly accurate rule can still fill the queue with false positives.

HACKTHESOCLesson 23The analyst mindset38 / 44

A 0.01% false-positive rate, worked through

  • A perfect catch rate still yields a queue that is over 98% noise
  • Bayes: the prior (rarity of intrusions) shapes trust as much as accuracy
Stefan Axelsson formalized this for intrusion detection in a classic 1999 paper.
HACKTHESOCLesson 23The analyst mindset39 / 44

Four terms that shape your priors

TermMeaningWhy it matters
Base rateHow common real intrusions actually areLow base rate: even accurate detectors mostly produce noise
False-positive rateHow often the detector fires on benign activitySmall in isolation, multiplied across millions of events
PPVFraction of alerts that are actually trueThe number that tells you how much to trust an alert type
Prosecutor's fallacyReading a low FP rate as a high chance the alert is realIgnores the base rate; overconfidence in noisy rules
Rare, historically real alert types deserve a strong prior; noisy ones need corroboration before escalation. Tuning raises PPV, which makes it a security control.

Key takeaways

  1. 01Form a specific, testable hypothesis and hunt hardest for the evidence that would disprove it.
  2. 02Ask who, what, when, where, how and so-what; the so-what impact chain is what drives escalation.
  3. 03Separate fact from assumption, label every inference's confidence, and never report an unsourced IOC.
  4. 04You cannot spot abnormal without normal: baseline users, hosts and the environment from data and people.
  5. 05Watch for confirmation bias, anchoring and fatigue; escalate on reasonable suspicion, not certainty.
  6. 06Pivot source by source, hold competing hypotheses, and close with confidence and notes for a stranger.

A PowerShell download cradle alert fires. You believe it is malware and run only confirming queries, skipping mail and proxy logs that might show a benign software deployment. Which practice best corrects this?

This is confirmation bias. The correction is hypothesis-driven investigation: hold plausible alternatives, including a benign deployment tool, and hunt for disconfirming evidence such as the mail and proxy logs you were about to skip.
