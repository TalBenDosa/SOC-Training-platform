Section 1 of 12

Hypothesis, baseline, and the hunt loop

Three terms carry this whole lesson, each with a precise meaning in hunting.

HACKTHESOCLesson 34Threat hunting6 / 90

Three terms the whole lesson rests on

  • HypothesisA written one- or two-sentence statement of what an adversary might be doing now, precise enough for one query to test.
  • BaselineWhat normal looks like in your environment, observed over a representative stretch of time.
  • The hunt loopHypothesis, query, analyze, then escalate or document, and on to the next hypothesis.
Pin these down before the method: every later section assumes all three.
HACKTHESOCLesson 34Threat hunting7 / 90

A hypothesis is narrow enough for one query to test

Not a hypothesis

'Someone might be doing something bad.' No technique, no scope, no query can prove or disprove it.

A hypothesis

'An adversary may be using scheduled tasks with legitimate-sounding names to persist on our Windows servers.' One technique, one scope, one query.

The test: could one query return evidence if it is true, and come back empty if it is false?

Same idea as an everyday educated guess you can test, made specific. Ask students to rewrite the first column into something like the second before moving on.
HACKTHESOCLesson 34Threat hunting8 / 90

Unusual only means something once you know usual

Without a baselineWith a baseline
A scheduled task nameCould be alarming or routine: no way to tellKnown to be an everyday admin script here, or never seen before
A one-off process nameEvery single occurrence looks suspiciousExplained by your variety: a dev shop vs a locked-down call center
The time windowHours: a monthly patch cycle looks like an anomalyWeeks: routine but infrequent activity is already in normal
A task name that would alarm one company can be a daily admin script at another. Baselining is unglamorous, but every rarity technique later in the lesson depends on it.
HACKTHESOCLesson 34Threat hunting9 / 90
A mature hunting program is never finished. It is always mid-loop.

The output of one hunt, a new rule, proven coverage, or an exposed gap, shapes the next hypothesis.

The rest of the lesson teaches running that loop well: where hypotheses come from, how a baseline shows what stands out, and how every loop closes.
HACKTHESOCLesson 34Threat hunting10 / 90

Both branches of the loop feed the next hypothesis

  1. 1HypothesisA specific, testable statement.
  2. 2Query the dataPull exactly what tests it.
  3. 3Analyse resultsAsk: is there a finding?
  4. 4Yes: escalateEscalate to IR and write a detection rule.
  5. 5No: documentRecord proven coverage or a visibility gap.
  6. 6Back to a hypothesisEach outcome shapes the next hunt.
A finding becomes a rule that removes that gap from future hunts. A clean hunt gives coverage to build on or a gap to close before the next attempt.

Hunting vs. triage: proactive versus reactive

Not a difference of degree, but of the starting assumption.

HACKTHESOCLesson 34Threat hunting12 / 90

Triage answers alerts; hunting searches where no rule looks

Triage (reactive)Hunting (proactive)
StartsAn alert firedA hypothesis, no alert fired
QuestionIs this alert real?Is an adversary here undetected?
Data usedThe alert plus quick pivotsRaw telemetry, broad queries
VolumeHigh, time-boxed per caseLow, deliberate, exploratory
SuccessCorrect verdict, fastFinding OR proven coverage
FeedsEscalation, containmentNew detection rules
Detections only catch what someone thought to write a rule for. Skilled adversaries study which behaviors get detected and operate outside that boundary.
HACKTHESOCLesson 34Threat hunting13 / 90

Why a patient attacker can generate zero alerts

  • Rules are frozen hypothesesEvery detection rule encodes one idea about attacker behavior, as it was understood when the rule was written.
  • Adversaries study the boundarySkilled attackers learn which behaviors get detected, including by recon on common EDR products, and operate outside it.
  • Discipline beats noiseA patient attacker avoids noisy techniques. Not invisible: their sequence of actions was simply never anticipated by a rule.
  • The data is often already thereThe evidence can sit in the SIEM for months. What is missing is a rule that looks at that combination.
This is the reason hunting exists. The problem is rarely missing logs alone; it is that nothing is looking at the right combination of them.
HACKTHESOCLesson 34Threat hunting14 / 90
The gap: data that sits in your SIEM for months while no rule looks at the combination that reveals the intrusion.

Triage can only catch what a rule anticipated. Hunting searches inside that gap on purpose.

A patient attacker can generate zero alerts, not because they are invisible, but because their sequence of actions was never anticipated by any rule.
HACKTHESOCLesson 34Threat hunting15 / 90

A mature SOC needs both, not one or the other

Only triage

Only ever as good as its detection engineering, forever one clever technique behind.

Only hunting

Drowns the moment a real alert needs an urgent response and nobody has bandwidth.

Both, feeding each other

A successful hunt becomes a new rule, moving that technique from the hunting workload into triage permanently.

The hunter's question: if I wanted to stay hidden here, what would I do, and where would the evidence live?

Hunters think like an adversary, not a rule-writer: imagine the goal, then work backward to the artifacts that goal must leave behind.
HACKTHESOCLesson 34Threat hunting16 / 90

The hunter mindset: work backward from the goal

Not "is this alert real?" but "if I wanted to stay hidden here, what would I do?"

  1. 1Pick an attacker goalPersistence, lateral movement, or data collection.
  2. 2Imagine doing it quietly hereIn this specific environment, avoiding the noisy techniques your rules already catch.
  3. 3List the artifacts it must leaveEvery goal leaves evidence somewhere: a task, a process, a connection, a ticket request.
  4. 4Check what watches themIf no existing rule looks at those artifacts, you have found the gap.
  5. 5Formalize itTurn that idea into a written hypothesis, the subject of the next section.
Hunters think like an adversary, not a rule-writer. The rest of the lesson turns this occasional flash of intuition into a repeatable process.

Section 3 of 12

The hypothesis: where a good hunt begins

Write it down before you touch a single log.

HACKTHESOCLesson 34Threat hunting18 / 90

Three reliable sources of hypotheses

MITRE ATT&CK

Pick a technique with weak or no coverage, e.g. T1055 or T1218. Sub-techniques like T1055.012 Process Hollowing give specificity for free.

Threat intelligence

A report on an actor relevant to your industry or region details a TTP. Hypothesize that actor, or a copycat, is using it on you.

Environmental knowledge

Known blind spots: a legacy system with no EDR, an unreviewed over-privileged service account, a segment with weak logging.

The most common new-hunter failure is opening the SIEM and scrolling, hoping something looks wrong. 'Let's look at the logs' is the absence of a plan.
HACKTHESOCLesson 34Threat hunting19 / 90

From too broad to testable: rewriting a draft

DraftProblemTestable rewrite
"Someone is using process injection"Too broad: a whole technique familyT1055.012 Process Hollowing: malicious code inside a legitimate process image
"Something suspicious on our servers"Not falsifiable, not scopedSYSTEM-run scheduled tasks with plausible names on our Windows servers (T1053.005)
"Check every process for maliciousness"Unfalsifiable in practiceOne technique, one scope, answerable in hours, not weeks
ATT&CK sub-techniques give specificity for free. Use this table as a class exercise: give a vague draft, ask for a rewrite.
HACKTHESOCLesson 34Threat hunting20 / 90

Building the scheduled-task hypothesis from an intel report

  1. 1Read the intelAn actor persists via SYSTEM-run scheduled tasks with plausible names on Windows Server.
  2. 2Name the techniqueT1053.005 Scheduled Task.
  3. 3Set the scopeOur Windows Server fleet, created outside normal change-management windows.
  4. 4Say why it was missedOur existing rule only flags task creation with known-malicious names.
  5. 5Make it testablePull every Event ID 4698 (task created) on the servers; compare each to normal per host.
Each step removes ambiguity. By step 5 the hypothesis already dictates the query, which is the sign it is a good one.
HACKTHESOCLesson 34Threat hunting21 / 90

Worked example: a hypothesis filled in before any query

  • Names a technique, a scope, and why detection missed it
  • 4698 is the Windows event for a scheduled task being created
A good hypothesis is falsifiable and answerable in hours, not weeks. The written hypothesis is what a hunt lead reviews before approving the time, and it is attached to the final report either way.
HACKTHESOCLesson 34Threat hunting22 / 90

Good hypotheses are falsifiable and sized for hours

Good

A query can prove or disprove it within a reasonable investigation window.

  • Returns evidence if true, nothing if false
  • Scoped to hosts, users or a segment
  • Answerable in hours, not weeks
  • Says why detection might miss it

Bad

'An adversary might be doing something suspicious somewhere' or 'check every process for maliciousness'.

  • Too vague to query
  • So broad it cannot be falsified
  • No scope, no reason it evaded
Reject and rewrite anything in the right column before a single query is written.
HACKTHESOCLesson 34Threat hunting23 / 90

Why the hypothesis is written down before any query

  • It forces disciplineA structured investigation instead of an unfocused fishing expedition through the logs.
  • It gets the time approvedThe written hypothesis is what the hunt lead reviews before approving the investment.
  • It travels with the resultIt is attached to the final hunt report, whatever the outcome.
Writing first is the line between a hunt and a browsing session. Browsing does not scale to modern telemetry volumes.

Before the query: data readiness and the backlog

A hypothesis you cannot test is not a hunt yet. Check the data first, or a clean result means nothing.

HACKTHESOCLesson 34Threat hunting25 / 90

Readiness checks for common hypotheses

Hypothesis is aboutData that must existWhat to verify first
Command-line abuse4688 with command line enabled, Sysmon Event ID 1, or EDR process eventsThe command-line field is populated, not blank
PowerShell contentScript Block Logging, Event 4104The policy is on fleet-wide, not just admin servers
LSASS accessSysmon Event ID 10 with lsass.exe in the config, or EDR process accessWhich hosts run a config that logs it
KerberoastingEvent 4769 from every domain controllerAudit Kerberos Service Ticket Operations on all DCs
BeaconingPer-connection proxy, firewall or EDR network recordsRecords are not summarized per session or hour
Rare domainsDNS query logs: resolver, Sysmon Event ID 22, or EDR DNSWhich resolvers or segments are missing

Measure presence, % of in-scope hosts reporting, and retention. "Tested on 98% of endpoints for 30 days" is a coverage statement.

"Nothing found" is not a coverage statement. Write all three readiness measurements into the hunt record before you run the hunt.
HACKTHESOCLesson 34Threat hunting26 / 90

Three measurements before every hunt

  1. 1Is the data present at all?The source the hypothesis depends on is actually in the SIEM, e.g. 4769 from domain controllers.
  2. 2What share of hosts report it?The percentage of in-scope hosts that actually send it, not just the ones you happened to check.
  3. 3Does retention cover the window?A 30-day hunt needs 30 days of data for every host in scope.
  4. 4Write them into the hunt recordThen a clean result can become a statement like "tested on 98% of endpoints for 30 days".
Without these three numbers, "nothing found" could just mean "nothing collected".
HACKTHESOCLesson 34Threat hunting27 / 90

Readiness check: do all DCs send 4769, and since when?

  • Compare the Computer list with your DC inventory
  • A missing DC, or one whose First is three days ago, voids a clean result for that DC
Record the gap in the hunt record before running the Kerberoasting hunt itself.

Baseline and rarity: the hunter's best tool

Do not ask whether a thing is bad. Ask whether it is common.

HACKTHESOCLesson 34Threat hunting32 / 90

Rarity analysis: ask whether it is common, not whether it is bad

  • The observationCamouflaged or not, an adversary's activity almost always stands out as statistically unusual against everything around it.
  • One host of 5,000A process on one machine is more interesting than one on all 5,000, before you know anything about what it does.
  • Why it beats signaturesIt needs no prior knowledge of the malware, so it surfaces intrusions no signature-based detection would catch.
  • LFOLeast frequency of occurrence: normal activity repeats by definition; a one-off attacker action does not.
Adversaries, by the nature of hiding, are rarely common. That single idea, applied rigorously, is the hunter's signature technique.
HACKTHESOCLesson 34Threat hunting33 / 90

How a stacking query works

  1. 1Pick the fieldWhatever the hypothesis calls for: parent-child pairs, command lines, task names, DNS queries.
  2. 2Count every distinct valueAcross every host in scope, plus how many distinct hosts show it.
  3. 3Sort ascendingThe rarest values rise to the top of the list.
  4. 4Read the long tailValues seen once or a handful of times are where anomalies and intrusions live.
  5. 5Check against the baselineExplain the rare-but-normal rows; what remains is worth a look.
A hunter given any field relevant to a hypothesis builds the frequency distribution and goes straight to the tail, not the head.
HACKTHESOCLesson 34Threat hunting34 / 90

Stacking parent-child process pairs in SPL

  • One row per parent-child pair, counted across the whole fleet
  • dc(host) shows whether a pair lives on one host or thousands
  • sort ascending puts the long tail at the top; head 25 reads only that
Hypothesis behind it: an adversary uses an uncommon parent-child pair to blend in, on one host, undetected by existing rules.
HACKTHESOCLesson 34Threat hunting35 / 90

Stacking surfaces the one line that matters

Count each distinct value of a field across every host, sort ascending, read the top.

Thousands of hosts

Head of the stack

svchost.exe with parent services.exe. Completely standard Windows behavior.

1 host of 5,000

Long tail

rundll32.exe with parent winword.exe. Jumps to the top of a rarity stack immediately.

Attackers cannot make their technique on your host look identical to organic behavior across the whole fleet.

LFO, least frequency of occurrence: legitimate activity repeats by definition, while a one-off attacker action does not. Go straight to the tail, not the head.
HACKTHESOCLesson 34Threat hunting36 / 90

The same stack on DNS: rare, freshly registered domains

domain_age is an enrichment lookup the team maintains (fed from WHOIS or a newly-registered-domain feed), not a built-in field.

The second query finds a domain queried by exactly one host, three times or fewer, registered under 30 days ago: the profile of freshly stood-up C2 that no reputation list caught yet.

Rarity is only meaningful against a baseline. Pull weeks, not hours.

Weeks smooth over routine but infrequent activity like monthly patch cycles. A dev shop has far more unique process names than a locked-down call center.

A hunter who treats every single-occurrence value as suspicious drowns in false positives from an environment more heterogeneous than they assumed.
HACKTHESOCLesson 34Threat hunting39 / 90

Rare is where to look, not proof of malice

Three rare rows from a 5,000-host stack, read against a 30-day baseline.

1 host

Rare, explained

backup_agent.exe from services.exe on the file server that runs the nightly backup. The baseline expects it.

2 hosts

Rare, explained

mmc.exe from explorer.exe on the two IT admin jump servers. An admin console where admins work.

3 hosts

Rare, unexplained

rundll32.exe from winword.exe on finance laptops: a document launching a code-loading utility, with no business reason.

The rarest row is not the most suspicious one. Rarity plus an unexplained behavior is what earns attention; treating every single occurrence as bad is the false-positive trap.

Worked example: hypothesis to query to triage

A complete LSASS credential-dumping hunt, end to end, with real Sysmon fields.

HACKTHESOCLesson 34Threat hunting41 / 90
Hypothesis: an unusual process requested full memory access to lsass.exe, which our tool-name-based detection would miss.

Source: an intel report on an actor who dumps LSASS directly instead of using a named tool like Mimikatz.

The actor avoids named tools specifically to evade detections that only look for known tool filenames.
HACKTHESOCLesson 34Threat hunting42 / 90

Stack process access to LSASS by source and access level

  • Sysmon Event ID 10 (ProcessAccess) records one process opening a handle to another
  • 0x1FFFFF is a full-access handle; normal processes essentially never request it on LSASS
Scope to the technique, then stack by who asked and what they asked for: the same rarity habit as the previous section.
HACKTHESOCLesson 34Threat hunting43 / 90

Reading the one row the LSASS hunt returned

FieldWhat it recordsIn this hit
SourceImageThe process that opened the handlesvchost.exe in the Windows Temp directory
TargetImageThe process being openedlsass.exe, the process credential dumping reads
GrantedAccessThe access rights requested0x1FFFFF: full access, essentially never asked of LSASS normally
occurrence_countTimes this source and access pair appeared fleet-wide in the window1, on a single host

occurrence_count is fleet-wide rarity, not that host's process history.

Students often read a count of 1 as "first time this ran on the host". It is how often the pair appeared across the whole query window and fleet.
HACKTHESOCLesson 34Threat hunting44 / 90

The name is trusted; the location is not

Real svchost.exe

Always runs from C:\Windows\System32. A protected system directory a normal user cannot write to.

This svchost.exe

Runs from the Windows Temp directory, which is user-writable. A file merely named svchost.exe, borrowing a trusted name.

Access level plus location: two independent red flags on one row.

Neither "it ran once" nor "no rule fired" separates this from a false positive; every hunt result has no rule behind it. The path does.
HACKTHESOCLesson 34Threat hunting45 / 90

One row back, and what the hunter does with it

  1. 1The resultsvchost.exe in Windows Temp, GrantedAccess=0x1FFFFF, occurrence_count=1, one host.
  2. 2Red flag 1: access level0x1FFFFF against lsass.exe is a red flag on its own.
  3. 3Red flag 2: locationThe real svchost.exe always runs from System32. One in Temp is impersonating the name.
  4. 4Triage it like an alertPull the process tree, find what dropped the file, check for outbound connections.
  5. 5Close the loopEscalate to IR and add a rule: non-System32/SysWOW64 process requesting 0x1FFFFF on LSASS.
If the process tree shows a phishing payload dropped the file minutes earlier, the hunt just found a live credential-theft attempt no existing rule caught.

A hunter's query kit: endpoint, network, identity

The same least-frequency idea covers the other places adversaries hide. Each query is a starting point to adapt.

Start with persistence: a plausible task name is easy, so read how many hosts have it and what created it.
HACKTHESOCLesson 34Threat hunting47 / 90

Endpoint: stack scheduled task names across the fleet

  • T1053.005 persistence hides behind plausible names; read tasks on only 1 or 2 hosts
  • Splunk equivalent: stack EventCode=4698 by Task_Name with dc(host)
  • schtasks.exe under an Office or script-host parent beats your deployment agent
The same rarity habit applies to parent and child pairs in KQL: summarize DeviceProcessEvents by InitiatingProcessFileName and FileName, keep pairs on two hosts or fewer.
HACKTHESOCLesson 34Threat hunting48 / 90

Read who created a rare task, not just its name

Look first

A task created by schtasks.exe under an Office or script-host parent, on one or two hosts only.

Usually explained

A task created by your software-deployment agent. Rare on a given day, but part of the baseline.

The Creators column of the query is what makes this distinction possible.

Persistence through scheduled tasks hides behind plausible names, so the name alone rarely decides it. The creating process does.
HACKTHESOCLesson 34Threat hunting49 / 90

Endpoint: rare parent and child pairs, in KQL

  • The SPL stack from section 5, rewritten for Defender advanced hunting
  • Keeps pairs seen on two hosts or fewer, five runs or fewer
  • A starting point to adapt, not a finished detection
Same least-frequency idea, different query language. Students should recognize the shape: summarize by the field, filter to the tail, sort ascending.
HACKTHESOCLesson 34Threat hunting50 / 90

How the beaconing query measures regularity

  1. 1Keep the right connectionsSuccessful connections to public IPs over the last day.
  2. 2Line them upSort by device, then destination IP, then time.
  3. 3Measure each gapDelta: seconds since the previous connection, same device and IP.
  4. 4Summarize each pairConnection count, average gap, and the spread of gaps (stdev).
  5. 5Keep timer-like pairs50 or more connections with a spread under 20% of the average gap.
Human browsing is irregular. An implant calling home on a timer stays regular even with jitter, so a low spread relative to the average gap is the tell.
HACKTHESOCLesson 34Threat hunting51 / 90

Network: beaconing shows up as regular gaps

  • Implants call home on a timer; even with jitter the gaps are regular (T1071)
  • Many connections with a low spread is the shape to read
Expect update agents, telemetry clients and monitoring tools at the top. Baseline them out by process and destination; what remains is the interesting list.
HACKTHESOCLesson 34Threat hunting52 / 90

Regular traffic is not always an implant

Expected at the top

Update agents, telemetry clients and monitoring tools also call home on a timer. Baseline them out by process and destination.

What remains

A process talking to a public IP on a steady timer with no baseline explanation: the shape of command and control (T1071).

The first run of this query is mostly legitimate software. That is expected; the value comes after the baseline is subtracted.
HACKTHESOCLesson 34Threat hunting53 / 90

Identity: RC4 service-ticket bursts across many SPNs

  • 0x17 is RC4; many distinct service accounts in an hour is the T1558.003 shape
  • Legacy apps that still need RC4 appear too: know them from your baseline
Against that baseline, a user account requesting ten service tickets in one hour stands out.

The Pyramid of Pain

Why what you hunt for matters as much as how. A framework by David Bianco.

HACKTHESOCLesson 34Threat hunting56 / 90

Six layers, from free to change to painful to lose

The pain you inflict by detecting an indicator type roughly determines how much it costs the attacker when you catch it.
HACKTHESOCLesson 34Threat hunting57 / 90

What each layer costs the attacker to evade

LevelExampleAttacker cost to evade
TTPs (apex)SYSTEM-run scheduled task persistence, then AD discovery via native commandsSevere: retrain, rewrite playbook, relearn tradecraft
ToolsCobalt Strike, Mimikatz, a specific malware familyHigh: rebuild or replace the tool
Network/host artifactsMutex name, registry key, file path, User-Agent stringModerate: modify the tooling itself
Domain namesevil-c2-domain.exampleLow: register a new domain in minutes
IP addresses203.0.113.44Trivial: rotate infrastructure in minutes
Hash values (base)A SHA-256 of one fileZero: recompile and the hash changes
Detecting a TTP forces the attacker to retrain operators, rewrite playbooks, and abandon muscle memory built over months or years of campaigns.
HACKTHESOCLesson 34Threat hunting58 / 90
Detecting a TTP does not make the attacker recompile. It makes them change how they operate.

Retrain operators, rewrite playbooks, abandon muscle memory built over months or years of campaigns. That is far more expensive than any new hash, IP or domain.

This is why good hunts aim as high on the pyramid as the hypothesis allows: the behavior is what makes the adversary effective.
HACKTHESOCLesson 34Threat hunting59 / 90

Design hunts around behavior, not static indicators

Expires fast

'Processes matching this specific hash' or 'has anyone connected to IP 203.0.113.44'. Defeated by a recompile or an infrastructure rotation.

Survives campaigns

'A process injects into a remote process, then opens an outbound connection within thirty seconds'. Survives rebuilds and infrastructure changes.

Hash and IP hunting is fast, cheap, and useful for sweeping known intel. A program that only works there plays a game the adversary wins on cost.

The programs that meaningfully degrade an adversary consistently push their hunts toward the TTP layer.

Section 9 of 12

Closing the loop: hunts into permanent defense

A hunt is finished when its outcome persists after you move on.

HACKTHESOCLesson 34Threat hunting61 / 90

Every hunt ends in one of three documented outcomes

Confirmed finding

Escalate into the standard IR workflow: triage, validate, scope, contain. And convert the query into a standing detection rule.

Clean, data sufficient

Document as proven coverage for this technique, scope and time window. A genuine success, not 'nothing happened'.

Clean, data insufficient

Document a visibility gap: missing log source, field or retention. Arguably the most actionable outcome.

Today's hunt hypothesis becomes tomorrow's detection content, freeing hunters for the next gap instead of re-running the same manual search.

The Hunting Maturity Model

How capable is the program as a whole? Bianco's HMM, first published in 2015.

HACKTHESOCLesson 34Threat hunting70 / 90

The HMM scores a program on three inputs

David Bianco, author of the Pyramid of Pain, published the Hunting Maturity Model in 2015.

  • Data collectionHow much relevant data the organization routinely collects.
  • Data accessibilityHow easily and quickly that data can be searched.
  • Analyst skillWhether analysts can follow published hunts, or create original ones.

The combination reduces to one level, HM0 to HM4.

The HMM answers the question every hunt lead gets from leadership: how mature is the hunting program as a whole, not one hunt.
HACKTHESOCLesson 34Threat hunting71 / 90

LevelWhat it looks likeHunting?
HM0 InitialRelies on automated alerting (IDS, SIEM rules, AV); effort goes to resolving alertsNo
HM1 MinimalStill alert-driven, but routine collection of data beyond what alerting needsBarely
HM2 ProceduralCan follow hunt procedures published by others; little original workYes
HM3 InnovativeAnalysts create new procedures tailored to their own environmentYes

HM2 is where most organizations that have 'started hunting' actually sit. HM4 is the closing-the-loop discipline formalized as the top rung.

Three starting points, three strengths, three blind spots.

HACKTHESOCLesson 34Threat hunting76 / 90

Each model starts from a different place

From a TTP

Structured

A hypothesis about a specific technique from ATT&CK or intel, tested forward. Can catch a technique never seen here before.

From a trigger

Unstructured

An IOC from a feed, an alert that did not fully resolve, an odd artifact. Dig backward and forward in time around it.

From an entity

Situational

A newly disclosed vulnerability in software you run, a departing-employee concern, or the crown jewels.

Unstructured hunting is reactive in its start but proactive in its follow-through. It cannot find an adversary who left no trigger at all.

Crown jewel analysis, step by step

  1. 1List the crown jewelsThe 5 to 10 highest-value assets: customer PII database, source-code repo, domain controllers, CFO mailbox.
  2. 2Picture access to eachWhat would an adversary's access to this asset look like in logs we already collect?
  3. 3Hunt those paths firstIndependent of any known TTP.
The reasoning: a sophisticated adversary's path must eventually touch a high-value entity, however well they evade detection elsewhere.

Why a mature program runs all three each quarter

  • Only unstructuredReally just triage with extra steps: it never starts without a signal.
  • Only structuredMay prove coverage for techniques that were never relevant to your crown jewels.
  • Only situationalMisses an intrusion that never approaches a crown-jewel asset, even a damaging one.

None of the three is strictly superior; each catches a profile of behavior the others cannot.

Detection as code: YARA and Sigma

Two open, vendor-neutral formats for turning a hunt into a portable rule.

HACKTHESOCLesson 34Threat hunting80 / 90

Pick the format by what the finding is about

Victor Alvarez, 2013

YARA

Strings or byte patterns plus a boolean condition. For the content of a file or memory region: a dropped binary, a malware family's fingerprint.

Introduced 2017

Sigma

Human-readable YAML for a sequence of logged events, translated into SPL, KQL, Elastic DSL. SigmaHQ holds thousands of ATT&CK-mapped rules.

Every confirmed finding should leave behind a YARA rule, a Sigma rule, or both.

YARA rules run against file shares, memory dumps, or inside many EDR platforms, and can sweep the fleet for the same dropped artifact in seconds.

Key takeaways

  1. 01Triage is reactive, hunting is proactive; a mature SOC needs both, and successful hunts feed new rules into triage.
  2. 02A real hunt starts with a written, falsifiable hypothesis from ATT&CK, threat intel, or environmental knowledge.
  3. 03Rarity analysis, stacking a field and reading the long tail against a baseline, is the hunter's most powerful tool.
  4. 04Hashes and IPs are nearly free to rotate; TTPs force a tradecraft rebuild, so aim hunts high on the pyramid.
  5. 05Every hunt closes into a finding plus a rule, proven coverage, or a visibility gap; a clean result can be a success.
  6. 06Check data readiness first, run hunts from a prioritised backlog (PEAK, TaHiTI), and end each hunt record in a coverage statement.
