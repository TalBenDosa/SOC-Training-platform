A non-privileged domain user can walk away with a powerful service account's password.

The hard part happens entirely offline, where no lockout and no alert can stop it. That is Kerberoasting, MITRE ATT&CK T1558.003.

Open by stressing the asymmetry: no admin rights needed, and the dangerous step is invisible to your defences.

Section 1 of 8

The one fact Kerberoasting turns on

Assumes Kerberos basics; this is the single detail the attack weaponises.

HACKTHESOCLesson 36Kerberoasting6 / 38

The theme-park ticket booth analogy

  1. 1Kerberos and the KDCThe domain controller (KDC) is the ticket booth that proves who you are in Active Directory.
  2. 2TGT at the gateLog in once with your password, get a wristband: the Ticket-Granting Ticket.
  3. 3TGS per rideTrade the TGT for a per-service ticket (TGS) for one specific service.
  4. 4A new TGS each timeDatabase, file share, web app: each needs its own fresh service ticket.
The wristband proves you are verified; the paper ride-ticket is good for just one ride. Every different service means trading the TGT for a new TGS.
HACKTHESOCLesson 36Kerberoasting7 / 38

SPN, service account, and the one fact

  • SPNService Principal Name, e.g. MSSQLSvc/sqlprod01.example.com:1433. You ask the KDC for a ticket by SPN.
  • Service accountA dedicated domain account a running service logs in as, often with elevated permissions.
  • The one factThe KDC encrypts the TGS with a key derived from the service account's password, to prove the ticket is genuine.
  • The consequenceWhoever holds that ticket holds something encrypted with the password, offline and away from any alert.
The KDC looks up which account runs the SPN you ask for. The next section shows exactly how that encrypted ticket becomes a cracked credential.

Section 2 of 8

What Kerberoasting is and why it works

Abusing a by-design Kerberos feature, not a bug.

HACKTHESOCLesson 36Kerberoasting9 / 38
The service ticket is encrypted with the service account's own password hash.

This is how Kerberos proves the ticket is genuine. But the requester now holds a blob encrypted with that password, and can take it away to crack.

Stress: this is not a bug. It is exactly how Kerberos works, which is why the attack hides so well.
HACKTHESOCLesson 36Kerberoasting10 / 38

Two facts that are dangerous together

Any user can request it

Any authenticated domain user can request a TGS for any SPN. No admin, no special permission. One low-privilege or phished account is enough.

Crackable offline

The ticket is encrypted with the account's password hash, so the attacker guesses the password on their own hardware, never touching the DC again.

The target, a service account, is often over-privileged with a password set once years ago and never changed.

A routine Kerberos request becomes a password-cracking opportunity against exactly the kind of account worth stealing.

Section 3 of 8

The attack, step by step

Short mechanics, which is part of why it is so popular.

HACKTHESOCLesson 36Kerberoasting12 / 38

From one ordinary account to a real password

  1. 1Enumerate SPNsQuery AD over LDAP for all accounts with an SPN. A normal read any user can perform.
  2. 2Request a TGSOne Kerberos request returns the ticket, encrypted with the target's password hash.
  3. 3Extract the ticketPull the encrypted ticket from memory and save it to a file.
  4. 4Crack it offlineFeed it to hashcat or John with huge wordlists. A weak password falls, revealing the real cleartext.
The result is not a replayable hash but the actual cleartext password, used to log in directly as the service account wherever it has access.
HACKTHESOCLesson 36Kerberoasting13 / 38
The only parts that touch your network look almost exactly like legitimate Kerberos activity.

Because they are legitimate Kerberos requests. Everything after extraction happens offline, invisible to your defences.

This is why detection depends on subtle patterns, not an obviously malicious action.

Section 4 of 8

Worked example: enumeration to cracked password

The exact log fields an analyst sees at each step of the attack.

HACKTHESOCLesson 36Kerberoasting15 / 38

Steps 1 and 2 in telemetry: enumerate, then request

  • Enumerate SPNsAn LDAP query for every account with an SPN. A normal directory read any user may run, so it rarely stands out alone.
  • Request the TGSOne Kerberos service-ticket request, encrypted with the target's password hash. On the DC it lands as Event 4769, shown next.
  • The stable targetService passwords set years ago and never changed are what the attacker counts on. Watch accounts whose PasswordLastSet is ancient.
A service account whose password was last set in 2019 and never rotated is exactly the never-changed target the attacker relies on. The next slide shows the 4769 that the request step generates.
HACKTHESOCLesson 36Kerberoasting16 / 38

The Event 4769 the request generates on the DC

  • 0x17 is RC4, requested on purpose because it cracks far faster than AES
  • 4768 (TGT) logs once per logon; 4769 (TGS) logs once per service ticket
A burst of 4769s with no matching burst of 4768s is itself a tell: one session requesting many services in a row.
HACKTHESOCLesson 36Kerberoasting17 / 38
Step 3, cracking the ticket, happens entirely offline on the attacker's own hardware.

The DC sees nothing after the Step 2 request: no logon attempts, no lockouts, no alerts. A weak service-account password can fall in minutes to hours.

The artifacts an analyst actually has: 4769s for svc-sql, RC4 (0x17), and an ordinary workstation client address. None alone proves an attack, which is why the pattern matters more than any single line.

Section 5 of 8

Why it is so effective and so stealthy

Low cost, high reward, and almost no footprint.

HACKTHESOCLesson 36Kerberoasting19 / 38

Four properties that put it at the top of the playbook

No privilege to start

Works from any domain account: a phished user, an intern's laptop, a low-privilege foothold. A favourite early move after initial access.

Huge reward

Service accounts are often over-privileged, sometimes Domain Admin. One cracked password can hand sweeping access.

Offline and untouchable

Lockout policy, rate limits and failed-logon alerts never apply: there are no logon attempts against you. The attacker can grind for days.

Password rarely changes

Service passwords are set once and left for years to avoid breaking the service. A large, stable target.

One technical wrinkle helps the attacker: the encryption type.

RC4-encrypted tickets crack dramatically faster than AES, so attackers request RC4 where they can, which is also one of the clearest signals of an attack.

Section 6 of 8

Detecting Kerberoasting in the logs

The network part is a normal request, so hunt the anomalous pattern.

HACKTHESOCLesson 36Kerberoasting21 / 38

The tells inside Event 4769 volume

  • RC4 (type 0x17)A service ticket using RC4 where AES is expected. One of the highest-value AD detections you can build.
  • Volume and fan-outOne account requesting tickets for many SPNs in a short window. A real user touches a handful, not dozens in a burst.
  • Unusual SPNsRequests for SPNs the account never normally uses, or a spike against sensitive service accounts.
  • Honeypot SPN accountA decoy account with an SPN, a tempting name, a strong password and no access. Any 4769 for it is almost certainly an attacker.
Event 4769 ('service ticket requested') is logged on the DCs and the raw volume is huge, so the skill is filtering. The honeypot gives a near-zero-false-positive alarm.
HACKTHESOCLesson 36Kerberoasting22 / 38
No single 4769 is malicious, so you hunt the pattern.

RC4 downgrade, one account fanning out to many SPNs, or a hit on a honeypot. 4769 volume reveals SPN enumeration; 4768 is just the one-time TGT at logon.

Mirror the rest of the curriculum: it is specifically 4769 volume, not 4768, that reveals enumeration.
HACKTHESOCLesson 36Kerberoasting23 / 38

The 4769 fields a good detection reads together

  • TicketEncryptionType0x17 (RC4) is the headline signal; 0x12/0x11 are AES.
  • TicketOptions0x40810000 is normal; baseline it so an odd options mask stands out.
  • ServiceName / ServiceSidWhich account was targeted. Exclude krbtgt and $ computer accounts: constant legit 4769s, not roastable.
  • TargetUserName, IpAddressThe who and where. A workstation requesting many ServiceNames in seconds is the fan-out.
  • Status / FailureCode0x0 is a successful issue.
A raw 4769 carries more than the encryption type. Reading several fields together is what separates a tuned alert from a noisy one.
HACKTHESOCLesson 36Kerberoasting24 / 38

A tuned 4769 hunt: keep RC4, drop noise, count fan-out

  • Excludes the two biggest false-positive sources: machine accounts and krbtgt
  • Drop the DistinctSpns floor, keep the RC4 filter, to catch low-and-slow targeted roasts
The same idea in Splunk SPL stacks dc(Service_Name) by Account_Name and Client_Address, filtering Ticket_Encryption_Type=0x17 and excluding $ and krbtgt.
HACKTHESOCLesson 36Kerberoasting25 / 38

Managed detection, and proving AES actually took effect

Defender for Identity

Ships a built-in alert, 'Suspected Kerberos SPN exposure (Kerberoasting)', watching the enumerate-then-request pattern. Treat it as corroborating your own 4769 rule.

Verify, do not assume

Encryption type is per account: AES only when msDS-SupportedEncryptionTypes includes the AES bits (0x18 = both). A blank value stays RC4-crackable despite any GPO.

Audit with Get-ADUser svc-sql -Properties msDS-SupportedEncryptionTypes; the live 4769 showing 0x12 is the proof, not the GPO.

Setting the domain GPO is not proof the per-account attribute took effect. Confirm the fix in telemetry: the next 4769 for that service should show 0x12, not 0x17.

Section 7 of 8

Defending against Kerberoasting

Make the password impossible to crack, or remove the reward if it is.

HACKTHESOCLesson 36Kerberoasting27 / 38

The whole attack and where defence bites

Walk the three numbered steps on the figure, then point at the two defence ideas: worthless to crack, and worthless if cracked.
HACKTHESOCLesson 36Kerberoasting28 / 38

Two families of control, both needed

Make it uncrackable

Offline cracking only wins against guessable passwords.

  • 25+ char random passwords (the single most important control)
  • gMSA: AD-managed, auto-rotated every 30 days
  • Enforce AES over RC4 domain-wide

Remove the reward

If the password is cracked anyway, keep the blast radius small.

  • Least privilege: never Domain Admin 'to be safe'
  • Audit and reduce SPNs on accounts that no longer need them

You cannot stop a user requesting a ticket; that is Kerberos by design.

A gMSA password is both uncrackable and constantly changing, the modern best-practice answer. Over-privileged service accounts turn a roast into a domain compromise.
HACKTHESOCLesson 36Kerberoasting29 / 38

Responding the moment a roast alert fires

  1. 1Rotate the account nowAssume the ticket is already cracking. Reset to a long random value, or convert to a gMSA: any captured ticket becomes worthless.
  2. 2Review logons since the requestPull 4624/4672 and 4769 for the account across all hosts: was the password cracked and already reused, and from where?
  3. 3Scope and containIf the account logged on anywhere unexpected, isolate those hosts and widen the investigation.
  4. 4Confirm the fix landedThe next 4769 for that SPN should show AES (0x12), not RC4 (0x17).
Treat a fired alert as a race: you cannot stop a user requesting a ticket, so you make the ticket worthless to crack and worthless if cracked, and rotate the targeted account immediately.

Section 8 of 8

Encryption types, hashcat modes, targeted vs mass

Two attacker choices that change crack time and visibility.

HACKTHESOCLesson 36Kerberoasting31 / 38

Encryption type sets crack speed, not just the algorithm

Etype (code)Kerberos nameHashcat modeRelative crack speed
23 (0x17)RC4-HMAC13100Fastest; key derived from the NT hash
17 (0x11)AES128-CTS-HMAC-SHA1-9619600~1000x+ slower: PBKDF2 with 4,096 iterations
18 (0x12)AES256-CTS-HMAC-SHA1-9619700Slower still: ~half AES128, so ~1000x+ over RC4
RC4 is flagged not because it is 'the attack' (legacy apps negotiate it too) but because it is far cheaper to brute-force. Disabling RC4 forces every roast onto slower AES. AES is not immune, just slower.
HACKTHESOCLesson 36Kerberoasting32 / 38

Mass roasting is loud; targeted roasting is quiet

Mass roast

Requests a ticket for every SPN at once. Produces dozens of Event 4769s from one client in seconds: a clear fan-out that a volume rule catches easily.

Targeted roast

Requests one high-value SPN, pre-selected via recon. A single 4769 blended into normal traffic trips no volume rule and leaves a minimal footprint.

Cracking an AES256 ticket (etype 18) is far slower than RC4, but a weak password still falls.

Targeted (low-and-slow) roasting hits one SPN pre-selected via BloodHound recon, so it slips under a fan-out threshold entirely.
HACKTHESOCLesson 36Kerberoasting33 / 38
A mature detection stack needs all three: volume, encryption type, and a canary account.

A volume rule catches mass roasting but is blind to a single well-aimed request, which is why RC4 filtering and the honeypot SPN matter as much as fan-out counting.

A determined attacker simply avoids whichever single signal you rely on alone.
HACKTHESOCLesson 36Kerberoasting34 / 38

Key takeaways

  1. 01Kerberoasting (T1558.003) abuses Kerberos by design: any domain user requests a TGS encrypted with a service account's password hash.
  2. 02No admin needed, and the cracking is offline, so lockouts, rate limits and failed-logon alerts never apply.
  3. 03Service accounts are often over-privileged with weak, never-changed passwords; attackers downgrade to RC4 to crack faster.
  4. 04Detect via Event 4769 with RC4 (0x17), one account fanning out to many SPNs, or a hit on a honeypot SPN account.
  5. 05Defend by making the password uncrackable (long/random, gMSA, AES) and worthless if cracked (least privilege).
