What a Security Operations Center actually is

People, process and technology that detect and respond to threats, around the clock.

HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is6 / 48

The core SOC loop

Everything you learn later fits inside these four steps.

  1. 1Systems emit logsLaptop, server, firewall, cloud and email each write a record of what they do.
  2. 2The SIEM collects themMillions of logs a day, with detection rules running across them.
  3. 3A rule matchesAn alert lands in the analyst queue, e.g. 5+ failed logins then a success.
  4. 4A human decidesThe analyst investigates and reaches a verdict.
Stress: an alert is a question the machine is asking you. Your job is to answer it, not just close it.
HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is7 / 48

The three ingredients of every SOC

People

Analysts and managers who make the judgment calls no machine can.

Process

Agreed steps for handling, escalating and communicating, so the response does not depend on who is on shift.

Technology

SIEM, EDR on every endpoint, ticketing, threat-intelligence feeds.

Great tools without process is chaos. Great process without skilled people is slow, shallow investigation.

You are the most valuable ingredient: you supply the one thing that cannot be automated, the ability to ask why.

A day on shift

The queue, triage, and the three-way verdict.

HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is13 / 48

The queue as a Tier 1 analyst sees it

  • Work by priority: highest severity first
  • Within a severity, oldest first so nothing breaches SLA
  • Triage is a fast first read, not a full investigation
Most of what a SIEM produces is noise. The art is closing noise fast and correctly while catching the one alert in fifty that is real.
HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is14 / 48

Every triaged alert ends in one verdict

Benign

Legitimate activity that tripped a rule. The user mistyped, then got it right. Close as false positive and note why.

Suspicious

Can't prove bad, can't prove fine. Unusual time, place or process. Investigate further or get a second pair of eyes.

Malicious

Evidence of genuine attacker activity: malware, intrusion, data leaving. It becomes an incident and is escalated now.

Suspicious is a professional verdict, not indecision, as long as you say what would resolve it.

Beginners force everything into benign or malicious. Push back on that hard.

Inside a real shift

The screens you work in, what the queue columns tell you, how you close, and the handover note.

HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is17 / 48

Four screens Tier 1 lives in

Whatever the vendor names, most SOCs give Tier 1 the same four windows.

SIEM / XDR queue

Where alerts arrive, grouped into incidents. Sentinel and Defender XDR call it Incidents; Splunk ES calls it Incident Review.

EDR console

Defender for Endpoint, CrowdStrike Falcon, SentinelOne. Open the host, read the process tree, isolate if the playbook allows.

Ticketing / case

ServiceNow, Jira Service Management, TheHive, or the SIEM's own incident page. Holds the record, runs the SLA timers.

Chat / war room

Teams or Slack. Page Tier 2, ask whether a change was planned, and run a major incident's bridge.

The tool names change from SOC to SOC; the four jobs do not. Learn the job each screen does and any vendor's console becomes readable.

Every queue column answers a triage question

ColumnThe question it answers
SeverityHow bad is this if it is real?
Created / last activityHow close am I to breaching the SLA?
Title and detection sourceWhich rule or product raised it (SIEM analytic, EDR, identity protection, email)?
EntitiesWhich user, host, IP, mailbox, or file hash is involved?
Alert countOne alert, or a dozen correlated into one incident?
OwnerIs anyone already working it? Unassigned means nobody.
StatusWhere is it in its lifecycle?

Assign the item to yourself the moment you open it.

Two analysts silently working the same incident, or each assuming the other has it, is one of the most common shift failures. The regulars that fill the queue: user-reported phishing, failed-logon bursts and spray, EDR detections already quarantined, impossible travel, DLP alerts. Knowing them is how you spot the one that does not fit.
HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is19 / 48

Benign positive is not false positive

Detection engineers tune rules from the classification you close with. Sentinel offers four.

True Positive

Suspicious activity. A real threat, correctly detected.

Benign Positive

Suspicious but expected. An authorized pen test trips the brute-force rule: the rule correctly saw real brute forcing.

False Positive

Incorrect alert logic or incorrect data, e.g. a rule fires because a field was parsed wrongly.

Undetermined

You could not reach a conclusion.

Defender XDR: True positive, Informational/expected activity, False positive. Sentinel statuses: New, Active, Closed.

Close the pen test as a false positive and you have told the detection engineer to fix a rule that was working. Defender XDR statuses run Active, In progress, Resolved; ticketing systems usually add Pending and Escalated.

The questions a good analyst asks

The difference between catching attacks and clearing the queue.

HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is22 / 48

Five questions, every alert

  1. 1Normal for this entity?Compare against this user's or host's own baseline.
  2. 2What fired the rule?Open the raw log. Read the exact field and value that matched.
  3. 3Before and after?Widen the timeline 15 to 60 minutes. Find the sequence.
  4. 4Who else?Pivot on the IP, hash, user or domain. Is this isolated?
  5. 5Impact if real?Test box or domain controller? Impact drives urgency.
Most real detections come from a deviation from a person's own normal, not from the activity being exotic.

From alert to handoff

Documentation, escalation, and why the ticket is the product.

HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is25 / 48

What a good ticket contains

Write it so a colleague who never saw the alert can follow your logic end to end.

  • The alertWhat fired and when.
  • What you checkedThe specific logs, users, hosts and IPs.
  • What you foundRaw evidence: a log line, a command, a source IP, not an impression.
  • Verdict and reasoningBenign, suspicious or malicious, and why.
  • ActionClosed as FP, escalated to Tier 2, host isolated.
  • ATT&CK tagThe technique the activity matches, e.g. T1110 (Brute Force), so readers know the class of behaviour.
Avoid 'looks fine'. Say why it looks fine.
HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is26 / 48

A ticket that stands on its own

  • If you wrote nothing, none of this investigation exists tomorrow
Ask: would this ticket hold up if a manager, an auditor or a courtroom read it?

Section 7 of 12

Event, alert, incident

Three escalating stages, not synonyms.

HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is28 / 48

The SOC is a funnel

Billions a day

Event

Any observable occurrence (NIST SP 800-61). Neutral and unread. Nobody decides; it just happens.

Hundreds to thousands

Alert

A rule fired on an event. A claim that a human should look. Mostly benign.

A handful

Incident

Actual or potential harm to confidentiality, integrity or availability. Declared by a human.

You never respond to events. You triage alerts. You declare and manage incidents.

Treat every alert as an incident and you burn out. Treat an incident as just another alert and you miss the breach.

The four outcomes

True and false, positive and negative.

HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is30 / 48

Every detection lands in one of four boxes

HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is31 / 48
Reduce false positives without creating false negatives.

The fastest way to zero false positives is turning the rule off, which converts every future true positive into a miss.

This is why 'just make the alerts stop' is a dangerous request. Too many FPs cause alert fatigue; tuning is choosing where on the dial to sit.

Severity, priority, and the clock

Why two alerts with the same label are not worked in the same order.

HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is33 / 48

Severity is not priority

Severity

How bad is this if it is real? A fixed scale, Low to Critical, mostly a property of the alert.

Priority

How fast must I act? Severity weighted by the asset's urgency and exposure. Priority orders your queue.

A Critical on a dead test box can wait. A High on the domain controller is drop-everything.

Use the two-alerts-at-the-same-second example from the lesson.

The stack beyond the SIEM

  • SOARAutomates enrichment and response steps through playbooks.
  • EDR / XDRProcess-level endpoint visibility; XDR correlates endpoint, network, email, cloud.
  • TIPIngests known-bad IPs, domains, hashes and enriches alerts.

HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is41 / 48

Career path, certifications, and on-call reality

  • ProgressionOne to three years at Tier 1, then Tier 2, detection engineering, threat hunting, or incident response.
  • Entry certificationsCompTIA Security+ or hands-on BTL1; SC-200 or Splunk Core Certified User where the SOC runs those.
  • Later stepsCompTIA CySA+, GIAC GCIH (incident handling) and GCIA (intrusion analysis).
  • Shifts24/7 rotation: 12-hour days and nights on a 2-2-3 pattern, or three 8-hour shifts. Nights, weekends, holidays.
  • On-callTier 2 and 3 carry a pager: reachable and logged in within a set time when a Critical escalates.
Be realistic with students about the hours. The shift pattern is part of the job, not a temporary phase.

The NIST incident response lifecycle, in brief

What happens once an incident is declared. NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 3 (April 2025) maps it onto CSF 2.0.

HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is43 / 48

Four phases, designed as a cycle

  1. 1PreparationDetections, playbooks, logging, tabletop exercises.
  2. 2Detection & AnalysisTriage and confirm. Where your five questions live.
  3. 3Containment, Eradication & RecoveryStop it, remove the cause, restore and verify.
  4. 4Post-Incident ActivityLessons learned feed back into Preparation.
Label your recommended actions by phase: isolate = containment, reimage and rotate creds = eradication, verify and reconnect = recovery.
HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is44 / 48

Key takeaways

  1. 01A SOC is people, process and technology turning logs into alerts and alerts into decisions, 24/7.
  2. 02Tier 1 triages, Tier 2 investigates, Tier 3 responds and hunts; SLAs and MTTD/MTTR set the pace.
  3. 03Every alert ends benign, suspicious or malicious; close with an accurate classification: benign positive is not false positive.
  4. 04Run the five questions on every alert: normal, what fired, before/after, who else, impact.
  5. 05Event, alert and incident are escalating stages; the false negative is the outcome to fear.
  6. 06The ticket is the product: evidence plus reasoning makes a verdict defensible, and the handover note lets the next shift carry on.
