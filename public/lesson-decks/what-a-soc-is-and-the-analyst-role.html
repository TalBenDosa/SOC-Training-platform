What a Security Operations Center actually is

People, process and technology that detect and respond to threats, around the clock.

HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is6 / 84

The core SOC loop

Everything you learn later fits inside these four steps.

  1. 1Systems emit logsLaptop, server, firewall, cloud and email each write a record of what they do.
  2. 2The SIEM collects themMillions of logs a day, with detection rules running across them.
  3. 3A rule matchesAn alert lands in the analyst queue, e.g. 5+ failed logins then a success.
  4. 4A human decidesThe analyst investigates and reaches a verdict.
Stress: an alert is a question the machine is asking you. Your job is to answer it, not just close it.
HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is7 / 84

One alert, followed through the loop

The four steps as the SIEM actually experiences them.

  • The rule is a pattern over many logs, not a single event
  • The alert title is the rule's guess; the verdict is yours
Read the rule line aloud: it counts failures and a success from one IP inside a window. That is all a rule is, a pattern. Everything after the alert line is human work.
HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is8 / 84

Everything that happens leaves a log; rules hunt for patterns

The SOC never watches screens directly. Systems send a record of each action to the SIEM.

Pattern a rule looks forWhy it might mean trouble
One user logging in from two countries an hour apartNobody travels that fast: a second person may hold the password
A program the antivirus flaggedKnown-bad code is present on a machine
A burst of failed passwordsSomeone may be guessing their way into an account

A login is a log. A file opened is a log. A firewall allowing a connection is a log.

Millions of logs a day, a handful of patterns. Each row here is a later lesson; for now, the point is that alerts come from patterns across ordinary records.
HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is9 / 84

Why companies pay for someone to watch, 24/7

Attacks keep no office hours

Intrusions start at night, at weekends, on holidays. A desk staffed nine to five is empty when it matters.

Every wall leaks

A firewall blocks a lot and antivirus blocks more, but determined attackers get past both constantly. Something has to notice the ones that do.

The cost of not watching

Stolen customer records, ransomware that stops the business, regulatory fines. That is why even mid-sized companies build or rent a SOC.

Between a quiet break-in and a catastrophic breach stands one thing: a human who notices early and acts.

Ask the class which of the three they think costs most. The honest answer is the third, and the first two are why it happens.

The three ingredients of every SOC

Every SOC, three people or three hundred, runs on these. Remove one and the other two fail.

IngredientWhat it isWithout it

HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is11 / 84

People: the rule sees a pattern, the analyst sees a person

Same alert: 'login from a new country'. Watch what each side can and cannot know.

Pattern only

What the rule knows

Country changed from last login. That is the whole of its knowledge.

  • No idea the user is on holiday
  • No idea which device was used
  • No idea what the account can reach

Context

What the analyst asks

Is there a travel request? Does the device match? Did MFA pass? Is this a high-value account?

  • Context lives outside the log
  • History lives in the user's baseline

A verdict

The outcome

Routine for the travelling salesperson, account takeover for the clerk who never leaves the country. Only the human can tell them apart.

The tooling narrows it down; you decide. This is why Tier 1 is a judgment role from day one, not just clicking through alerts.
HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is12 / 84

Process: a phishing runbook gives every shift the same steps

Without it, three analysts handle one reported email three ways. With it, 3am gets the same rigour as noon.

  1. 1Read the headersWhere did the message really come from, and through which servers?
  2. 2Check sender reputationHas this domain or address been seen before, and was it bad?
  3. 3Detonate the URLOpen the link safely in a sandbox and see where it leads.
  4. 4Run a blast-radius queryWho else received the same email, and did anyone click?
  5. 5Close or escalate on the SLAThe Service Level Agreement sets how fast each severity is picked up.

A runbook is the SOC's muscle memory: it turns one expert's method into the whole team's baseline.

Skipped steps and lost evidence are what process prevents. SLA is defined properly later; here just plant that process puts a clock on the work.

Technology: what each tool sees, and where it is blind

Tools give visibility and memory, not decisions. Know each one's blind spot before you lean on it.

ToolWhat it seesWhat it misses
SIEMLogs from every connected source, correlated into one timelineAnything not sent to it, and any pattern no rule describes
EDR (endpoint agent)Process launches, file writes, network calls on each host; can isolate itMachines without the agent installed
TicketingThe case's memory: what was seen, decided and doneAnything the analyst never wrote down
Threat intelligenceKnown-bad IPs, hashes and domains others have reportedBrand-new indicators nobody has reported yet

SIEM = the timeline; EDR = depth on one host; ticketing = memory; threat intel = outside context.

Each tool is defined fully in later lessons. The habit to start now: when a tool says 'nothing found', ask whether it could have seen it at all.

Three tiers, three depths

TierWhat they doHospital analogy

HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is16 / 84

How an alert is born and where it goes

  1. 1A rule matches in the SIEMA log pattern fits a detection. The alert appears and a clock starts.
  2. 2Tier 1 triagesA fast first read: nothing, needs more digging, or must go up.
  3. 3Close or escalateNoise is closed with a reason. Anything real or too big is handed up.
  4. 4Tier 2 confirmsPulls more data sources and decides whether a real incident is underway.
  5. 5Tier 3 responds and huntsHandles serious incidents, hunts for what no rule caught, improves detections.
The lifecycle exists so nothing falls through the cracks. Every hand-off carries the ticket with it.

SLAs, the clock, and when to escalate

  • First-touch SLA = time until a human picks it up, not time to solve it

HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is19 / 84

Four signs it is time to escalate

Escalation is the formal hand-off to a higher tier. Escalate when ANY of these is true, and write down why.

  • The evidence says it is realYou have enough to believe this is a compromise, not a false alarm.
  • It is bigger than oneMore than one host, account or system is involved.
  • It is beyond your permissionsThe action needed, such as isolating a server, is not yours to take.
  • The playbook is exhaustedYou have run every step you were given and it is still unclear.
Good escalation is not failure; it is what the process asks you to do. Escalating a genuine incident fast beats sitting on it because you were unsure.
HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is20 / 84

Two beginner mistakes the tiers exist to prevent

Drowning alone

Trying to solve everything yourself. A real incident sits in your queue for an hour while you chase it past your depth and your permissions.

Escalating everything

Handing up every alert. Tier 2 stops reading your escalations, and the real one gets lost in the noise you created.

Working at the right depth

Close the obvious, dig where it is cheap, hand up what meets a trigger, with your notes attached.

Both mistakes come from uncertainty. The four triggers on the previous slide are the cure: they turn 'I'm not sure' into a checklist.

HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is24 / 84

Every triaged alert ends in one verdict

Benign

Legitimate activity that tripped a rule. The user mistyped, then got it right. Close as false positive and note why.

Suspicious

Can't prove bad, can't prove fine. Unusual time, place or process. Investigate further or get a second pair of eyes.

Malicious

Evidence of genuine attacker activity: malware, intrusion, data leaving. It becomes an incident and is escalated now.

Suspicious is a professional verdict, not indecision, as long as you say what would resolve it.

Beginners force everything into benign or malicious. Push back on that hard.
HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is25 / 84

What noise looks like, and why you still read it

Most of what a SIEM produces is noise. The art is closing it fast and correctly while catching the one in fifty that is real.

  • A scheduled scannerAn approved tool probing the network on its usual timetable trips scan rules.
  • Legitimate admin maintenanceAn administrator's normal work can look like an attacker's. Ask whether the change was planned.
  • A mistyped passwordA few failures followed by a success from the user's usual device.
  • Note why you closed itThe reason feeds rule tuning, and protects you if the same pattern later turns out to be real.
Noise you understand is fast to close. Noise you close without reading is how the clever attack disguised as noise gets through.

Inside a real shift

The screens you work in, what the queue columns tell you, how you close, and the handover note.

HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is28 / 84

Four screens Tier 1 lives in

Whatever the vendor names, most SOCs give Tier 1 the same four windows.

SIEM / XDR queue

Where alerts arrive, grouped into incidents. Sentinel and Defender XDR call it Incidents; Splunk ES calls it Incident Review.

EDR console

Defender for Endpoint, CrowdStrike Falcon, SentinelOne. Open the host, read the process tree, isolate if the playbook allows.

Ticketing / case

ServiceNow, Jira Service Management, TheHive, or the SIEM's own incident page. Holds the record, runs the SLA timers.

Chat / war room

Teams or Slack. Page Tier 2, ask whether a change was planned, and run a major incident's bridge.

The tool names change from SOC to SOC; the four jobs do not. Learn the job each screen does and any vendor's console becomes readable.

Every queue column answers a triage question

ColumnThe question it answers
SeverityHow bad is this if it is real?

Title and detection sourceWhich rule or product raised it (SIEM analytic, EDR, identity protection, email)?

Assign the item to yourself the moment you open it.

Two analysts silently working the same incident, or each assuming the other has it, is one of the most common shift failures. The regulars that fill the queue: user-reported phishing, failed-logon bursts and spray, EDR detections already quarantined, impossible travel, DLP alerts. Knowing them is how you spot the one that does not fit.
HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is30 / 84

The regulars that fill almost every Tier 1 queue

Learn what the regulars look like; that is how you notice the one that does not fit.

  • User-reported phishingFrom the report button in Outlook. Often the single largest category.
  • Failed logons and password sprayBursts against VPN gateways, Entra ID and Okta.
  • EDR detectionsMostly unwanted apps or malware the agent already quarantined, with the occasional live behavioural detection.
  • Impossible travel, risky sign-insFrom the identity provider; many explained by VPNs, proxies and mobile carriers.
  • DLP and policy alertsData loss prevention flags, e.g. a large upload to personal cloud storage.
An already-quarantined EDR detection and a live behavioural one look similar in the queue. Read which it is before you close.
HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is31 / 84

Benign positive is not false positive

Detection engineers tune rules from the classification you close with. Sentinel offers four.

True Positive

Suspicious activity. A real threat, correctly detected.

Benign Positive

Suspicious but expected. An authorized pen test trips the brute-force rule: the rule correctly saw real brute forcing.

False Positive

Incorrect alert logic or incorrect data, e.g. a rule fires because a field was parsed wrongly.

Undetermined

You could not reach a conclusion.

Defender XDR: True positive, Informational/expected activity, False positive. Sentinel statuses: New, Active, Closed.

Close the pen test as a false positive and you have told the detection engineer to fix a rule that was working. Defender XDR statuses run Active, In progress, Resolved; ticketing systems usually add Pending and Escalated.
HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is32 / 84

Statuses track progress; classifications teach the rules

ToolStatusesClosing classifications
Microsoft SentinelNew, Active, ClosedTrue Positive, Benign Positive, False Positive, Undetermined
Defender XDRActive, In progress, ResolvedTrue positive, Informational/expected activity, False positive
Ticketing systemsUsually add Pending and EscalatedFollow the SIEM's classification

Status says where the work is. Classification is what detection engineers tune from, so it matters more.

Pending means waiting on a user or another team. Defender's Informational/expected activity covers security tests, red-team work and expected behaviour from trusted apps.

The questions a good analyst asks

The difference between catching attacks and clearing the queue.

HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is36 / 84

Five questions, every alert

  1. 1Normal for this entity?Compare against this user's or host's own baseline.
  2. 2What fired the rule?Open the raw log. Read the exact field and value that matched.
  3. 3Before and after?Widen the timeline 15 to 60 minutes. Find the sequence.
  4. 4Who else?Pivot on the IP, hash, user or domain. Is this isolated?
  5. 5Impact if real?Test box or domain controller? Impact drives urgency.
Most real detections come from a deviation from a person's own normal, not from the activity being exotic.
HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is37 / 84

Q1, normal for this entity: same action, opposite verdicts

A baseline is what is normal for THIS user or host. Compare against it, not against the abstract.

Routine here

An administrator runs PowerShell at 2am as part of their job. A travelling sales rep logs in from Germany.

Alarming here

An accountant runs PowerShell at 2am. Someone who has never left the country logs in from Germany.

You answer Q1 by pulling that user's or host's history. Most real detections are a deviation from a person's own normal.

Nothing in either action is exotic. The verdict flips purely on whose baseline it breaks.
HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is38 / 84
Q2, what fired the rule: the title is a summary, the raw field is the evidence.

'Suspicious PowerShell' means nothing until you read the command line that actually ran. That tells you if the rule caught something real or misfired.

This is also how you learn: every alert you open and truly read makes the next one faster.
HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is39 / 84

Q3, before and after: one frame becomes a movie

An alert is a single frame. Widen the timeline 15 to 60 minutes and look for the sequence.

  1. 1A failed loginOn its own: nothing. Users mistype every day.
  2. 2Then a successSame account, shortly after. Maybe the user, maybe a guess that worked.
  3. 3Then a new admin accountCreated right after the success. Now it is an attack unfolding.

Attacks are sequences, e.g. phishing click, download, suspicious process, connection out.

Pivoting on time is how you turn one alert into the story around it.
HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is40 / 84

Q4 and Q5: who else, and what is at stake

QuestionHow you answer itWhat changes the answer
Who else? (scope)Pivot on the IP, hash, user or domain across all logsSame IP on other users, same hash on other laptops
Impact if real?Ask what system and data are at stakeTest box with no data vs domain controller or finance server

Scope is the question that most often turns a Medium alert into a Critical incident.

Run all five even when an alert looks obviously benign. The discipline of asking is what catches the attack disguised as noise.

From alert to handoff

Documentation, escalation, and why the ticket is the product.

HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is43 / 84
An investigation you did not write down never happened.

Next week the same alert starts from zero. If a breach surfaces later, there is no record of what was checked. The ticket is what survives.

Analysts are judged not by how many alerts they closed but by the quality of the record they left behind.
HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is44 / 84

What a good ticket contains

Write it so a colleague who never saw the alert can follow your logic end to end.

  • The alertWhat fired and when.
  • What you checkedThe specific logs, users, hosts and IPs.
  • What you foundRaw evidence: a log line, a command, a source IP, not an impression.
  • Verdict and reasoningBenign, suspicious or malicious, and why.
  • ActionClosed as FP, escalated to Tier 2, host isolated.
  • ATT&CK tagThe technique the activity matches, e.g. T1110 (Brute Force), so readers know the class of behaviour.
Avoid 'looks fine'. Say why it looks fine.
HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is45 / 84

Vague notes versus a ticket someone can act on

Weak

Impressions with no evidence. The reader must redo your work or message you.

  • "Looks fine, closed"
  • "This looks bad, help"
  • No log lines, no IPs, no reasoning

Strong

Evidence plus reasoning. A colleague who never saw the alert can follow it end to end.

  • 12 failed logins, then success at 09:40
  • Never signed in outside Israel before
  • Same IP hit two more users

Don't write 'looks fine'. Write WHY it looks fine.

Ask: would this ticket hold up if a manager, an auditor or a courtroom read it?
HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is46 / 84

A ticket that stands on its own

  • If you wrote nothing, none of this investigation exists tomorrow
Ask: would this ticket hold up if a manager, an auditor or a courtroom read it?
HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is47 / 84

An escalation Tier 2 can act on in minutes

Escalation must carry your documentation with it. A bare 'help' restarts the clock.

  1. 1Here is the alertWhat fired, when, at what severity.
  2. 2Here is what I checkedThe logs, users, hosts and IPs you examined.
  3. 3Here is why I think it is maliciousThe evidence and the reasoning that links it.
  4. 4Here is what I recommendE.g. kill the session, reset password and MFA, check the other two users.
Tier 2 should never have to redo your triage. If they do, the escalation cost the incident time.

Section 7 of 12

Event, alert, incident

Three escalating stages, not synonyms.

HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is49 / 84

The SOC is a funnel

Billions a day

Event

Any observable occurrence (NIST SP 800-61). Neutral and unread. Nobody decides; it just happens.

Hundreds to thousands

Alert

A rule fired on an event. A claim that a human should look. Mostly benign.

A handful

Incident

Actual or potential harm to confidentiality, integrity or availability. Declared by a human.

You never respond to events. You triage alerts. You declare and manage incidents.

Treat every alert as an incident and you burn out. Treat an incident as just another alert and you miss the breach.
HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is50 / 84

The three stages side by side

TermWhat it isDaily volumeWho decides
EventAny observable occurrence (NIST SP 800-61)BillionsNobody: it just happens
AlertA rule fired on an event or pattern of eventsHundreds to thousandsA detection rule
IncidentActual or potential harm to C, I or A, or a policy violationA fewA human analyst
An alert is not a fact, it is a claim. An incident is a human judgment, not a tool output. NIST Rev. 3 (April 2025) keeps these definitions unchanged.
HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is51 / 84

What confidentiality, integrity and availability mean

An incident is harm to one of these three. This is the CIA triad, the goal every control protects.

Confidentiality

Only authorized people can read the data. Broken when secrets leak: files stolen, a mailbox exfiltrated, a database dumped.

Integrity

Data and systems are not changed without authorization. Broken when records are tampered with, logs wiped, or files encrypted by ransomware.

Availability

Systems and data are there when the business needs them. Broken when a server is knocked offline by DDoS or locked up by ransomware.

Name which leg an incident threatens: it frames the impact and the urgency for everyone who reads your ticket.

The triad is the vocabulary for impact. A data leak is a confidentiality incident, tampering is integrity, an outage is availability; ransomware often hits integrity and availability at once.
HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is52 / 84

Watch one login climb the ladder

  1. 1Eventa.cohen signs in. One of billions of records today. Nobody looks.
  2. 2AlertThe sign-in came from a new country after 12 failures. A rule fires: a human should look.
  3. 3IncidentTriage confirms account takeover on a payroll account. Confidentiality is at stake: a human declares it.

You never respond to events. You triage alerts. You declare and manage incidents.

Treat every alert as an incident and you burn out. Treat an incident as just another alert and you miss the breach.

The four outcomes

True and false, positive and negative.

HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is54 / 84

Every detection lands in one of four boxes

HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is55 / 84

The four outcomes in words, not just the grid

Two yes/no axes: did an alert fire, and was it actually malicious? Their combinations give four cells.

OutcomeAlert fired?Actually malicious?What it means
True PositiveYesYesReal attack, alarm fired. The catch you want.
False PositiveYesNoAlarm with no real threat. Noise that drives fatigue.
True NegativeNoNoNormal activity, correctly silent. The invisible default.
False NegativeNoYesReal attack, no alarm at all. The miss you fear most.

You see True and False Positives in the queue. You almost never see a False Negative, which is exactly why it is dangerous.

This closes the gap the SVG leaves: a student who cannot see the matrix still learns all four cells and why the false negative is the one to fear.
HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is56 / 84

What the two errors look like on a real shift

Annoying

False positives

Nothing wrong, alert fired anyway. Too many cause alert fatigue: analysts reflexively close alerts as benign.

  • Backup job that looks like exfiltration
  • Admin script that looks like an attacker
  • VPN placing one user in two countries

Dangerous

False negatives

A real threat and no alert at all. You cannot investigate what never alerted.

  • Attacker moving through a blind spot
  • A detection that was never written
  • A rule tuned into silence
Alert fatigue is how false positives create false negatives in people: the reflex-close habit lets a real one slip past.
HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is57 / 84

Tuning is choosing where to sit on one dial

The two errors trade against each other. Every rule is set somewhere between them, deliberately.

  1. 1Tighten the ruleFewer false positives, but a slightly different real attack may slip through.
  2. 2Loosen the ruleCatches every variant, but floods the queue until nobody reads it.
  3. 3Choose per ruleDecide the trade-off on purpose for each detection, based on what a miss would cost.
This is the core of detection engineering. Your accurate closing classifications are the data that tuning depends on.
HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is58 / 84
Reduce false positives without creating false negatives.

The fastest way to zero false positives is turning the rule off, which converts every future true positive into a miss.

This is why 'just make the alerts stop' is a dangerous request. Too many FPs cause alert fatigue; tuning is choosing where on the dial to sit.

Severity, priority, and the clock

Why two alerts with the same label are not worked in the same order.

HACKTHESOCLesson 01What a SOC is60 / 84

Severity is not priority

Severity

How bad is this if it is real? A fixed scale, Low to Critical, mostly a property of the alert.

Priority

How fast must I act? Severity weighted by the asset's urgency and exposure. Priority orders your queue.

Key takeaways

  1. 01A SOC is people, process and technology turning logs into alerts and alerts into decisions, 24/7.
  2. 02Tier 1 triages, Tier 2 investigates, Tier 3 responds and hunts; SLAs and MTTD/MTTR set the pace.
  3. 03Every alert ends benign, suspicious or malicious; close with an accurate classification: benign positive is not false positive.
  4. 04Run the five questions on every alert: normal, what fired, before/after, who else, impact.
  5. 05Event, alert and incident are escalating stages; the false negative is the outcome to fear.
  6. 06The ticket is the product: evidence plus reasoning makes a verdict defensible, and the handover note lets the next shift carry on.
