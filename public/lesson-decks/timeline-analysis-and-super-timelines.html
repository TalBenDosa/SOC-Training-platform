Trace backward to patient zero, forward to the blast radius

Both answers come from walking the same ordered list in two directions.

  1. 1Start at one trusted eventAn alert or confirmed malicious action: the point you know is real.
  2. 2Walk backwardAsk what caused each event, then what caused that, until no earlier malicious step exists.
  3. 3Name patient zeroThe first malicious action, the initial access you must fix so it cannot happen again.
  4. 4Walk forwardList every machine and account the attacker touched after the start point.
  5. 5Size the containmentThat list is the blast radius: everything containment must cover, not just the alerting host.
Students often stop at the alerting host. The forward walk is what tells you the incident is bigger than the alert.

Timestamps: the raw material of timelines

MACB, two NTFS structures, and the time-zone trap.

HACKTHESOCLesson 79Timeline analysis11 / 60

MACB: four times per file

  • M: ModifiedWhen the file's content last changed.
  • A: AccessedLast read; updates are sometimes disabled for performance, so not always reliable.
  • C: ChangedWhen the MFT record (metadata) last changed.
  • B: BornWhen the file was created on the volume.

Modified before created is normal for copied, extracted or downloaded files; a created time matching the intrusion places a tool in the attack.

This is the same MACB set from the artifacts lesson, now as timeline raw material. The real timestomping tell is the two NTFS structures disagreeing, not M before B.
HACKTHESOCLesson 79Timeline analysis12 / 60

Reading MACB together: what each pattern means

Pattern on a fileWhat it usually meansAnalyst action
Modified earlier than BornCopied, extracted or downloaded: the original M is kept, a new B is stampedNormal on its own; do not call it tampering
Born inside the intrusion windowThe file first appeared on this volume during the attackStrong lead: a tool or payload placed by the attack
Changed differs from ModifiedThe MFT record (metadata) changed while the content did notNote it; metadata and content changes are separate events
Accessed time onlyLast read, but updates may be disabled for performanceWeak evidence; corroborate before relying on it
The trap is treating M before B as suspicious. It is the everyday result of downloading or unzipping. The real tamper check is the next slide.
HACKTHESOCLesson 79Timeline analysis13 / 60

Two NTFS structures, side by side in MFTECmd

  • SI = $STANDARD_INFORMATION: what tools show, and programs can change
  • FN = $FILE_NAME: harder for normal software to alter
  • The duplication becomes decisive in the anti-forensics section
Keep this example in mind; it reappears in the worked reconstruction.
HACKTHESOCLesson 79Timeline analysis14 / 60

SI or FN: which timestamp set to trust

SI

$STANDARD_INFORMATION

The times most tools and users see. Programs can change them, so they are the set an attacker backdates.

FN

$FILE_NAME

A second copy of the times that normal software has trouble altering. It is the cross-check for SI.

Reading them together

Agreeing SI and FN: no sign of tampering. Disagreeing on Created by years: a strong tamper lead.

In the evil.ps1 example SI says 2019 and FN says the intrusion day. FN is the one to trust; the anti-forensics section builds on this.
HACKTHESOCLesson 79Timeline analysis15 / 60

Time zones and clocks: the classic pitfall

  • Sources disagree on zoneSome record local time, some record UTC (Coordinated Universal Time).
  • Normalise firstConvert every source to one reference, usually UTC, before ordering anything.
  • What goes wrongMixed zones mis-order events: a one-hour error can make the effect precede the cause.
  • Clock driftHosts that drift or are not synchronised via NTP give subtly wrong times; note any known skew.
This is why enterprises synchronise clocks. Drift is seconds or minutes; a time-zone offset is a whole, known number of hours you correct for.
HACKTHESOCLesson 79Timeline analysis16 / 60

Worked conversion: two sources, one reference

A firewall logs in UTC; the workstation's exported event log is in local time, UTC+2.

SourceAs recordedConverted to UTC
Workstation log (UTC+2)16:05:03 powershell.exe created14:05:03: subtract the two-hour offset
Firewall log (UTC)14:06:10 outbound connection14:06:10: already UTC
Raw order (wrong)Connection first, process two hours laterThe effect appears before its cause
Normalised order (right)Process first, connection 67 seconds laterA coherent chain you can act on
Ask students to do the subtraction aloud. Adding the offset instead of subtracting is the most common slip.
HACKTHESOCLesson 79Timeline analysis17 / 60
Get timestamps wrong and a beautifully detailed timeline tells a confidently incorrect story.

MACB meaning, the two NTFS structures and one consistent time zone are the foundation everything else builds on.

Sleuth Kit bodyfile and mactime

The classic timeline format the super-timeline builds on.

HACKTHESOCLesson 79Timeline analysis19 / 60

The classic two-step timeline: list, then sort

The Sleuth Kit (TSK) splits timeline building in two, joined by a file called the bodyfile.

  1. 1List the filesfls reads a disk image (or mac-robber walks a live or mounted file system).
  2. 2Write the bodyfilePlain text, pipe-delimited, one line per file, timestamps as raw Unix epoch values.
  3. 3Sort with mactimemactime -b reads the bodyfile and orders every timestamp in time.
  4. 4Read the timelineOne row per timestamp event, labelled with which of M, A, C, B it is.
Still common on Linux/Unix and in small single-source cases, and it is the idea the super-timeline scales up.
HACKTHESOCLesson 79Timeline analysis20 / 60

One bodyfile line, field by field

Pipe-delimited: MD5|name|inode|mode_as_string|UID|GID|size|atime|mtime|ctime|crtime

FieldValue for evil.ps1
MD53d8e1a9c7f04b2e6d5a1c08f9b3e7d42
name / inode/Users/Public/evil.ps1 / 84213-128-3
mode / UID / GIDr/rrwxrwxrwx / 0 / 0
size1024 bytes
atime / mtime1786716360 / 1786716302
ctime / crtime1786716303 / 1786716302

mode_as_string r/rrwxrwxrwx is TSK rendering NTFS permissions in POSIX rwx form, not real Unix perms.

Timestamps are raw Unix epoch values, seconds since 1 January 1970: here 1786716302 decodes to 2026-08-14 14:05:02 UTC, the real creation instant. Compact but not human-readable, an intermediate format produced by fls or mac-robber.
HACKTHESOCLesson 79Timeline analysis21 / 60

fls builds the bodyfile, mactime renders it

  • One row per timestamp event, so one file can become up to four rows
  • m..b: Modified and Born fired at this instant; dots for A and C
mactime does for a bodyfile what psort does for a Plaso store: sort by time and render readably.
HACKTHESOCLesson 79Timeline analysis22 / 60

Reading mactime's flag column

Each position stands for one timestamp in M, A, C, B order; a dot means that one did not fire.

  • m..bModified and Born at the same instant: typical of a file written once when it was created.
  • .a..Only Accessed: the file was read at this time. Weak evidence if access updates are disabled.
  • ..c.Only Changed: the MFT record (metadata) changed while the content did not.
  • macbAll four at once: the whole set was stamped in the same second.
The flag column is the MACB idea from section 2 made literal. One file can appear on up to four rows, each with its own flags.
HACKTHESOCLesson 79Timeline analysis23 / 60

fls + mactime (Sleuth Kit)log2timeline + psort (Plaso)

Building a super-timeline with Plaso

Every timestamped source, merged into one chronology.

HACKTHESOCLesson 79Timeline analysis25 / 60

A timeline versus a super-timeline

One source

Basic timeline

Usually file-system timestamps only. It shows files appearing, but not why or what ran them.

Every source

Super-timeline

MACB times, event logs, Prefetch, Amcache, registry key times, browser history: one ordered list.

Only the merged view shows a Run key created 2 s after a program ran, 1 min after a suspicious logon.

No single source shows that chain. The super-timeline does, because all three sit side by side in time.
HACKTHESOCLesson 79Timeline analysis26 / 60

Two steps: extract, then render

  • Input can be a disk image or a KAPE collection
  • case01.plaso is a compact internal store, not read directly
  • -o dynamic: a wide CSV you open like a spreadsheet
Plaso stands for 'Plaso Langar Að Safna Öllu', roughly 'Plaso, gather everything'. log2timeline is its main extraction tool.
HACKTHESOCLesson 79Timeline analysis27 / 60

What each step does under the hood

  1. 1log2timeline walks the evidenceA disk image or a KAPE collection, file by file.
  2. 2Recognise formats automaticallyDozens of parsers: MFT, event logs, registry hives, browser history and more.
  3. 3Normalise every eventOne common structure and one time reference, written into the .plaso store.
  4. 4psort renders the storeSorts every event and writes it in the output module you choose, such as dynamic CSV.
log2timeline produces no human-readable output on its own. Students who open the .plaso file expecting a table are confused; psort is the rendering step.

Why merging beats reading each source separately

Separately

Open the MFT output, the registry output and the event log one by one, then cross-reference times in your head. Slow, and a forgotten source is a missed step.

Merged

log2timeline and psort merge mechanically and exhaustively, so nothing is left out because someone forgot to check it.

The cost

Exhaustive means enormous: millions of rows. Section 5 is how you read them.

Frame the cost honestly: the super-timeline trades analyst memory for data volume, and windowing is how you pay that bill.

Section 5 of 7

Reading a super-timeline: pivot and window

Millions of rows, mostly benign. Read strategically.

HACKTHESOCLesson 79Timeline analysis36 / 60
A super-timeline captures everything, so almost every row is benign. You cannot read it top to bottom; you read it strategically.

Routine file times, normal registry updates and everyday application activity bury the handful of attacker rows.

Set expectations: three million rows is normal for one workstation. The skill is reducing them, not scrolling them.
HACKTHESOCLesson 79Timeline analysis37 / 60

Anchor on a pivot, then window around it

  1. 1Find a pivotOne event you trust completely: an EDR alert time, a known-bad Amcache hash, a suspicious login.
  2. 2Filter a tight windowE.g. ten minutes before, thirty after: a date/time range filter in Timeline Explorer.
  3. 3Read the window in fullAttacker actions cluster tightly; background noise falls outside.
  4. 4Expand outwardEach new lead (filename, IP, username) becomes a mini-pivot. Widen or refilter and repeat.
Timeline Explorer, from Eric Zimmerman's toolkit, handles very large CSVs with fast filtering. Combine a time window with a source or keyword filter to get to a few dozen rows.
HACKTHESOCLesson 79Timeline analysis38 / 60

StepChoice in the WKS-DESKTOP07 case
PivotEDR alert at 14:06:10 UTC: powershell.exe connecting out

Read in fullWord opens, evil.ps1 appears, PowerShell starts, a Run key is added
New lead: a filenameFilter on evil.ps1 across every source and time
New lead: a hostA logon to SRV-FILE02 appears: pivot there and repeat
Each new name, IP or user found in the window becomes a mini-pivot. That loop is how the worked example in section 7 was built.
HACKTHESOCLesson 79Timeline analysis39 / 60

Cut rows two ways: by time and by column

Time window

A date and time range around the pivot removes the background noise from the rest of the day.

Source filter

Only rows where source is WINREG, for example, when hunting persistence.

Keyword filter

Only rows whose desc contains a filename, IP or username that just surfaced.

Combined, a tight window plus a source or keyword filter takes a million rows down to a few dozen.

Timeline Explorer, from the Eric Zimmerman toolkit, does this with fast column filters on very large CSVs.
HACKTHESOCLesson 79Timeline analysis40 / 60

Filter at the psort stage, before any viewer

  • The filter expression is psort's final argument
  • Fields: date, timestamp, parser, source_short, message
  • Combine with AND, OR, contains, is
This produces a pre-narrowed CSV straight from the store instead of rendering millions of rows first.
HACKTHESOCLesson 79Timeline analysis41 / 60

Filter in the viewer or at the psort stage

In Timeline Explorer

Render the full CSV once, then filter interactively. Good for exploring when you do not yet know what you want.

At the psort stage

The filter expression is psort's final argument, so only matching rows are ever written. Good for a known window or source.

Both read the same .plaso store. Neither requires re-running log2timeline, which is the slow step.

Section 6 of 7

Anti-forensics and reading timelines critically

Attackers corrupt the temporal record; the tampering is evidence.

HACKTHESOCLesson 79Timeline analysis43 / 60

How a super-timeline catches timestomping

The trick

Backdate a malicious file to look old and legitimate, so a naive timeline places it far from the intrusion. Indicator Removal: Timestomp.

The catch

Tools alter $STANDARD_INFORMATION but not $FILE_NAME. log2timeline extracts both as separate rows, so one sits outside the window while the other lands inside it.

Two rows for the same file that should agree, but say 2019 and last Tuesday.

The super-timeline surfaces the contradiction automatically.
HACKTHESOCLesson 79Timeline analysis44 / 60

Catching a stomped file, step by step

  1. 1Window on the pivotA $FILE_NAME creation row for evil.ps1 sits at 14:05:02, inside the window.
  2. 2Look for its twin rowFilter on the filename across all time to find the $STANDARD_INFORMATION row.
  3. 3Compare the twoSI says 2019-03-11, years before the intrusion. FN says the intrusion day.
  4. 4CorroboratePrefetch and event-log rows around 14:05 agree with FN, not with SI.
  5. 5Record it as evidenceBackdating is Timestomp, T1070.006: the cover-up is itself a finding.
The super-timeline does the hard part: it extracts both sets as separate rows, so the contradiction is visible as soon as you filter on the file.

Other ways attackers corrupt the record, and their tells

Clear logs

Event-log entries that would sit on the timeline are removed.

  • Tell: a log-clear event
  • Tell: silence while other artifacts show activity

Delete artifacts

The tool or script is removed from disk after use.

  • Tell: Prefetch, Amcache or $MFT name a file that is gone

Blend in

Activity is timed for busy periods to hide among normal rows.

  • Tell: execution artifacts with no matching log entries
Covering every source is extremely hard, so gaps and inconsistencies appear. The gap is the clue.

HACKTHESOCLesson 79Timeline analysis48 / 60
Anti-forensics is itself an event on the timeline: the cover-up in one source is timestamped by another.

A log clear, a service stop or two contradicting rows are not absence of evidence. They are evidence of someone removing evidence.

This is why a multi-source super-timeline makes anti-forensics self-defeating.
HACKTHESOCLesson 79Timeline analysis49 / 60

Reading a timeline critically

  • Corroborate across sourcesPrefetch, the FN time and an event log agreeing is solid, even if one SI time was stomped.
  • Treat anomalies as leadsA mismatch, a log gap, an out-of-order artifact is often the thread that reveals the hiding.
  • Anchor on the hard-to-fakePrefer timestamps that are difficult to alter; be cautious with easily modified ones.
Where the timeline does not make sense is frequently where the attacker tried hardest to disappear.

Key takeaways

  1. 01Timelines answer sequence: trace backward to patient zero and forward to the blast radius.
  2. 02Know MACB and the SI vs FN structures, and normalise every source to one time zone, usually UTC.
  3. 03fls builds a bodyfile and mactime renders it; log2timeline and psort scale that to every artifact.
  4. 04Anchor on a trusted pivot, window tightly, filter by source or keyword, then expand outward.
  5. 05Timestomping shows as disagreeing SI and FN rows; log clears and service stops are events too.
  6. 06Corroborate across sources and treat gaps and mismatches as leads, not inconveniences.

A Windows event log in local time (UTC+2) shows powershell.exe launching at 16:06:10. A cloud sign-in log in UTC shows a suspicious sign-in for the same user at 14:30:00. After normalising both to UTC, which is correct?

16:06:10 at UTC+2 is 14:06:10 UTC, so PowerShell ran about 24 minutes before the sign-in. Comparing raw times makes the effect precede the cause; 18:06 adds the offset instead of subtracting; drift is seconds or minutes, not whole hours.

MFTECmd shows evil.ps1 in C:\Users\Public with SI Created 2019-03-11 10:02:00 UTC but FN Created 2026-08-14 14:05:02 UTC, inside your intrusion window. What does this most likely indicate?

Tools change SI but not the harder-to-alter FN, so SI 2019 vs FN in the window is backdating (T1070.006). Copying explains Modified before Created, not SI and FN disagreeing on Created; drift is not seven years; FN is the one to trust.
