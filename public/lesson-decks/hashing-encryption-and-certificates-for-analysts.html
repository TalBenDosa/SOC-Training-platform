Section 1 of 13

A hash is a fingerprint, not a container

Four words you must not confuse, then the fingerprint itself.

HACKTHESOCLesson 04Hashing, crypto and certs6 / 54

One sealed letter, four different ideas

Photo of the wax seal

Hashing

A unique fingerprint of what is inside. No key, no way to rebuild the letter.

Morse code

Encoding

Anyone who knows the public alphabet translates it back. Nothing is hidden, only reformatted.

A locked box

Encryption

Symmetric: one shared key locks and unlocks. Asymmetric: a public key locks, only the private key unlocks.

A notary's stamp

Certificate

Hides nothing. A CA's signed statement that this public key belongs to this named sender.

Every field in this lesson slots into one of these four boxes. Hold them apart.
HACKTHESOCLesson 04Hashing, crypto and certs7 / 54

Hashing identifies; encryption conceals and reverses

SHA-256 always gives exactly 64 hex characters, whether you fed it one byte or one terabyte. A 4 GB file cannot live inside 64 characters: the hash only identifies it.
HACKTHESOCLesson 04Hashing, crypto and certs8 / 54

Deterministic, one-way, and avalanche

  • One character in changed essentially every character out
  • Digests are never 'similar': test only for EXACT equality
  • A digest is a safe, portable name: share it, not the malware
Integrity checking falls out of this too: hash a file today and next month; if digests differ, something changed it even if size and timestamp look untouched.
HACKTHESOCLesson 04Hashing, crypto and certs9 / 54

Tell the algorithms apart by length alone

AlgorithmDigest lengthStatus
MD532 hex charactersBroken: attackers can craft two files with the same MD5
SHA-140 hex charactersBroken: shown publicly in 2017 with two PDFs sharing one digest
SHA-25664 hex charactersCurrent standard; prefer it whenever you have a choice

Broken means: fine as a lookup key, not proof that two files are the same when an adversary controls the file.

Your logs will be full of MD5 for years. Using one to look up a known sample is perfectly reasonable.

Section 2 of 13

Why a hash is the weakest thing to hunt on

A file hash identifies one exact build, and the attacker controls the file.

HACKTHESOCLesson 04Hashing, crypto and certs11 / 54

A hash match confirms; it does not hunt

What it is great at

Fast, unambiguous identification of a known sample. A hash match is essentially never a false positive.

Where it fails

Add a comment, pad junk bytes, change a config string. Same behaviour, same operator, new hash. The blocklist does not fire.

Pyramid of Pain: hashes sit at the bottom, TTPs at the top, because changing a hash costs the attacker seconds.

A strategy built only on known-bad hashes catches yesterday's malware and misses today's. That is why EDR moved to behavioural detection and why threat hunting exists.

Section 3 of 13

Encryption: built to be reversed

Plaintext to ciphertext and back. The secrecy lives in the key, never the algorithm.

HACKTHESOCLesson 04Hashing, crypto and certs13 / 54

Two families solve different problems

AES

Symmetric

One shared key encrypts and decrypts. Fast, so it carries bulk data. Hard part: getting the key to the other side unseen.

RSA, elliptic curve

Asymmetric

A public and private pair. What the public key locks, only the private opens. Slow, so used for key agreement and signing.

HTTPS uses both: asymmetric briefly to agree a shared secret, then fast symmetric for the traffic.

Public key as a post box slot: anyone can drop mail in, only you hold the key to take it out. Proprietary 'secret' encryption is a warning sign, not a selling point.
HACKTHESOCLesson 04Hashing, crypto and certs14 / 54

Signing runs the same maths backwards

  • Digital signatureEncrypt with my private key; anyone with my public key verifies. Useless for secrecy, perfect for proof.
  • Where you meet itDKIM proving an email came from a domain; certificates proving a server is who it claims.
  • Metadata is not hiddenEncryption protects content, not who talked to whom, when, how long and how many bytes.
That metadata is often enough to detect beaconing, exfiltration or a known-bad destination, which is why network detection still works in an HTTPS world.
HACKTHESOCLesson 04Hashing, crypto and certs15 / 54

Base64 is encoding, not encryption

  • Encoded? Decode it now and read the attacker's intent
  • Encrypted? No decoding helps: pivot to who, when, how much, to where
This catches nearly every beginner. Attackers rely on analysts assuming that unreadable means inaccessible.

Section 4 of 13

Certificates: how strangers decide to trust

Binding a public key to an identity so you know the key really is the bank's.

HACKTHESOCLesson 04Hashing, crypto and certs17 / 54

Four checks before the browser shows the padlock

A certificate binds a domain name, a public key and a CA signature. Trust chains back to built-in root CAs.

  1. 1Name matchesThe name on the certificate matches the site actually being visited.
  2. 2Dates validWithin its validity dates and not expired.
  3. 3Chain to a rootThe signature chains back to a root CA in the local trust store.
  4. 4Not revokedIt has not been revoked since it was issued.
Notice what is on that list, and more importantly what is not: anything about the operator's honesty.
HACKTHESOCLesson 04Hashing, crypto and certs18 / 54
The padlock proves the connection is encrypted and the name matches. It proves nothing about whether the people running the site are honest.

Domain-validated certificates are free and issued in seconds, so essentially every phishing site now has a padlock.

Say it plainly to users who ask: 'it is secure' and 'it is trustworthy' are different claims, and the certificate supports only the first.
HACKTHESOCLesson 04Hashing, crypto and certs19 / 54

Certificate fields that do investigative work

  • IssuerWho signed it. Self-signed (issuer equals subject) means nobody independent vouched for it.
  • Subject / CN and SANWhich names the certificate claims to cover.
  • Validity datesA certificate created hours before you saw it is worth a second look.
  • FingerprintA hash of the certificate itself, to track one exact cert across sightings.
  • JA3 / JA3SA fingerprint of how client and server negotiated, which can identify the client program.

Self-signed is normal on printers, appliances and labs; short-lived certs are standard practice. Reasons to look, never verdicts.

Expect false positives: internal management interfaces and dev environments have no reason to buy a public certificate.

Worked example: hash, verdict, certificate

The two moves you make almost every shift, with real command output.

HACKTHESOCLesson 04Hashing, crypto and certs21 / 54

Hash locally, then check the exact digest

  • Linux and macOS equivalent: sha256sum suspicious_invoice.exe
  • 41 engines flag the exact same digest: about as certain as detection gets
  • No detonation, no code reading: you matched a fingerprint
The next shift or a threat-intel API never needs the actual file, only the digest.
HACKTHESOCLesson 04Hashing, crypto and certs22 / 54

Read the certificate on the outbound connection

  • Subject equals Issuer: self-signed, nobody independent vouched
  • Not Before is hours before the connection happened
  • Plus an infostealer verdict on the same host: a pattern, not coincidence
Neither fact alone proves malice. Hash to identify, look up to get a verdict, read the certificate for context: three cheap checks turn one alert into a confident call.

Where this shows up in the logs you read

Each idea, connected to the field you will literally see.

HACKTHESOCLesson 04Hashing, crypto and certs24 / 54

The same ideas in four real log sources

Hashes in EDR are the single most common use of cryptography in daily work: check intel, find every machine that ran the same binary, confirm two alerts share a sample.
HACKTHESOCLesson 04Hashing, crypto and certs25 / 54

Each field is a lead, not a verdict

DKIM pass

Proves the domain really sent it, unaltered. A compromised account or a lookalike domain sends DKIM-valid phishing.

0x17 vs 0x12

0x17 (23) is RC4, crackable offline, a Kerberoasting signal (T1558.003). 0x12 (18) is AES-256. Legacy accounts request RC4 innocently.

Self-signed issuer

Suspicious on an external destination; completely normal on an internal printer or appliance.

DKIM answers 'did this domain really send it', not 'should you trust it'.
HACKTHESOCLesson 04Hashing, crypto and certs26 / 54

Hashes as credentials, and encryption as a weapon

  • Pass-the-hashFor NTLM, the stored hash itself is enough to authenticate. Treat a stolen hash like a stolen password.
  • RansomwareStrong, correctly implemented encryption. Recovery without the key is not realistic: backups, not decryption, are the answer.
  • C2 inside TLSCommand-and-control hides in TLS for the same reason your bank does.
The mathematics is neutral; only the intent differs.

Section 7 of 13

Fuzzy hashing and imphash

Catching the malware family, not just one file.

HACKTHESOCLesson 04Hashing, crypto and certs28 / 54

Exact hashes catch one file; similarity catches the family

Recompiling, re-packing or adding a meaningless byte costs the attacker seconds and defeats an exact-hash blocklist.
HACKTHESOCLesson 04Hashing, crypto and certs29 / 54

Trade exactness for similarity

ssdeep, TLSH

Fuzzy hashing

Mostly-same files give mostly-same hashes. Two 97% identical variants score about 97% similar, so v1, v2, v3 cluster.

Windows executables

Imphash

A hash of the import table. Same source or toolkit tends to import the same functions in the same order.

Sections, properties

Structural hashes

Hash a part or property that stays stable across variants, not the whole byte stream.

Exact hash: 'have I seen this exact file?' Fuzzy and imphash: 'have I seen anything like this, anywhere?'

That second question is how one sample on one host expands into the other five machines running slightly different builds. It is where you start climbing the Pyramid of Pain.

When traffic is encrypted, do not give up. Pivot to the metadata the encryption cannot hide.

The TLS handshake is not itself encrypted, so the certificate, its issuer, validity and rarity sit readable on top of a body you will never see.

Most malicious traffic today is encrypted, as the C2 lesson covers. These tells are how you flag C2 you could never decrypt.

Section 9 of 13

Code signing: trust, and how attackers steal it

A certificate-backed promise that software came from this publisher, unaltered.

HACKTHESOCLesson 04Hashing, crypto and certs34 / 54

What a valid signature does and does not prove

Authenticode

What it proves

Origin: it really is from that publisher. Integrity: not tampered with since signing. Hence 'verified publisher: Microsoft Corporation'.

What it does not

That the behaviour is safe, that the signing key was not stolen, or what the program goes on to load and run.

A signature vouches for the file it is on, and nothing more.

The trust a signature confers stops at the file's edge. Everything that file does is still yours to judge.
HACKTHESOCLesson 04Hashing, crypto and certs35 / 54

Three ways attackers get around signing

  • Stolen signing keysA real company's private key signs malware so it looks trusted. A prized supply-chain target.
  • Signed but abused toolsLOLBins and DLL sideloading: a signed executable is led into loading the attacker's unsigned DLL.
  • Fraudulent or lapsed certsAcquired under a shell company, or one whose trust should have lapsed.

Treat a valid signature as one useful data point, not a clean bill of health.

The most damaging intrusions in recent memory have ridden on stolen signing certificates.

Diffie-Hellman and forward secrecy

How two strangers agree on a secret in public without ever sending it.

HACKTHESOCLesson 04Hashing, crypto and certs37 / 54

Diffie-Hellman with small numbers

  • Both reach s = 2; neither private value ever crossed the network
  • Recovering a, b or s from p, g, A, B is the discrete logarithm problem
  • Infeasible once p is a 2048+ bit prime instead of 23
Paint-mixing analogy: a public starting colour, each side mixes in a secret colour and sends only the mixture, each adds their secret again, both end with the same colour a watcher cannot reproduce. Published 1976.
HACKTHESOCLesson 04Hashing, crypto and certs38 / 54

Static RSA versus ephemeral Diffie-Hellman

Static RSA key exchange

Client encrypts its part with the server's long-term key. Steal that key years later and every recorded session decrypts retroactively.

Ephemeral DH (DHE / ECDHE)

A fresh temporary key pair per session, then discarded. The long-term cert only signs the exchange. Perfect Forward Secrecy.

TLS 1.3 removed static RSA key exchange entirely and made ephemeral elliptic-curve DH mandatory.

In logs, a named curve like x25519 or secp256r1 as the negotiated group is a quick positive signal; static RSA, only possible on legacy TLS 1.2, is not forward-secret and worth flagging when hardening.

Section 11 of 13

Passwords need slow, salted hashes

Fast is right for files and exactly wrong for passwords.

HACKTHESOCLesson 04Hashing, crypto and certs40 / 54

Why speed is the vulnerability

  • Fast hash leaksGPUs compute enormous numbers of SHA-256 per second; a wordlist cracks any non-random password.
  • SaltRandom, unique per password, stored openly beside the hash. Defeats precomputed rainbow tables.
  • PepperA secret value shared app-wide, stored apart from the database. Hand-rolled schemes are now a mistake.
  • Slow on purposeA fraction of a second per login, prohibitive across billions of guesses.
Modern password-hashing algorithms handle salting internally and correctly. It is easy to get a home-made scheme wrong in a way that looks fine and is not.
HACKTHESOCLesson 04Hashing, crypto and certs41 / 54

Three purpose-built password hashes

AlgorithmHardnessNotes
bcryptCPU-hard, not memory-hardCost factor doubles the work per increment; more exposed to GPUs and ASICs
scryptMemory-hardNeeds lots of RAM per guess, expensive to parallelize
Argon2idMemory, time and parallelism tunable2015 PHC winner, RFC 9106; NIST and OWASP default for new systems

FIPS-only? OWASP: PBKDF2 (NIST SP 800-132) with ~600,000 iterations of HMAC-SHA-256. The count is OWASP's, not NIST's.

NIST SP 800-63B requires a salted, purpose-built one-way KDF, and its latest revision calls for a memory-hard scheme.
HACKTHESOCLesson 04Hashing, crypto and certs42 / 54

The hash string tells you how exposed a breach is

A bcrypt or Argon2id prefix means a real but bounded exposure. Bare 32-char hex (MD5) or 64-char hex with no salt cracks at enormous speed: treat every non-random password as already compromised.

Revocation: CRL and OCSP

Telling the world to stop trusting a certificate before its printed expiry.

HACKTHESOCLesson 04Hashing, crypto and certs44 / 54

Three revocation mechanisms, three trade-offs

MechanismFreshnessSizePrivacy
CRLStale between publication cyclesCan be large (whole list)No per-check leak
OCSP (direct)Real-timeSmallCA sees every site you visit
OCSP staplingReal-time, server-refreshedSmall, pre-fetchedNo leak: CA only talks to the server
Revocation is needed when a private key is stolen, a CA issued in error, or the owner no longer controls the domain. CRL is a signed list of revoked serial numbers; OCSP returns good, revoked or unknown.
HACKTHESOCLesson 04Hashing, crypto and certs45 / 54

Checking OCSP status directly

With stapling, the web server queries the responder itself, caches the signed response and staples it into the TLS handshake for every visitor.
HACKTHESOCLesson 04Hashing, crypto and certs46 / 54
Most browsers soft-fail: if the revocation check cannot complete, they trust the certificate anyway.

An attacker who can block OCSP traffic can make a stolen, already-revoked certificate look valid. The industry answer is short-lived certificates.

Hard-fail would break too much legitimate traffic whenever a responder has an outage, so revocation fails open. Certs valid for only days need revocation far less.

Section 13 of 13

HMAC: proving origin with a shared secret

Integrity and authenticity using only fast symmetric hashing.

HACKTHESOCLesson 04Hashing, crypto and certs48 / 54

Verifying an HMAC-signed webhook

  • A plain hash can be recomputed by anyone who alters the message
  • Mixing in a shared secret means only key-holders can forge it
  • Same idea in AWS Signature Version 4 and JWT HS256
Investigative angle: invalid or missing HMAC on a webhook endpoint means misconfiguration or someone forging events. Check whether the failures come from outside the expected sender's infrastructure.
HACKTHESOCLesson 04Hashing, crypto and certs49 / 54

HMAC vs digital signature: who can do what

Produce a valid valueVerify a valid valueFits
HMACAnyone with the shared secret keyAnyone with the same secret keyClosed pair, e.g. one API integration
Digital signatureOnly the private key holderAnyone with the public keyOpen, unknown verifiers, e.g. every browser
HMAC cannot prove which of the two key-holders sent a message, only that the sender knows the shared secret.

Key takeaways

  1. 01Hashing is one-way and identifies without containing; compare digests only for exact equality.
  2. 02A hash confirms a known sample but never hunts new ones; fuzzy hashing and imphash catch the family.
  3. 03Encryption is reversible by design; base64 is encoding, so decode it and read it.
  4. 04The padlock proves encryption and a name match, not honesty; signatures vouch for origin, not behaviour.
  5. 05Certificate, DKIM, 0x17 and JA3 fields are leads to investigate, not verdicts on their own.
  6. 06Prefer Argon2id or bcrypt for passwords, ECDHE for forward secrecy, HMAC for closed-pair signing.

Check your understanding, question 1 of 3

Your team blocks a malware sample by its SHA-256 hash. The same actor returns the following week with functionally identical malware, and the hash block does not fire. What is the most likely explanation?

Because of the avalanche property, any change to the file produces an entirely different digest while behaviour stays intact. That is why hashes sit at the bottom of the Pyramid of Pain. Reversing a hash is exactly what the one-way property prevents.

Check your understanding, question 2 of 3

A user asks whether a site is safe, pointing out that the browser shows a padlock and a valid certificate. What does that certificate actually establish?

The browser checks name match, validity dates, chain to a trusted root and revocation, all properties of the connection, not the operator. Domain-validated certs are free and automatic, so a phishing domain registered minutes ago has a valid one.

Check your understanding, question 3 of 3

During triage you find a PowerShell command line with a long base64 string passed to -enc. What is the correct conclusion and next step?

Base64 is a transport encoding with no key, so decoding it is a standard triage step that usually reveals the attacker's command. Attackers use it to obscure intent from casual inspection and naive string matching, not to protect it.
