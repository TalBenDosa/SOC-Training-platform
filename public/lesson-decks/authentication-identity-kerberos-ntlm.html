An attacker with a valid credential does not need an exploit. They simply log in, and to your logs they look like a legitimate user.

That is why authentication is the most important domain for a SOC analyst to understand deeply.

Almost every intrusion touches identity. If you cannot read a logon event, you will stare at the records that describe a domain compromise and see nothing wrong.
HACKTHESOCLesson 07Authentication and identity5 / 59

Four words this whole lesson rests on

Identity

The claim: a username, an account, the fact 'I am jsmith.'

Authentication

The proof: demonstrating the claim is true, by presenting something only the real jsmith would have.

Hash

A one-way, scrambled fingerprint of a password. Easy to compute, not easy to reverse, so systems store hashes, not passwords.

Ticket

A small, cryptographically sealed pass that says the KDC already verified this identity, so no need to check the password again.

Hold onto these four words. Once the difference between them is clear, the mechanics ahead are just details.

What Active Directory is

A company phone directory bolted onto a building keycard system.

HACKTHESOCLesson 07Authentication and identity7 / 59

AD stores identities and enforces access

The phone directory

The database of every person, computer, group and printer: who they are, their department, their manager.

The keycard system

The enforcement layer: when you swipe, something checks whether you may pass. AD decides who may access what, thousands of times per second.

Active Directory is the identity and access backbone of nearly every corporate Windows network.

Before you can spot an attack against AD you have to know what it is made of and why it exists. This analogy carries the whole section.
HACKTHESOCLesson 07Authentication and identity8 / 59

Domain, DCs, OUs, groups and the forest

  • DomainThe boundary of trust. Every account in corp.example.com trusts the same servers to answer 'is this person who they claim?'
  • Domain controller (DC)Holds the AD database (NTDS.dit), runs authentication. Control a DC and you control every identity in the domain.
  • Organizational UnitsFolders that organize objects and apply policy. Administrative convenience, not a hard security boundary.
  • GroupsHow permissions are granted at scale. Domain Admins, Enterprise Admins, Account Operators are the crown jewels.
  • ForestThe outermost boundary: domains sharing a schema and global catalog. A compromise in one can become a path into another.
Events from a DC, such as ticket requests, replication and account changes, deserve extra scrutiny because the DC is the single most valuable target.
HACKTHESOCLesson 07Authentication and identity9 / 59

The AD map an analyst pivots on

  • Know which servers are DCs and where trust boundaries lie
  • Privileged group changes (4728, 4732, 4756) always warrant review
You do not need to administer AD, but you must reason about it: which accounts are privileged, which servers are DCs, and where an attacker with a foothold will go next.

How a logon really works: Kerberos

Your password is never sent across the network. Encrypted tickets prove identity instead.

HACKTHESOCLesson 07Authentication and identity11 / 59

Three steps from password to service access

  1. 1AS-REQ to the KDCThe workstation sends a timestamp encrypted with a key derived from your password. The KDC runs on every DC.
  2. 2TGT issued: Event 4768If the KDC can decrypt the timestamp, it returns a Ticket-Granting Ticket, your master pass for the day.
  3. 3Service ticket: Event 4769Present the TGT, ask for a TGS for one SPN, e.g. MSSQLSvc/db01.corp.example.com.
  4. 4Ticket to the serviceThe service validates the TGS and grants access. It never calls back to the DC: the ticket is the proof.
Kerberos is the default, preferred protocol in modern AD. Knowing this flow is what lets you tell normal logons from ticket-based attacks.
HACKTHESOCLesson 07Authentication and identity12 / 59

Encryption type: RC4 on a ticket is a question

The field is TicketEncryptionType. Two values appear constantly.

AES256-CTS-HMAC-SHA1-96

0x12: AES256

The modern, strong default. In a healthy domain, service ticket requests should overwhelmingly use AES.

RC4-HMAC

0x17: RC4

Legacy, weak and offline-crackable. Some legacy apps need it, but Kerberoasting deliberately requests RC4 tickets to crack the service account password.

A spike of RC4 4769 events from one source against many SPNs is very likely Kerberoasting in progress.

RC4 is not proof of attack, but it is worth asking every time: normal for this account, or is someone farming crackable tickets? It is a low-noise field to build detection around.
HACKTHESOCLesson 07Authentication and identity13 / 59

A 4769 with an RC4 ticket on a service account

  • AccountName is the requester; ServiceName is the targeted service account
  • TicketEncryptionType 0x17 = RC4 (weak); 0x12 = AES256 (normal)
  • Hunt: 0x17 plus many distinct TargetServiceName from one account = T1558.003
Status 0x0 means success. The request itself is legitimate Kerberos; the pattern across many SPNs is what makes it Kerberoasting.

Worked example: one logon from AS-REQ to file

Correlate several events into one story: which land on the DC, in what order, and how they tie together.

HACKTHESOCLesson 07Authentication and identity15 / 59

jsmith's morning, minute by minute

  1. 108:59:58 unlockWindows sends an AS-REQ for her. The KDC returns a TGT and DC01 logs 4768.
  2. 231 seconds laterShe opens the Finance share. The workstation presents the TGT and requests a service ticket.
  3. 3DC01 logs 4769The KDC issues the ticket for the share's SPN.
  4. 4Access grantedThe share accepts the ticket. No further contact with the DC is needed.
Two events, one human action, one continuous story. That is what you should see when you pull DC01's Security log for her AccountName in that minute.
HACKTHESOCLesson 07Authentication and identity16 / 59

The correlated pair: what normal looks like

  • Same AccountName in both events
  • Same IpAddress in both events
  • Timestamps close together in a plausible human sequence
This boring, legitimate chain is the pattern you build your baseline from. Note AES (0x12) on both tickets.
HACKTHESOCLesson 07Authentication and identity17 / 59

Change one field and the story breaks

Intact chain

The 4769 comes from the same IpAddress as the 4768 for that account, seconds later. One person, one machine.

Pass-the-ticket, T1550.003

Broken chain

The 4769 arrives from a different IpAddress, a server she never uses. A stolen TGT is being replayed from another host, no password needed.

Automate it: flag any 4769 whose IpAddress does not match the preceding 4768 for the same account.

A single 4769 tells you almost nothing. The 4768-then-4769 pair correlated on account, source and time is the story, and a broken correlation is the incident. This rule needs nothing about the attacker's tooling.

NTLM and why it is still around

The older challenge-response protocol behind pass-the-hash, often less closely monitored.

HACKTHESOCLesson 07Authentication and identity19 / 59

The NTLM challenge-response exchange

  1. 1NEGOTIATEThe client announces it wants to authenticate.
  2. 2CHALLENGEThe server replies with a random number, a nonce.
  3. 3AUTHENTICATEThe client encrypts the challenge with its NTLM hash and sends the response.
  4. 4Verify and logThe server or DC verifies it. DC logs 4776; the target logs 4624 with package NTLM.

The hash itself proves identity. The plaintext password is never needed at authentication time.

That design fact is the seed of the whole pass-the-hash problem. Steal the hash and you can authenticate as the user.
HACKTHESOCLesson 07Authentication and identity20 / 59

NTLM appears wherever Kerberos cannot be used

  • Connecting by IP addressKerberos needs an SPN tied to a DNS name, so addressing a resource by IP forces NTLM.
  • No trust pathSystems in a domain without a proper trust path fall back to NTLM.
  • Legacy and toolingOlder applications, some VPN and proxy authentication, many scanning and admin tools.
  • What the logs show4624 with Authentication Package NTLM on the target, and 4776 (credential validation) on the DC.
A sudden rise in NTLM where you would expect Kerberos, especially to sensitive servers, is worth investigating.
HACKTHESOCLesson 07Authentication and identity21 / 59

Pass-the-hash: why stealing the hash is enough

T1550.002

What happens

An attacker reads a user's NTLM hash from LSASS memory, the process that holds credentials, and uses it directly to authenticate. No cracking needed.

What you see

A valid NTLM 4624 from an unexpected source workstation, often LogonType 3 or 9, frequently from a machine the account has never touched.

What to ask

Pass-the-hash rarely announces itself loudly.

  • Does this user normally log on from this host?
  • Should this have been Kerberos?
  • Privileged account on NTLM from a workstation, not its jump box?
The lesson names Mimikatz as a common tool for reading LSASS. As defenders we care about the result in the logs: identity moving in ways the human behind it never would.
HACKTHESOCLesson 07Authentication and identity22 / 59

The 4624 signature to hunt

  • LogonType 3 is a network logon; NTLM where Kerberos was expected
  • TargetUserName is a privileged account
  • Ask: does admin-jsmith normally log on from WKS-4471?
Nothing in this single record is malformed. It is the combination of package, account and source host that makes it worth a second look.
HACKTHESOCLesson 07Authentication and identity23 / 59

Kerberos needs a ticket; NTLM only needs the hash

Use this to recap: NTLM on a domain-joined host deserves a second look because the hash alone proves identity.

Logon types and reading authentication logs

4624 and 4625: decode LogonType for how, and SubStatus for why it failed.

HACKTHESOCLesson 07Authentication and identity25 / 59

LogonType tells you how someone authenticated

LogonTypeWhat it means
2 (Interactive)Someone at the console typed credentials, or a local KVM/RDP console session
3 (Network)Accessing a resource over the network: share, printer, web app. By far the most common
4 (Batch) / 5 (Service)Scheduled tasks / service accounts starting services

9 (NewCredentials)Appears with runas /netonly; common in pass-the-hash and credential abuse

Type 10 to servers, especially by privileged accounts from unusual IPs, is a prime lateral-movement indicator. An interactive Type 2 logon by a normal user on a server is also unusual.

HACKTHESOCLesson 07Authentication and identity27 / 59

A 4625 from a password spray

  • TargetUserName varies each event: agarcia, bpatel, cwong...
  • Status 0xC000006D is the top-level failure; SubStatus gives the reason
  • Hunt: count distinct TargetUserName by IpAddress in 5m
One external source, many valid users, wrong password each time. The single event is ordinary; the count of distinct users is the detection.
HACKTHESOCLesson 07Authentication and identity28 / 59

Know the boring Tuesday so anomalies jump out

Normal chain

A user arriving in the morning.

  • 4768 TGT on a DC, then 4769 as apps open
  • 4624 Type 2 or 7 on the user's workstation
  • 4624 Type 3, Kerberos, AES to servers
  • Occasional human-paced failures that self-correct

Abnormal pattern

Ordinary telemetry, wrong pattern.

  • Fifty 4625 0xC000006A from one IP in 90 seconds
  • 4624 Type 10 to a DC by a service account
  • Run of RC4 4769 against a dozen SPNs

What makes it an incident is the pattern, the rate, and the account and source involved.

Reading logs well is pattern recognition anchored in knowing what normal looks like for your environment.

How attackers abuse authentication

Four techniques, each leaving a fingerprint in the fields you just learned, and the question to ask.

HACKTHESOCLesson 07Authentication and identity30 / 59

Four credential attacks and their fingerprints

TechniqueFingerprint in the logsThe analyst's question
Kerberoasting T1558.003Spike of 4769, 0x17, one account, many distinct high-value SPNsIs RC4 normal here? Why one user, a dozen services?
AS-REP roasting T1558.0044768 for accounts with pre-auth not required, often RC4Which accounts have pre-auth disabled, and why?
Pass-the-hash T1550.0024624 Type 3 or 9, NTLM, privileged account from an odd hostShould this be Kerberos? LSASS access on the source?
Password spraying T1110.003Many 4625 0xC000006A, many users, one source, tight windowHow many users failed? Was there a success after?
Indicators produce alerts; questions produce conclusions. A spray often ends in one 4624 success: that login is the breach. Kerberoasting works because any domain user can request a ticket for any account with an SPN, and service accounts are often over-privileged with rarely changed passwords.
HACKTHESOCLesson 07Authentication and identity31 / 59

Four hunts in SPL, anchored on these fields

  • 1: Kerberoasting, more than 8 RC4 SPNs per account and source
  • 2: AS-REP roasting, Pre_Authentication_Type=0; 3: NTLM by admin-* accounts
  • 4: spray, more than 15 distinct users failing from one source
Thresholds like 8 and 15 are starting points; tune them to your environment. Across all four, ask: given who this account is and where it works, does this authentication make human sense?

MFA: the control that makes a stolen password useless

Passwords get stolen. MFA changes what a stolen password is worth.

HACKTHESOCLesson 07Authentication and identity33 / 59

Two factors of different types, or it is not MFA

Something you know

A password or PIN.

Something you have

A phone with an authenticator app, a hardware token, or a FIDO2 security key.

Something you are

A fingerprint or face (biometric).

A password plus a security question is not MFA: both are something you know, and both are phishable together.

Passwords are phished, dumped from LSASS, cracked from a hash or bought from breach databases. With real MFA the attacker who has the password still hits a wall. It is consistently the highest-impact account security control.
HACKTHESOCLesson 07Authentication and identity34 / 59

Attackers attack the wall itself, and each leaves a signal

MethodHow it worksThe signal
MFA fatigue / push-bombing T1621Attacker has the password and spams approval prompts until the user taps ApproveMany denied prompts, then one approval, often at an odd hour
SIM swap / SMS interceptionVictim's phone number hijacked to receive SMS codesWhy SMS is the weakest factor; FIDO2 resists phishing entirely
Token theftSession cookie stolen after MFA succeeded and replayed: bypass, not breakSame session token from a new device or impossible-travel location
MFA reframes triage. A login with a stolen password may now be 'attempt, blocked by MFA', not 'breach'. Denials then approval, or a post-MFA session moving geography, separate a defeated attack from a successful one.
HACKTHESOCLesson 07Authentication and identity35 / 59

MFA breaks the stolen-credential chain

Kerberos or NTLM? When Windows picks which

Windows chooses automatically by rules, and the choice itself is a detection signal.

HACKTHESOCLesson 07Authentication and identity37 / 59

Windows prefers Kerberos and falls back to NTLM

Kerberos when...NTLM fallback when...
AddressingConnecting by hostname / FQDNConnecting by IP address
AccountsDomain-joined account and service with a registered SPNLocal account, or a workgroup / untrusted domain
InfrastructureA domain controller is reachable to issue ticketsNo DC reachable, or an older/legacy app forces it
Attackers frequently cause NTLM to be used. Knowing the rules tells you when NTLM is expected and when it is a smell.
HACKTHESOCLesson 07Authentication and identity38 / 59

Why forced NTLM matters at the console

  • Connecting by IPAttack tools moving laterally often connect by IP, forcing NTLM. A burst of NTLM between workstations by IP is a lateral-movement smell.
  • No mutual authenticationNTLM lacks it, which makes NTLM relay possible: a victim's authentication relayed to a third system.
  • The hash is the passwordThe NT hash is password-equivalent: replay it to authenticate as the user (pass-the-hash). Captured NetNTLMv1/v2 responses can be cracked offline.
  • Lean on 4776The DC validating an NTLM credential. A rise in 4776 in a Kerberos-first environment is worth a look.
Captured challenge-responses come, for example, from a poisoned LLMNR request. The habit: know normal per host. Servers normally reached by name suddenly seeing NTLM-by-IP, or NTLM between systems with no business talking, are anomalies a baseline makes visible.

Event 4648 and explicit credentials

A process using different credentials than the session it runs in: the 'runas' signal.

HACKTHESOCLesson 07Authentication and identity40 / 59

What 4648 records, and why it punches above its weight

Normal session

Everything you do runs under your own identity; your token flows along automatically. No 4648.

Event ID 4648

Explicit credentials

Something deliberately supplies a different username and password. Legitimate example: an admin using runas, or mapping a drive with alternate credentials.

  • Who was logged on (subject)
  • Which alternate account (target)
  • What system it aimed at
It is one of the quieter events in the Security log, and precisely because it is quiet, it is one of the most useful when it appears where it should not.
HACKTHESOCLesson 07Authentication and identity41 / 59

Why attackers light up 4648, and how to use it

  • Lateral movementTools like PsExec, WMI exec and runas supply explicit credentials to reach the next machine.
  • Credential testingStolen credentials get tested explicitly; 4648 records the attempt whether or not it succeeded.
  • Correlate subject and targetInteresting cases: logged-on user and explicit account differ, especially when the target is privileged.
  • Follow the destinationThe target host is your next pivot in a suspected lateral-movement chain.
A normal user's activity almost never generates 4648. An interactive session that suddenly spawns explicit-credential logons to other hosts is a strong lead.

Forging trust: Golden, Silver tickets and DCSync

Not stealing a crackable ticket, but the key that signs tickets, so tickets are manufactured, never requested.

HACKTHESOCLesson 07Authentication and identity43 / 59

What each technique leaves in the logs at creation

TechniqueForged or stolenKDC contactEvent at creation
DCSync T1003.006Any account's hash, via replicationYes, disguised as replication4662 with the replication GUID, from a non-DC
Golden Ticket T1558.001A TGT signed with the krbtgt hashNo, built offlineNone; first trace is the 4769 when used
Silver Ticket T1558.002A TGS signed with a service account hashNo, built offlineNone; only the target app's own logs
A forged ticket is signed with the same key, so it looks real to anything that only checks the signature. No 4768 is ever generated for a Golden Ticket's creation. A Silver Ticket has no 4769 either, so detection relies on the application showing access with no matching authentication chain on the DC.
HACKTHESOCLesson 07Authentication and identity44 / 59

The keys behind the forgeries

  • krbtgtIts hash signs every TGT in the domain. With it, any user, any groups including Domain Admins, any expiry.
  • Rotate krbtgt twiceIt keeps one generation of history, so rotate twice after any suspected domain compromise.
  • Service account hashA Silver Ticket targets one service, e.g. one SQL Server: stealthier, but limited to that account's access.
  • DCSyncImpersonates a DC over MS-DRSR using GetNCChanges. Needs Replicating Directory Changes and Changes All rights.
Domain Admins hold those replication rights, and misconfigured delegated accounts sometimes do too. DCSync gets hashes without touching LSASS or a DC's disk.
HACKTHESOCLesson 07Authentication and identity45 / 59

The DCSync tell: replication rights from a non-DC

  • SubjectUserName is NOT a domain controller account
  • ObjectType is the Domain-DNS object
  • Properties GUID = Replicating Directory Changes All
Hunt: 4662 with the replication GUID where SubjectUserName is not a known DC computer account. The strongest defense is upstream: protect krbtgt and privileged hashes from ever being extractable.

Kerberos delegation: unconstrained, constrained, RBCD

Letting a service impersonate a user one hop further: powerful by design, dangerous when granted too broadly.

HACKTHESOCLesson 07Authentication and identity47 / 59

Three kinds of delegation, three attack surfaces

TypeWho controls the trustIf compromised
UnconstrainedAny authenticating user's TGT is cached on the server (TRUSTED_FOR_DELEGATION)Harvest cached TGTs, including a Domain Admin's, or coerce a DC
Constrained (S4U2Proxy)The front-end account's msDS-AllowedToDelegateTo listImpersonate any user toward the specific allowed SPNs
Resource-based (RBCD)The target's msDS-AllowedToActOnBehalfOfOtherIdentityAnyone with write access to the target (GenericWrite, or NTLM relay to LDAP) can grant it
Legitimate use: a web front-end querying a back-end SQL database as the logged-in user. The Printer Bug and PetitPotam coerce a DC to authenticate to an unconstrained host, capturing the DC's own TGT.
HACKTHESOCLesson 07Authentication and identity48 / 59

Why delegation turns ordinary users into admins

  • S4U2Self and S4U2ProxyExtensions that let a service get a ticket as a user without their password, then exchange it for a listed target.
  • Protocol transition"Use any authentication protocol" (TRUSTED_TO_AUTH_FOR_DELEGATION): S4U2Self gets a forwardable ticket for any user, Domain Admins included.
  • MachineAccountQuotaAny authenticated user can create up to ten computer accounts. That supplies the delegating account, not the write permission.
  • RBCD needs WRITEWrite rights on the target computer object: a misconfigured ACL (GenericWrite, GenericAll, WriteProperty) or NTLM relayed to LDAP.
  • The chainA user with that write access creates a computer account, sets RBCD on the target, requests a ticket as a Domain Admin to it.
"Kerberos only" needs a forwardable ticket from a user who really authenticated. Under either setting the service class is unprotected, so HTTP/sql01 can be swapped for CIFS/sql01 on the same host. BloodHound often finds the misconfigured ACLs.
HACKTHESOCLesson 07Authentication and identity49 / 59

TransitedServices reveals an S4U2Proxy hop

  • TransitedServices is empty on an ordinary, directly requested ticket
  • Non-empty = an S4U2Proxy delegation chain took place
  • Also note RC4 (0x17) and a requester that is a computer account
Hunt list: TRUSTED_FOR_DELEGATION on anything but a DC is a finding to remediate now. Track changes to msDS-AllowedToActOnBehalfOfOtherIdentity on sensitive servers like a privileged group change.

Beyond the domain: SAML, OAuth/OIDC, Golden SAML

An Identity Provider vouches for users, and many services trust that vouching instead of checking passwords.

HACKTHESOCLesson 07Authentication and identity51 / 59

Federated identity: who vouches and how

Identity Provider

On-prem AD FS or cloud Azure AD/Entra ID vouches for who a user is. Service Providers trust it instead of authenticating users themselves.

SAML 2.0

Older XML assertion, common in enterprise SSO. Signed with the IdP's private token-signing certificate; the SP never re-checks the password.

OAuth 2.0 / OIDC

JSON and token based. OAuth handles delegated authorization ('access my calendar'); OIDC adds authentication ('prove who I am').

The entire SAML trust relationship rests on one signing key never leaving the IdP. Keep that sentence in mind for the next slide.
HACKTHESOCLesson 07Authentication and identity52 / 59

Two ways to skip the login entirely

T1606.002

Golden SAML

Steal the AD FS token-signing key (or its DKM container in AD) and forge assertions offline as any user, including Global Admin. MFA is bypassed entirely.

T1528

Consent phishing

A malicious OAuth app tricks a user into clicking Accept on broad permissions such as Files.Read.All and Mail.Read. No password, no MFA prompt.

The SolarWinds/Sunburst campaign (UNC2452) used stolen AD FS token-signing certificates to move into Microsoft 365 and Azure AD.

MFA is enforced at the authentication step, which Golden SAML never goes through. With consent phishing the user authorized the app themselves, producing a persistent token.
HACKTHESOCLesson 07Authentication and identity53 / 59

The hybrid sync model changes the attack surface

ModelWhere the password check happensWhat an attacker needs to forge SSO

Microsoft recommends PHS as the more resilient default. PTA removes AD FS as a target, but the PTA agent becomes the new one.
HACKTHESOCLesson 07Authentication and identity54 / 59

Detecting forged tokens downstream

  • tokenIssuerType AD FS: a federated sign-in, not PHS or PTA
  • ipAddress does not match the user's normal geography
  • isInteractive false: token presented directly, no fresh MFA
A forged SAML assertion is not a request you can fully capture by itself, so detection relies on the sign-in telemetry it produces. Azure AD Identity Protection ships this named 'Anomalous Token' detection.

Key takeaways

  1. 01AD is the identity backbone: DCs are the top targets; review privileged group changes (4728/4732/4756).
  2. 02Kerberos: TGT is 4768, service ticket is 4769; RC4 (0x17) instead of AES (0x12) is a high-signal Kerberoasting field.
  3. 03NTLM proves identity with the hash itself, so pass-the-hash works; NTLM where Kerberos is expected is a smell.
  4. 04Decode 4624/4625 fast: LogonType 2, 3, 10 and SubStatus 0xC000006A (spray) vs 0xC0000064 (enumeration).
  5. 05Correlate 4768 and 4769 on account, IP and time; a broken chain is pass-the-ticket.
  6. 06Forgery (Golden, Silver, Golden SAML) skips the KDC's logs, so protect krbtgt and signing keys upstream.
