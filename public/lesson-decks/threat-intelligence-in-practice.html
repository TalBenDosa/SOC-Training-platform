Threat intelligence turns a bare indicator on your screen into a decision you can act on with confidence.

Most analysts meet it as a black box: a feed that lights a dashboard red, a VirusTotal tab pasted into out of habit.

ATT&CK describes what an adversary did once caught. Intelligence is knowing about adversaries before, during and after an alert fires.

Three levels: tactical, operational, strategic

Altitude tells you what a piece of intelligence is good for, and what it is not.

HACKTHESOCLesson 19Threat intelligence6 / 87

Two terms that recur throughout

  • AttributionNaming who is behind an activity.
  • Intel feedA continuous stream of threat data, usually indicators, sometimes with context, that your tools check activity against automatically.
IOCs, TTPs and the Pyramid of Pain each get their own treatment later in the lesson.
HACKTHESOCLesson 19Threat intelligence7 / 87

Three altitudes, three audiences

SOC analyst

Tactical

Hashes, IPs, domains, URLs, registry keys, mutexes. "Has this exact artifact been seen before?" Fast to check, fast to expire.

Threat hunter

Operational

A campaign's or actor's typical delivery, persistence and lateral-movement patterns. Lets you hunt before any alert fires.

CISO, leadership

Strategic

Sector targeting, rising techniques, industry risk. "Where should we invest next?" Not day-to-day triage.

Operational example from the lesson: spearphishing with password-protected ZIPs, then a living-off-the-land persistence technique, then lateral movement within 48 hours.
HACKTHESOCLesson 19Threat intelligence8 / 87

One question per altitude, and how fast the answer expires

AltitudeTypical exampleQuestion it answersShelf life
TacticalA file hash or a C2 IP pasted into a lookup toolHas this exact artifact been seen before?Short: a C2 domain rotated weekly is worthless next month
OperationalSpearphish with locked ZIPs, LOTL persistence, lateral move in 48 hHow does this actor or campaign typically operate?Long: behavior changes slowly
StrategicWhich industries ransomware groups are prioritizing this yearWhere should we invest our security budget next?Long horizon: informs budget and policy
LOTL means living off the land: abusing tools already on the system. The rotating C2 domain is the lesson's example of tactical decay: the actor's capability did not change, only the artifact.
HACKTHESOCLesson 19Threat intelligence9 / 87

How operational context changes what one hash means

Same artifact, two very different responses.

  1. 1Tactical matchAn analyst pastes a hash from an alert into a lookup tool and gets a match.
  2. 2Add operational contextIntel ties the hash to a known ransomware affiliate's toolkit, not to an unknown, unattributed sample.
  3. 3The response changesYou now expect that actor's typical next moves, persistence and lateral movement, not just one bad file.
  4. 4Hunt the patternSearch the environment for the campaign's known behavior, even where no IOC matches.
This is why the lesson calls operational intelligence the bridge: it turns a single artifact into an expectation about what else the actor is likely doing.
HACKTHESOCLesson 19Threat intelligence10 / 87

Conflating the levels causes real mistakes

Strategic used as tactical

A trend report has nothing concrete to search for. The result is vague, unfocused hunting.

One IOC read as the full story

Overconfidence that you have seen everything an actor can do, or closing an alert because a single indicator looked weak.

Always ask: what altitude is this, and what is it actually telling me at that altitude?

Knowing a hash belongs to a known ransomware affiliate's toolkit changes your entire response versus an unknown hash.

What each altitude cannot tell you on its own

Tactical alone

An IOC with no operational context says almost nothing about the actor's real capability or what they will do next.

Strategic alone

A trend without tactical grounding gives a hunter nothing concrete to query.

None of the three replaces the others; each answers a different question for a different reader.

Use this as a quick check: when someone hands you intelligence, name its altitude and the altitude it is missing.

IOC vs IOA

The distinction that decides how much to trust an indicator and for how long.

HACKTHESOCLesson 19Threat intelligence15 / 87

What happened versus what they are trying to do

Short shelf life

IOC

Static artifact left by one instance of activity: hash, domain, C2 IP, registry key. One changed byte is a new hash; a new domain costs a few dollars.

Durable

IOA

Behavior revealing intent and technique, independent of tools or infrastructure. Can catch malware never seen before.

  • Code injected into a legit system process
  • Script disables Defender before a payload
  • Kerberoasting-style ticket requests
  • Obfuscated PowerShell after a document opens
A blocklist of last quarter's IOCs catches almost nothing this quarter, even from the same actor using the same approach.
HACKTHESOCLesson 19Threat intelligence16 / 87

Why an IOC goes stale so quickly

  1. 1Recompile, new hashChanging one byte of a malware sample produces a completely different file hash.
  2. 2Re-register, new domainStanding up a new domain costs an adversary a few dollars and a few minutes.
  3. 3The blocklist ages outLast quarter's IOC list catches almost nothing this quarter, even from the same actor using the same approach.

IOC detection is cheap and precise when it hits, but its shelf life is short.

The actor's goal and method did not change in any of these steps. Only the artifact did, which is exactly what an IOC describes.
HACKTHESOCLesson 19Threat intelligence17 / 87

Four IOAs and the telemetry an analyst actually sees

Behavior (IOA)What the attacker is trying to doWhat shows up in your tools
Code injected into a legit system processHide execution inside a trusted processEDR alert on cross-process memory write; Sysmon Event ID 8 (CreateRemoteThread)
Script disables Defender before a payloadRemove the defense that would catch the payloadDefender Operational log Event ID 5001: real-time protection disabled

None of these rows names a hash or a domain. A brand-new sample still has to do one of these things to reach its goal, which is why a behavior rule can catch it.

IOCs are cheap to burn, IOAs are slow to change

Point to the bottom note on the figure: a clean lookup is not an all-clear.

HACKTHESOCLesson 19Threat intelligence20 / 87

The same threat, detected two ways

Bottom of the pyramid

IOC rule

Alert when this exact file hash appears. Precise and cheap, but recompiling the sample defeats it overnight.

Top of the pyramid

IOA rule

Alert when an Office process spawns obfuscated PowerShell. Catches a sample you have never seen, because it must still behave that way.

Use the IOC rule as a fast first pass; build lasting detection on the behavior.

Ask students which rule survives the attacker's next campaign. The answer is the one that targets the TTP, the hardest thing for them to change.
HACKTHESOCLesson 19Threat intelligence21 / 87
An IOC that comes back clean is not an all-clear.

It only means this artifact was not catalogued. Use IOCs as a fast first pass, and judge the behavior.

Build detection strategy and investigative instinct around behavior wherever you can.

Where intel comes from, and how to judge it

The skill is not collecting more sources but evaluating each one honestly.

HACKTHESOCLesson 19Threat intelligence23 / 87

The source landscape

SourceStrengthTrade-off or extra
Commercial feedsCurated, contextualized, actor profilingGeneric, not tuned to your environment
OSINTFree, often hours or days after a campaign emergesQuality varies, no guaranteed upkeep
ISACsFiltered by relevance: your own sector's real threatsMembership and sector-specific
Government advisories (CISA)Authoritative, law-enforcement sourcesComes with concrete detection guidance
DFIR reportsComplete real attack chains, down to command linesSample size of one incident
ISACs are among the highest-value sources precisely because a financial-sector ISAC is more likely to matter to a bank.
HACKTHESOCLesson 19Threat intelligence24 / 87

Two feed entries, run through the four questions

Weak on every question

Entry A

Hash from an automated sandbox detonation nobody reviewed, last validated eight months ago, from a campaign aimed at a different industry and tech stack.

Strong on every question

Entry B

Domain shared in your own sector's ISAC, taken from a member's real incident last week, by a source with a clean track record.

Entry A may be perfectly accurate and still be noise for you. Relevance is the question most often skipped.

Walk the four questions on each entry out loud: derivation, recency, track record, relevance. Entry B is worth an analyst's time; Entry A mostly produces alert fatigue.
HACKTHESOCLesson 19Threat intelligence25 / 87

Four questions before you trust a source

  1. 1How was it derived?Real observed incident, honeypot, or automated sandbox with no human review?
  2. 2How recent?When was it last validated as still active and relevant?
  3. 3Track record?Has this feed produced a lot of noise before?
  4. 4Relevant to us?Does it target our industry, geography or tech stack? The trap that catches experienced analysts.

Accurate but irrelevant intelligence still produces alert fatigue.

Blindly ingesting every IOC from every feed creates matches that were never realistically going to target you.

Section 5 of 14

Enrichment in the investigation

Pull outside context into an artifact, then interpret it correctly.

HACKTHESOCLesson 19Threat intelligence27 / 87

What VirusTotal actually gives you

  1. 1You submit an artifactA file hash, a URL or an IP address from the alert.
  2. 2Dozens of engines answerAntivirus and security vendors each return their own verdict and label for it.
  3. 3A ratio is computedThe headline number is how many engines flagged it out of how many checked it.
  4. 4You read the detailWhich vendors flagged it and what family name they gave matter more than the ratio.
VirusTotal is an aggregator, not a judge. The ratio is a summary of other people's opinions, each with its own blind spots.
HACKTHESOCLesson 19Threat intelligence28 / 87

Reading VirusTotal: quality over ratio

Both halves of the beginner instinct need correcting: high count does not automatically mean malicious, low count does not mean fine.
HACKTHESOCLesson 19Threat intelligence29 / 87

A high count can still be a weak signal

Strong high count

Several reputable engines independently name the same malware family. Their agreement is the signal.

Weak high count

The number is padded by generic heuristic engines with a history of over-flagging. Many votes, little information.

Count the names, not just the votes.

Students who only read the ratio miss this. Open the detection list and look for the same family name from engines you trust.
HACKTHESOCLesson 19Threat intelligence30 / 87

Two more lookups: passive DNS and WHOIS

  • Passive DNSThe historical resolution record of a domain: which IP addresses it pointed to over time, and when.
  • WHOISThe registration record of a domain, including the registrar and the creation date.
  • Why they pair wellAge and resolution history together show whether infrastructure is long-lived or disposable.
Both are fast, cheap checks that work even when no engine has an opinion about the domain yet.
HACKTHESOCLesson 19Threat intelligence31 / 87

Passive DNS and domain age

Looks legitimate

Resolved to the same stable IP for years, with a long registration history.

Looks disposable

Registered days or hours ago and already rotated through several IPs in a short window.

WHOIS registration date is a fast, high-value check: attacker domains are cheap and burned quickly.

Adversaries burn through domains to stay ahead of blocklists, so young is a recurring tell.
HACKTHESOCLesson 19Threat intelligence32 / 87

Three ways a feed match misleads you, and how to check

PatternWhat really happenedHow to check before trusting the match
StaleAccurate when captured, but the infrastructure was since reclaimed or resold for legitimate useWhen was it last validated? Does passive DNS show a new owner?
Shared hosting or CDNThe flagged IP hosts thousands of unrelated domains; only one was ever maliciousWhich hostname did your host actually contact on that IP?
Low-confidence originClassified by an automated sandbox detonation nobody verifiedHow was it derived, and what confidence does the feed give it?

Never let one enrichment source stand alone. Corroborate, and tie it back to the behavior.

Enrichment informs judgment; it does not replace looking at the behavior. Each of these false positives quietly erodes the team's trust in intel.

Outbound HTTPS to 198.51.100.23 right after a user opened an attachment.

Steps 1 and 2: VirusTotal and WHOIS

  • Two reputable engines naming a C2 beacon pattern beats the raw ratio
  • Five days old and privacy-shielded: neither alone proves malice
Plenty of legitimate services use privacy protection. It is the pattern pointing one direction that matters.

Step 3: passive DNS shows rotation

  • Two IP changes in four days in one narrow block: a rotation pattern
  • This corroborates the WHOIS finding rather than repeating it
The opposite of the long, stable history a legitimate service shows.

Three individually inconclusive checks, corroborated and tied to observed behavior, clear the bar for escalation.

No tool declared a verdict. Three independent sources pointed the same way, and they matched what the user actually did: open an attachment.

No single tool declared a verdict. That is enrichment done correctly.
HACKTHESOCLesson 19Threat intelligence38 / 87

The verdict is written in three parts

Fact

Confirmed

Outbound HTTPS to 198.51.100.23 immediately after an email attachment was opened.

Inference

Assessed

Likely C2 beacon activity: engines naming a beacon pattern, a 5-day-old privacy-shielded domain, IP rotation across two addresses in four days.

Gap

Unknown

What the attachment contained, and whether further payload execution occurred.

Keeping fact, inference and gap separate tells the next analyst exactly what to verify first: here, the attachment and any follow-on execution.

Section 7 of 14

The enrichment loop, visually

One tool's output is one data point in a chain, not a final answer.

HACKTHESOCLesson 19Threat intelligence40 / 87

Route every lookup back through the behavior

  1. 1Raw artifactIP, domain or hash from the alert.
  2. 2Check VirusTotalQuality of detections, not just the ratio.
  3. 3Passive DNS and ageResolution history and registration date.
  4. 4CorroborateAcross independent sources.
  5. 5Matches the behavior?Yes: raise confidence. No: inconclusive, keep investigating the behavior.
An inconclusive result is not a dead end and never a reason to close on a technicality.
HACKTHESOCLesson 19Threat intelligence41 / 87

Two wrong ways out of the loop, and the right one

Clean lookup, close

Zero detections read as safe. The behavior around the artifact is never examined.

Red lookup, escalate

A high count read as a verdict, without checking which engines or whether it matches what happened.

Route through behavior

Each lookup raises or lowers confidence in an assessment of what was observed. Inconclusive means keep investigating.

Both wrong exits make the same mistake: letting one tool's output substitute for judgment about the behavior.

Section 8 of 14

Attribution and its limits

Overvalued when chased reflexively, useful only when used for the right purpose.

HACKTHESOCLesson 19Threat intelligence43 / 87

The vocabulary

APT

Advanced persistent threat: sophisticated, well-resourced, often state-sponsored or affiliated, keeps long-term stealthy access.

Threat actor group

Broader: any identified cluster of related activity. Criminal, state-affiliated or hacktivist.

The same group often carries different names across vendors' reporting.

Vendor naming conventions have nothing to do with each other, so one campaign can appear under several aliases.
HACKTHESOCLesson 19Threat intelligence44 / 87

What a confident attribution would actually require

Correlated across multiple incidents, not read off one alert.

  1. 1Technical indicatorsHashes, domains and IPs that overlap with known activity.
  2. 2Infrastructure patternsHow the actor registers, hosts and rotates servers.
  3. 3Tooling choicesWhich malware families and utilities they prefer.
  4. 4Targeting patternsWhich sectors, regions and victims they pursue.
  5. 5Language and timezoneArtifacts that hint at where and when operators work.

Every one of these can be shared, reused or deliberately faked.

Even with all five, the next slide shows why confidence stays limited. An analyst on one alert usually has only the first item.
HACKTHESOCLesson 19Threat intelligence45 / 87

Why attribution is genuinely hard

  • Tooling is reused and shared across groups, sometimes deliberately to muddy attribution.
  • False-flag operations plant another group's indicators to misdirect investigators.
  • Commodity tools and living-off-the-land are used by everyone, from criminals to state actors.
  • Infrastructure is shared, resold and reused across unrelated campaigns, creating misleading overlaps.
Confident attribution needs indicators, infrastructure, tooling, targeting and even timezone artifacts correlated across many incidents.
HACKTHESOCLesson 19Threat intelligence46 / 87

The response plan does not ask for a name

Response stepWhat you doDepends on which group?
ContainIsolate the affected hostsNo
ResetReset the exposed credentialsNo
EradicateRemove the persistence mechanism you foundNo
Close the gapFix the initial-access weaknessNo

Attribution pursued past the evidence is an hour not spent on scoping and containment.

You eradicate what you actually find in your environment, not what a guessed group's past TTP list says should be there.
HACKTHESOCLesson 19Threat intelligence47 / 87

The response rarely depends on the name

Same regardless of group

Contain the hosts, reset exposed credentials, eradicate persistence, close the initial-access gap.

Where TI earns its keep

Operational and strategic intel from ISACs and advisories prioritizes what you check before an alert fires.

"Our sector's ISAC reports a VPN vulnerability exploited by ransomware affiliates": check your exposure now.

Chasing attribution past the evidence is time not spent on scoping, containment and eradication.

Section 9 of 14

The intelligence cycle

Intelligence is produced through a deliberate, repeatable process.

HACKTHESOCLesson 19Threat intelligence49 / 87

Six phases, and the last feeds the first

  1. 1DirectionDefine PIRs (Priority Intelligence Requirements): specific, answerable questions.
  2. 2CollectionFeeds, OSINT, ISACs, advisories, DFIR reports, internal telemetry.
  3. 3ProcessingClean, dedupe, normalize, enrich.
  4. 4AnalysisCorrelate, assign altitude, apply structuring models.
  5. 5DisseminationAnalyst gets an IOC list; CISO gets a risk summary.
  6. 6FeedbackDid it answer the need in time? Flows back into Direction.
PIR example: are we exposed to the VPN vulnerability currently exploited by ransomware affiliates targeting our sector?
HACKTHESOCLesson 19Threat intelligence50 / 87

Two analysts, the same noisy feed

Passive consumer

Tolerates a feed that keeps firing on irrelevant matches, closes each alert, and changes nothing upstream.

Cycle-aware analyst

Reports the noise as Feedback, so Direction can narrow the PIR or drop a source poorly matched to the sector.

A real coverage gap you keep seeing is the next cycle's PIR.

Example gap from the lesson: "we keep getting hit by a technique nobody's PIRs ever asked about." Surfacing that is how a program improves.
HACKTHESOCLesson 19Threat intelligence51 / 87

Why the cycle matters to a working analyst

  • Push feedback upwardA feed that never proves relevant is Feedback-phase data that should reshape Direction.
  • Surface gaps"We keep getting hit by a technique no PIR asks about" becomes the next cycle's requirement.
  • No PIR, no focusMore intelligence without a requirement creates alert fatigue at the program level.
This is what separates passive consumers of intelligence from analysts who shape what their organization receives.

Worked example: from a PIR to a finished assessment

One small piece of work that runs through all six phases of the cycle.

HACKTHESOCLesson 19Threat intelligence53 / 87

A good PIR is narrow enough to know when it is answered

Fails the test

"What are the threats to us?"

Passes the test

"Are threat actors exploiting the remote-access appliance we run, and is our deployment exposed?"

A PIR is a question a decision-maker actually needs answered.

Collection then lists, for each PIR, the sources that could answer it, who checks them and how often.
HACKTHESOCLesson 19Threat intelligence54 / 87

The collection plan includes your own telemetry

Your own VPN logs are often the best source for the exposure half of the question.
HACKTHESOCLesson 19Threat intelligence55 / 87

A finished assessment is built bottom line first

  1. 1Bottom lineThe direct answer to the PIR, with a likelihood word and a stated confidence.
  2. 2EvidenceThe sources behind it, each with its grade, plus what your own telemetry shows.
  3. 3GapsWhat you could not see, so readers know where the judgement is weakest.
  4. 4RecommendedConcrete actions with owners and deadlines: patch, hunt, block.
A CISO may read only the first item. Put the answer where a busy reader will see it.
HACKTHESOCLesson 19Threat intelligence56 / 87

The finished assessment: bottom line, evidence, gaps

Dissemination: the SOC gets the indicators and the hunt request, the CISO gets the three-line bottom line. Feedback asks whether it changed a decision: did the patch go in, did the hunt run?
HACKTHESOCLesson 19Threat intelligence57 / 87

Reading the assessment line by line

Phrase in the assessmentWhat it is doing
"very likely ... (moderate confidence)"Likelihood and confidence stated as two separate judgements
"Two of our three gateways run a vulnerable build"Answers the exposure half of the PIR from our own scan
"212 requests ... all rejected by the gateway"Our own telemetry: attempts seen, no sign of success
"ISAC report of 4 sector victims (source rated B2)"An outside source carried with its Admiralty grade
"GAPS: no visibility ... before 2026-07-15"Says plainly where the evidence stops
Each phrase maps to a habit from the lesson. Students can reuse this structure for any assessment they write.
HACKTHESOCLesson 19Threat intelligence58 / 87

Likelihood and confidence are stated separately

ICD 203 words

Likelihood

How probable the judgement is, on a fixed word scale standardised by US Intelligence Community Directive (ICD) 203.

  • Almost no chance, very unlikely, unlikely
  • Roughly even chance
  • Likely, very likely, almost certain

low, moderate, high

Confidence

How good the evidence behind the judgement is.

"Likely, with moderate confidence" is precise. "We think this is probably bad" is not.

Beginners merge the two. US intelligence practice standardises the likelihood words in ICD 203 so they mean the same thing to every reader.

Section 11 of 14

The Diamond Model of intrusion analysis

Caltagirone, Pendergast and Betz: relationships, not phases.

HACKTHESOCLesson 19Threat intelligence60 / 87

Kill chain and Diamond Model ask different questions

Sequence

Kill chain

Which phase does this event belong to? Describes the order of steps inside one intrusion, with no native way to link separate intrusions.

Relationships

Diamond Model

What connects this event's elements? Its edges let you link events across separate intrusions and organizations.

They are complementary. Each Diamond event even carries its kill-chain phase as a meta-feature.
HACKTHESOCLesson 19Threat intelligence61 / 87

Four corners, and the edges between them

  • AdversaryWho is carrying out the activity.
  • CapabilityMalware family, exploit, or living-off-the-land technique.
  • InfrastructureC2 server, phishing domain, compromised relay.
  • VictimThe person, system or organization targeted.

Meta-features: timestamp, kill-chain phase, result, direction, methodology, resources.

The kill chain describes sequence inside one intrusion; it has no native way to connect across intrusions. The Diamond's edges do.
HACKTHESOCLesson 19Threat intelligence62 / 87

From one event to an activity thread

  1. 1Describe the eventFill the four corners you know: victim, infrastructure, capability, adversary.
  2. 2Pick a known cornerFor example the infrastructure: a specific C2 IP address.
  3. 3Pivot along the edgeAsk which other victims connect to that same infrastructure.
  4. 4Find shared cornersThree incidents at three orgs on one C2 may share an adversary too.
  5. 5Name the threadConnected events form an activity thread: a campaign no single event showed.
None of these steps requires naming the actor. The relationship between infrastructure and victim is itself evidence.
HACKTHESOCLesson 19Threat intelligence63 / 87

A worked pivot from the enrichment example

The relationship between infrastructure and victim is itself evidence, independent of naming the actor.

Grading intel: the Admiralty/NATO code

STANAG 2511: a standard notation in place of "fairly confident".

HACKTHESOCLesson 19Threat intelligence65 / 87
"Fairly confident" means something different to every reader. "B2" means the same thing to everyone trained on the code.

The Admiralty Code came from British naval intelligence, was adopted across NATO and is now borrowed by cyber threat intelligence teams.

That portability is the whole point: the next analyst knows how much weight to give an indicator without re-deriving your reasoning.
HACKTHESOCLesson 19Threat intelligence66 / 87

Two independent axes

GradeSource reliability (track record)Information credibility (this report)
A / 1Completely reliableConfirmed by other independent sources
B / 2Usually reliableProbably true
C / 3Fairly reliablePossibly true
D / 4Not usually reliableDoubtful
E / 5UnreliableImprobable
F / 6Cannot be judgedCannot be judged
A trustworthy source can report something wrong; an unknown source can report something exactly right. That is why the axes are separate.
HACKTHESOCLesson 19Threat intelligence67 / 87

Grading one report, step by step

  1. 1Grade the sourceA commercial feed with a generally solid track record and minor past lapses: B.
  2. 2Grade this reportPlausible, but no second independent source confirms it yet: 2.
  3. 3Combine the twoLetter then number: B2.
  4. 4Write it downRecord "B2" with the IOC in the ticket so the next analyst can weigh it at a glance.
Grade the two axes separately on purpose. A trustworthy source can be wrong this time; an unknown source can be exactly right.
HACKTHESOCLesson 19Threat intelligence68 / 87

Reading a combined rating

B2

Usually reliable source, reporting something probably true but not yet independently confirmed.

F6

Brand-new untested source, information that cannot yet be checked against anything.

Ratings measure epistemic weight, not whether the news is good or bad.

Writing 'this IOC is B2' tells the next analyst exactly how much weight to give it without re-deriving your reasoning.

LabelWho you may share it with

The indicator lifecycle in a TIP

An indicator is not true forever: a list that only grows becomes a false-positive generator.

HACKTHESOCLesson 19Threat intelligence78 / 87

Four properties drive the lifecycle

A TIP such as MISP or OpenCTI exists largely to manage this.

  • ConfidenceSTIX carries 0 to 100; MISP has tags and a threat level. Auto-block only above a high score, send the rest as context.
  • Expiryvalid_until in STIX or a MISP decay model. Days to weeks for shared-hosting IPs, months for domains, longer for hashes.
  • SightingsRecords that an indicator was actually observed. In MISP: a positive observation, a false-positive report or an expiration.
  • False-positive feedbackRecord an FP sighting, lower confidence, or revoke (STIX revoked) so every consuming tool stops alerting.
The IP that hosted a C2 server in August may host a florist's website in October. A TIP that never receives feedback keeps pushing the same bad indicator to every SIEM it feeds.

Above the threshold

Below the threshold

Key takeaways

  1. 01Intelligence has three altitudes: tactical IOCs, operational TTPs for hunting, strategic trends for leadership.
  2. 02IOCs decay fast because they are cheap to change; IOAs describe behavior and are far more durable.
  3. 03Judge every source by derivation, recency, track record and, most often missed, relevance to you.
