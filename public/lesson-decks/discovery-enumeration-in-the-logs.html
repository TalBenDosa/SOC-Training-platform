Before any Kerberoast, DCSync or ransomware run, the attacker asks: what environment did I just land in, and what is worth taking?

There is no 'discovery virus'. There is only a pattern that separates a reconnaissance sweep from a Tuesday-afternoon helpdesk script.

MITRE calls this phase Discovery (TA0007): 'the adversary is trying to figure out your environment'. Almost every command that performs it is an ordinary built-in OS or cloud-API feature, so there is no single malicious event to alert on.

Host-level discovery (T1033)

System Owner/User Discovery: who am I, and who runs this box?

HACKTHESOCLesson 94Discovery in the logs6 / 39

The built-in toolkit, no malware on disk

  • whoami /groupsWho is this account, and which local and domain groups is it in.
  • query user / quserWho else is logged onto this machine right now.
  • net userLists every LOCAL account on the machine.
  • net config workstationBasic host identity and domain membership.
  • %USERNAME%Read by scripts without even spawning a new process.
  • net localgroup administratorsWho is local admin on THIS box: escalate here, or pivot to a better host?
net user <accountname> and net localgroup administrators are the two that actually generate a Windows Security event.
HACKTHESOCLesson 94Discovery in the logs7 / 39

Two events local group checks generate

net user <accountname>

Event 4798

A user's local group membership was enumerated. TargetUserName/TargetSid say whose memberships were read.

net localgroup administrators

Event 4799

A security-enabled local group membership was enumerated. GroupName/GroupDomain say which group was read.

Both carry SubjectUserName/SubjectDomainName (who ran it) and CallerProcessName (which executable).

Same shape of fields; the difference is whether the target is a user or a group.
HACKTHESOCLesson 94Discovery in the logs8 / 39

A 4799 worth a second look

  • Benign callers: mmc.exe snap-ins and dsa.msc used by IT every day
  • The tell: net.exe, cmd.exe or powershell.exe as the caller
  • Stronger after a phishing click or a first-ever logon to this host
Microsoft's own 4798 guidance recommends watching for a Process Name outside the expected admin-tool set or containing an offensive-tooling substring.

A web-server account working through the checklist

  • uid=33 is www-data; auid=4294967295 means no login session
  • Same ppid 21340, 40 seconds apart: one shell spawned by the web server
  • id, uname -a, /etc/passwd, ss -tulpn in two minutes: a web shell
No single uname -a matters. The same short checklist from one web-server account inside two minutes is the shape of someone who just got a shell through the web application.

Domain-wide discovery (T1087.002, T1069.002)

Who exists in the domain, and who actually holds power?

HACKTHESOCLesson 94Discovery in the logs14 / 39

Accounts vs privileged groups

Domain Account Discovery

T1087.002

Every account in the domain, not just the local machine.

  • net user /domain, net group /domain
  • Get-ADUser, Get-ADGroupMember, dsquery
  • AdFind, BloodHound, CrackMapExec

Domain Groups

T1069.002

Aimed at privileged groups: who is a Domain Admin?

  • net group "domain admins" /domain
  • dsquery, Get-ADGroup
  • A direct LDAP query
4662, ACL abuse and SharpHound/PowerView are covered in the companion lesson on LDAP and BloodHound. Do not re-teach them here.
HACKTHESOCLesson 94Discovery in the logs15 / 39

Where the local/domain boundary sits

CommandAsksTelemetry
net localgroup administratorsThis machine's local SAM databaseEvent 4799 on that machine
net group /domainThe Domain Controller, over LDAP or Netlogon/SAM-REntirely different telemetry, on the DC side
The point is recognizing where the boundary is and that the same generic detection principle applies to both.
HACKTHESOCLesson 94Discovery in the logs16 / 39
MITRE's tell for T1087.002: enumeration 'from non-domain-controller or non-admin endpoints'.

The trigger is not the command, it is the source. A marketing laptop should never originate net group "domain admins" /domain. Breadth then raises confidence.

One net user /domain from an unusual workstation is a lead; a new IT hire might run it once. Dozens or hundreds of accounts, or every privileged group in sequence, in a tight window is a signature.

Two close cousins: T1018 and T1046

Named correctly, met in the same investigations.

HACKTHESOCLesson 94Discovery in the logs18 / 39

What machines exist, and what runs on them

Remote System Discovery

T1018

Builds the map of what to pivot to next, feeding Lateral Movement.

  • ping sweeps
  • net view, net view /domain
  • arp -a (local ARP cache)
  • nltest /dclist (which hosts are DCs)

Network Service Discovery

T1046

Which ports are open, which services answer.

  • Port scanning, e.g. nmap
  • SMB enumeration for AD/NetBIOS
  • CrackMapExec
nltest /dclist is an obvious tell of intent: the attacker wants to know where the Domain Controllers are.
HACKTHESOCLesson 94Discovery in the logs19 / 39
The packets look the same. Telling a scan from an attack is an asset and identity management problem.

Nessus, Qualys and Rapid7 sweep the whole range on schedule. nmap-shaped traffic from an unauthorized workstation at 2 AM is the signal.

Know which hosts and service accounts are authorized to scan, and treat everything else as anomalous. This framing returns in section 7.

Share and SAM enumeration, and the hand-off to T1039

Traces on the target as well as the source, and the point where looking turns into taking.

HACKTHESOCLesson 94Discovery in the logs26 / 39

Same shares, different tactics

Discovery, TA0007

T1135

Network Share Discovery over SMB. net view \\remotesystem, net share, macOS sharing -l. Automated by CrackMapExec and PowerView via NetShareEnum.

Collection, TA0009

T1039

Data from Network Shared Drive: reading and harvesting files from shares T1135 found.

The Data Staging and Exfiltration lesson covers the T1039 half in depth. Here the point is the boundary: two different points of attacker commitment, and of defender cost.

What share and SAM enumeration leave on the target

Both run over SMB named pipes, so the queried server records them.

The chain from host recon to theft

  1. 1Host discoverywhoami, net user. T1033.
  2. 2Local group checknet localgroup administrators. Event 4798/4799.
  3. 3Domain enumerationnet user /domain, net group domain admins. T1087.002 / T1069.002.
  4. 4Share discoverynet view, net share. T1135, still Discovery.
  5. 5Mount and copynet use, Robocopy. T1039, now Collection.
  6. 6ExfiltrationThe data leaves.
Stopping net view means stopping them before they've taken anything. Stopping them after Robocopy makes it a breach-notification question.
HACKTHESOCLesson 94Discovery in the logs29 / 39

Correlate the two stages, not either alone

  • Early, cheap signalnet view/net share against multiple hosts, one source, short window.
  • Server-side view5145 srvsvc events from that source across many servers.
  • ConfirmationSame source, minutes later, a burst of large reads off a UNC path like \\fileserver\finance$\....
Neither stage alone is sufficient; the correlation is what shows Discovery became Collection.

Baselining, velocity, and false positives

A single discovery-shaped event is noise. A pattern is the signature.

HACKTHESOCLesson 94Discovery in the logs31 / 39

Count distinct discovery tools per account and host

Sample hit: WKS-0417, d.rosen, 03:10, six tools ending in nltest /dclist:corp and net group "domain admins" /domain from a sales laptop. Next pivot: the parent process and what the account did in the 30 minutes before. Tune the threshold and the allowlist of admin accounts and management-tool parents.
HACKTHESOCLesson 94Discovery in the logs32 / 39

Three questions that turn noise into signal

  1. 1Is it expected?A helpdesk account's nightly net user /domain audit is baseline. A marketing laptop running it once is not.
  2. 2Volume and breadth?A human touches a handful; a tool touches everything on schedule; an attacker bursts dozens to thousands.
  3. 3What surrounds it?After a phishing click or new sign-in? Followed by lateral movement or collection?
Discovery is almost never the incident itself; it is the leading indicator. Isolated and known-good is the bottom of the stack; between a credential alert and file access is the top.

Legitimate sourceWhat looks like Discovery

Alert outside the allowlist, not on the raw pattern

Too broad

Real attacker enumeration hides inside the allowlist.

Right-sized

Specific accounts, hosts and schedules authorized to generate this traffic. Everything else alerts.

Too narrow

The SOC drowns in tickets closing 'the vulnerability scanner did this, again'.

Define normal per identity and host, measure velocity and breadth, and treat Discovery as the leading edge of a story.

This is the single discipline that applies identically from a net localgroup read to a DescribeInstances call.

Key takeaways

  1. 01Discovery (TA0007) is in nearly every intrusion, but uses legitimate built-ins: alert on the pattern, not the command.
  2. 02Host discovery: T1033 (4798/4799, judged by CallerProcessName), T1082, T1016, T1057, T1518.001; on Linux, auditd records.
  3. 03Domain enumeration (T1087.002, T1069.002) from non-DC, non-admin endpoints shows on the target as 5145 samr/srvsvc and 4661.
  4. 04Cloud: T1580, T1526, T1619 in CloudTrail, Graph sweeps in MicrosoftGraphActivityLogs. A burst from one identity is the signal.
  5. 05T1135 (Discovery) feeds T1039 (Collection): catching the sweep is far cheaper than catching the copy.
  6. 06Count distinct discovery tools per account and host, then alert on what falls outside an authorized allowlist.
