"Brute force" is one word doing the work of four techniques that leave completely different evidence.

Triage a low-and-slow spray as brute force and you look for a burst against one username, and miss the wide, shallow fan-out.

Ask the class to name a credential attack. Most will say brute force. This lesson is about why that single word is not enough.

Section 1 of 13

What a credential attack actually is

Credentials, hashes and tokens, then the four techniques in one line each.

HACKTHESOCLesson 27Credential attacks6 / 56

Three things an attacker can steal or abuse

The front-door key

Credential

Anything that proves who you are: usually a username plus a secret such as a password, key or physical token.

Shredded paper

Hash

One-way scramble of the password. The system compares hashes. Some protocols accept a stolen hash directly: pass-the-hash.

Proof you logged in

Token

A session cookie or Kerberos ticket. Whoever holds it is treated as the user, password or not.

Stolen tokens matter as much as stolen passwords, sometimes more. Plant this now; sections 9 and 10 build on it.
HACKTHESOCLesson 27Credential attacks7 / 56

The four techniques in one line each

TechniqueWhat the attacker does
Brute forceMany passwords against one account, until one works or the account locks
Password sprayingOne or two passwords against many accounts, to stay under each account's lockout limit
Credential stuffingReplays passwords from a different company's breach, betting on password reuse
Credential theftNo guessing: lifts the password, hash or token from memory, a browser store or a keylogger
The rest of the lesson is each of these four rows in detail, with the exact log evidence each one leaves.

Section 2 of 13

Brute force: many passwords, one account

T1110.001 Password Guessing: the noisiest technique, and usually the least effective online.

HACKTHESOCLesson 27Credential attacks9 / 56

Where brute force still works

Offline against a hash

After exfiltrating NTDS.dit or dumping LSASS, billions of guesses per second on the attacker's own hardware, with no lockout counter watching.

Where lockout is weak

Legacy auth endpoints, some VPN concentrators, exposed RDP and SSH with a misconfigured or absent lockout policy.

Against an organisation with a sane lockout policy, pure online brute force against internet-facing logins is rare in real intrusions.
HACKTHESOCLesson 27Credential attacks10 / 56

The online signature: many 4625s, one account

  • 0xC000006A = bad password; 0xC0000064 = unknown username
  • Consistent LogonType: 3 for network, 10 for RDP
  • In SigninLogs: repeated ResultType failures for one UserPrincipalName
Once the attacker has confirmed the account exists, failures switch to bad-password status. This is the easiest of the four patterns to alert on because it concentrates on one identity.
HACKTHESOCLesson 27Credential attacks11 / 56

Three questions when you see it

  1. 1Did the account lock out?Event ID 4740 timestamp tells you how many attempts the attacker was willing to burn.
  2. 2Internal or external source?Internal changes this to a possible compromised-host pivot or an insider.
  3. 3Did any attempt succeed?One 4624 followed by unusual activity is no longer a failed attack: it is an active compromise.
Ask them in this order.
HACKTHESOCLesson 27Credential attacks12 / 56

The benign twin: stale cached credentials

A phone, mapped drive or scheduled task still holding the old password retries forever.

ClueStale credentialBrute force
RhythmMetronomic, every few minutes, for daysBursty, stops on success or lockout
SourceAn asset the user owns that you can identifyUnknown source
Status codeOnly bad-password 0xC000006AA username list also gives 0xC0000064
TriggerStarts right after a password change (4723 / 4724)No link to a reset
The most common cause of many failures for one account from one source in a real SOC is not an attacker. It still deserves a service-desk ticket, just not an incident.

Section 3 of 13

Password spraying: one password, many accounts

T1110.003: engineered to look unremarkable at the account level.

HACKTHESOCLesson 27Credential attacks14 / 56
The lockout counter is per account. One bad password to each of two thousand accounts never locks anything.

Passwords like Summer2026! or Welcome1 against every account, wait, then the next password. Sometimes spread over 24 hours.

Spraying is the technique of choice against Office 365, VPN portals and Citrix gateways, where firstname.lastname@company.com is trivial to build from LinkedIn.
HACKTHESOCLesson 27Credential attacks15 / 56

Group by source, count distinct accounts

  • Detection that groups by account will always miss spraying
  • Attackers often rotate through a botnet or residential proxy pool
  • Lockout policy is a partial mitigation, not a detection control
A source IP or ASN that touched 400 usernames in an hour with a low failure count per username is a spray candidate, even if no account crossed a threshold.
HACKTHESOCLesson 27Credential attacks16 / 56

The benign twin: everyone behind one address

Looks like a spray

Anything that authenticates for many people from one address.

  • Corporate NAT or VPN egress
  • Monday after a mass password expiry
  • Mail server, MDM or SSO connector
  • App retrying a stale service password

Three checks

A spray hits accounts that never existed and moves as a wave. Your gateway only fails for real employees.

  • Is the source one of your egress ranges?
  • Do the target accounts exist?
  • Is one password moving across the list?
Checking the source against your own egress ranges resolves most of these alerts on its own.

Worked example: spray vs brute force

Two real artifacts from the same hour, read side by side.

HACKTHESOCLesson 27Credential attacks18 / 56

Depth without breadth versus breadth without depth

Brute force, T1110.001

Artifact 1: Event 4625

j.mitchell from 203.0.113.77, LogonType 3, SubStatus 0xC000006A. 47 events between 02:14:03 and 02:16:41.

Spraying, T1110.003

Artifact 2: SigninLogs

198.51.100.9, ResultType 50126, 210 distinct UserPrincipalName values, Office 365 Exchange Online, 1-2 attempts per account.

0xC000006A on every failure means the attacker already knew j.mitchell exists. 50126 is Azure AD's generic invalid-credentials result.
HACKTHESOCLesson 27Credential attacks19 / 56
Before you can name the technique, you have to know which axis you are counting on.

Group Artifact 2 by account and every account shows 1-2 failures: nothing. Pivot to distinct accounts per source and the spray appears.

That single pivot is the difference between catching a spray campaign and letting it sail past your detections.

Section 5 of 13

Credential stuffing: never guessed

T1110.004: known-correct passwords from an unrelated breach, betting on reuse.

HACKTHESOCLesson 27Credential attacks21 / 56

What stuffing looks like in the logs

  • First-try successCompromised accounts show no failed-attempt trail before the successful logon. It looks like a normal login.
  • Volume across accountsMany attempts against a large distinct set of accounts that did not match.
  • Automation tellsTools like OpenBullit and Sentry MBA leave telltale User-Agents or unusually consistent timing.
  • Odd originsDatacenter or residential-proxy ranges, geographically inconsistent with the real owner.
  • Identity Protection'Anonymous IP address', 'unfamiliar sign-in properties' and 'leaked credentials' risk detections.
Microsoft matches passwords against its own feed of breach dumps and can flag a sign-in purely because the password is a known-leaked pair.
HACKTHESOCLesson 27Credential attacks22 / 56

High volume, many accounts, low success rate

  • MFA is the single most effective mitigation: a stuffed password alone is worthless
  • MFA does nothing for exempted accounts, legacy auth, or unenrolled service identities
The mitigation for stuffing is not a detection rule at all, which is why section 8 is precise about MFA's limits.

Section 6 of 13

Credential theft: no guessing at all

T1003 and T1555: the password, hash or token is taken from where it is stored.

HACKTHESOCLesson 27Credential attacks24 / 56

The log signature is what tells them apart

Conflating theft with the first three is the most consequential junior mistake, because the response is completely different.
HACKTHESOCLesson 27Credential attacks25 / 56

LSASS memory access in Sysmon Event ID 10

  • 0x1FFFFF + dbghelp.dll / dbgcore.dll CallTrace: a minidump-style dumper
  • Direct memory readers like Mimikatz sekurlsa ask for a narrow 0x1010
  • Neither mask is malicious alone: the signal is a reader not on your allow-list
AV and EDR products open LSASS too. What matters here is the source: an unsigned binary in a user-writable folder such as C:\Users\Public. LSASS can yield NTLM hashes, Kerberos tickets and WDigest cleartext.
HACKTHESOCLesson 27Credential attacks26 / 56

Infostealers, and why the usual defences fail

T1555 password stores

RedLine, Raccoon or Lumma read Chrome or Edge's Login Data SQLite database, decrypt it with the user's DPAPI key, and take saved session cookies too.

What does not help

None of the defences against the first three apply.

  • Lockout: no password is guessed
  • Rate limiting: maybe one normal logon
  • MFA: fails against session-cookie theft

Where to look

Endpoint telemetry: LSASS access, unexpected reads of browser credential stores, infostealer-like processes. The later logon looks normal.

One infostealer infection can yield dozens of working logins across banking, SSO and personal email, which is why those logs sell on criminal marketplaces.

Which technique is it? A fast checklist

Four questions, under a minute, before you write a single query.

HACKTHESOCLesson 27Credential attacks28 / 56

Ask these four questions in order

  1. 1Failed trail before success?A success with no failures at all points to stuffing or theft.
  2. 2How many accounts, sources?Many failures on one account = brute force. Few per account across many = spraying.
  3. 3Usernames that don't exist?0xC0000064 means a guessed list. Legitimate infrastructure only fails for real employees.
  4. 4Any authentication event?An unexpected process opening lsass.exe (0x1010, or 0x1FFFFF via dbghelp)? Endpoint telemetry: theft.
This habit is the difference between opening the right playbook immediately and spending twenty minutes chasing the wrong pattern.

Section 8 of 13

MFA, lockout and service accounts

Where the controls hold, and exactly where they break.

HACKTHESOCLesson 27Credential attacks31 / 56

Lockout policy cuts both ways

Holds

Locking after 5 or 10 bad passwords in 15 or 30 minutes completely defeats naive brute force.

Fails

Does very little against spraying, which is engineered never to cross the threshold on any single account.

Backfires

Too tight becomes a DoS tool: lock every executive out, flood the help desk, pressure an emergency reset without verification.

In some documented intrusions the lockout flood was used deliberately as cover or as pressure, which is exactly the opening social engineering needs.
HACKTHESOCLesson 27Credential attacks32 / 56

MFA stops three techniques, with three precise gaps

A correctly enforced MFA policy stops brute force, spraying and stuffing. It is not a binary safe switch.

  • Legacy authenticationPOP3, IMAP, older SMTP AUTH and some older ActiveSync clients often cannot do MFA. Spray and stuffing tools probe for them.
  • MFA fatiguePush-bombing does not break MFA technically; it wears down the human until they tap approve.
  • Stolen session tokenMFA protects the login event. A stolen authenticated cookie never passes through it.
Memorise these three gaps precisely. They come back in sections 9 and 10.
HACKTHESOCLesson 27Credential attacks33 / 56

Service accounts: the softest target

  • Often excluded from MFA: no human present to approve
  • Passwords set once and never rotated; often exempt from lockout
  • Broad permissions: one can be worth more than a hundred users
A spray hit or failures against an account named svc, sql or backup is a higher-severity finding than the same pattern on a human user, not a lower one.

Session and token theft

T1539 and T1550.004: when there is no password to steal.

HACKTHESOCLesson 27Credential attacks35 / 56
The password is the key to the door. The session token is the wristband you get after the bouncer checks your ID.

Pass-the-cookie: plant a stolen cookie and land inside with no password prompt and no MFA prompt. The auth log looks clean.

Tokens are stolen by infostealers reading the cookie store, by AiTM phishing in transit, or by a malicious OAuth app granted a long-lived refresh token.
HACKTHESOCLesson 27Credential attacks36 / 56

You cannot see the theft, only the mismatch

SignalWhy it indicates a stolen session
Same session from two distant IPs or countries within minutesOne session cannot physically be in two places
Sign-in from a hosting ASN while the user's device is elsewhereReplayed tokens come from attacker infrastructure
Success with no preceding interactive MFA event for that deviceThe token skipped the login flow entirely
New inbox rule, OAuth consent, new MFA method, mass mailbox readWhat a hijacked session does right after landing
When a login looks perfectly normal but the behaviour after it does not, stop looking at auth logs and look at what the session did.
HACKTHESOCLesson 27Credential attacks37 / 56

One session token replayed from multiple IPs

This is impossible travel on a single session. Cross-reference the Impossible Travel lesson.

MFA attacks: fatigue and AiTM

T1621 and T1557: the attacker already holds the password and goes after the second factor.

HACKTHESOCLesson 27Credential attacks39 / 56

MFA fatigue: a burst of denied or incomplete prompts

Dozens of prompts, often at 3 a.m., until the user taps approve. This is how several high-profile 2022 intrusions defeated otherwise solid MFA. Number matching is the defence.
HACKTHESOCLesson 27Credential attacks40 / 56

How adversary-in-the-middle phishing works

  1. 1Victim clicks a phishing linkA reverse proxy (Evilginx, EvilProxy, Tycoon2FA) relays the real login page.
  2. 2Victim signs in for realCorrect password and MFA, number matching included, against the real identity provider.
  3. 3Proxy captures the cookieThe post-MFA session cookie passes through the proxy to the attacker.
  4. 4Attacker replays itPass-the-cookie. The sign-in event on the identity provider is clean.
There is no failed logon and no denied MFA. As with token theft, detection is post-authentication behaviour.
HACKTHESOCLesson 27Credential attacks41 / 56

Number matching does not stop AiTM

MFA attackMechanismWhat stops it
Fatigue / push-bombing (T1621)Spam approval prompts until the user cavesNumber matching; prompt rate-limiting
AiTM phishing (T1557 + T1539)Proxy relays the real login, steals the post-MFA cookiePhishing-resistant MFA (FIDO2/passkeys, certificate-based)
Phishing-resistant MFA is bound to the real site's origin, so it will not release to a look-alike domain. Hunt the aftermath: unfamiliar ASN after a phish, new device, inbox rule, OAuth consent.

Section 11 of 13

Kerberoasting and AS-REP roasting

T1558.003 and T1558.004: attacking Kerberos for crackable service-account passwords.

HACKTHESOCLesson 27Credential attacks43 / 56

Two roasting techniques

T1558.003, Event 4769

Kerberoasting

Any domain user can request a TGS for any SPN. The ticket is encrypted with the service account's hash, so it is cracked offline with no lockout.

T1558.004, Event 4768

AS-REP roasting

Accounts set to not require Kerberos pre-authentication return an AS-REP without the password. It is cracked offline.

Requesting a service ticket is normal Kerberos, not a vulnerability. Weak, never-rotated service passwords often crack in hours.
HACKTHESOCLesson 27Credential attacks44 / 56

RC4 service tickets across many SPNs from one account

  • Cracking tools ask for RC4 (0x17) because it cracks far faster than AES
  • In a domain that should use AES, an RC4 request is itself suspicious
The single highest-fidelity Kerberoasting detection is not a threshold at all but a honeypot SPN, covered in section 13.

What NIST SP 800-63B recommends

Modern password policy overturned decades of advice that helped attackers.

HACKTHESOCLesson 27Credential attacks46 / 56
Complexity rules plus 90-day rotation manufactured exactly the passwords sprays feed on.

Predictable patterns like Summer2026!, and incremental changes like Password1 becoming Password2.

The old rules also trained users to write their passwords down.
HACKTHESOCLesson 27Credential attacks47 / 56

The guidance and why

GuidanceRationale
Length over complexity: min 8, recommend 15, support 64Length resists guessing far more than symbol-juggling
No mandated composition rulesForced mixes drive predictable patterns without real strength
No periodic rotation; change on evidence of compromiseScheduled rotation drives weak, incremental passwords
Screen against a breach/blocklistA known-leaked password cannot be set: defeats stuffing at creation
Allow all printable characters, spaces and pasteSo password managers and passphrases work
Rate-limit failed attemptsMitigates online brute force without complexity theatre
A breached-password screen prevents an attack that never becomes an alert. That is why the same stuffing campaign lights up one organisation and bounces off another.

Deception: honeytokens and honey accounts

Detections with (almost) no false positives.

HACKTHESOCLesson 27Credential attacks50 / 56

Decoys that catch credential attacks

DecoyHow it catches an attacker
Honey account, e.g. svc_backup_admAny 4624/4625 attempt means someone enumerated and is trying decoy credentials
Honeypot SPNAny 4769 for it is Kerberoasting in progress
Planted credentials in files or configsUse of them means an attacker dumped and is replaying credentials
Canary tokens: fake cloud key, beaconing doc, decoy DB rowFire the instant an attacker uses stolen data
Nothing benign should ever touch a honeytoken, so any interaction is malicious by definition. You skip triage and go straight to response.
HACKTHESOCLesson 27Credential attacks51 / 56

Any activity on a decoy account is an alert

  • Late in the attack, but near-certain: the complement to noisy early detections
Make decoys believable, monitored, unreachable by legitimate automation, and documented for the SOC. Not HONEYPOT_DO_NOT_USE.

Key takeaways

  1. 01Brute force is many passwords on one account; spraying is one password on many. Detect spraying by source, not account.
  2. 02Stuffing replays breached pairs: the tell is first-attempt success with no failed-attempt trail.
  3. 03Theft (T1003, T1555) has no guessing; LSASS dumps and stolen cookies bypass lockout and rate limiting.
  4. 04MFA stops three techniques but not legacy auth, push fatigue, or a stolen session token; AiTM beats number matching.
  5. 05Service accounts are the favourite target: often outside MFA and lockout, and over-privileged.

Check your understanding, question 1 of 3

A hosting IP outside your egress ranges fails once (ResultType 50126) against each of 340 distinct UserPrincipalName values in one hour, no successes. Ninety minutes later it fails once more against the same 340. What is this?

One failure per account, a pause, then another single pass over the same list is spraying, kept under lockout and visible only grouped by source IP. Brute force piles failures on one account; stuffing shows first-try successes; the IP is not your egress.

Check your understanding, question 2 of 3

A user has one successful sign-in, correct password on the first try, from a new residential IP that tried no other account. The same day Identity Protection raises 'leaked credentials' for the user. Which technique?

No failed-attempt trail plus a leaked-credentials detection is stuffing: the attacker already held a working password. Spraying would hit other accounts, brute force leaves failures, and a replayed cookie skips the password prompt entirely.

Check your understanding, question 3 of 3

After a phishing click, a user signs in: correct password, one MFA push approved with number matching. Minutes later a hosting IP reads the mailbox with the same SessionId; no sign-in is logged from that IP. Best explanation?

In AiTM the user genuinely completes password and number-matching MFA while a reverse proxy keeps the session cookie for replay (T1557 + T1539), so no attacker sign-in is logged. Fatigue needs a burst of pushes; IMAP and stuffing would log a sign-in.
