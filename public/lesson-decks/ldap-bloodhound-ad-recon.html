Before any Kerberoast, DCSync, or lateral move, the attacker asks one question: who has power over what?

Active Directory answers it freely over LDAP, and tools turn the answers into a graph of attack paths. Your job is to see that reading happen.

Section 1 of 10

The openness LDAP recon exploits

Most of the directory is readable by any authenticated user, by design. That is what recon reuses.

HACKTHESOCLesson 41LDAP and BloodHound6 / 59
By design, most of the directory is readable by any authenticated domain user.

The same rule that lets a helpdesk app look up your manager lets recon list Domain Admins, SPN-carrying accounts, and who holds dangerous rights, exploiting nothing.

LDAP (Lightweight Directory Access Protocol) is the language programs use to query AD, over TCP 389 (cleartext/STARTTLS) or 636 (LDAPS). Detection cannot rely on one query looking bad: reading all of this is cheap and permitted.
HACKTHESOCLesson 41LDAP and BloodHound7 / 59

SID and ACL: the two things recon reads

SID

A unique identifier like S-1-5-21-...-512. The trailing RID names well-known accounts: 500 is Administrator, 512 is Domain Admins. Lets a reader find the crown jewels by number.

ACL

A per-object list of who may read it, modify it, reset its password, or add members. Where unintended privilege hides, and the half most monitoring ignores.

A right granted once for one legitimate reason is rarely revisited, which is exactly where non-obvious escalation lives.

SID = Security Identifier. ACL = Access Control List. The point here is only that both are cheap to read; the wire mechanics come in section 4.
HACKTHESOCLesson 41LDAP and BloodHound8 / 59

Readable by design: same data, two very different readers

Every field recon wants has a legitimate daily reader, which is why volume and breadth, not the field, decide.

What is readBenign readerWhy recon wants it
Group membershipGPO processing, HR sync, an app resolving rolesFind who sits in Domain Admins and nested chains
servicePrincipalNameA service locating its own or a peer SPNA ready-made Kerberoasting target list in one filter
nTSecurityDescriptorA management tool showing an object's permissionsEnumerate GenericAll and ForceChangePassword edges
Drive home the core defensive idea early: you are never alarmed by a field being read, only by one principal reading far more of it, far wider, than its peers.

Section 2 of 10

Why recon comes first

AD compromise is relationship-driven, and relationships are readable, so mapping precedes every attack.

HACKTHESOCLesson 41LDAP and BloodHound10 / 59

Each later attack needs a fact recon provides

  • KerberoastingWhich accounts have SPNs (service principal names).
  • AS-REP RoastingWhich accounts have Kerberos pre-authentication disabled.
  • DCSyncWho holds the directory replication rights.
  • Privilege escalationWhich low-privilege account can reset a high-privilege account's password.

All discoverable by reading AD, not attacking it. Catch the reading and you catch the whole chain early.

This is why recon detection has outsized value: it is the one stage common to every AD attack, and the quietest to run.
HACKTHESOCLesson 41LDAP and BloodHound11 / 59
The attacker's mental model is a graph: principals are nodes, rights are edges.

Edges like MemberOf, AdminTo, GenericAll, ForceChangePassword. The job is a path from a low-privilege node to Domain Admins. A defender who thinks the same way finds the path first.

The graph, not brute force, is what makes modern AD compromise fast and reliable. Reframe it: the graph is also the defender's remediation map.

How a sweep looks in Event 1644 LDAP-search logging

With the diagnostic enabled, the DC records the client, the filter, and the size of each search.

  • A subtree (objectClass=*) from the domain root pulls the whole directory
  • One client, one search, tens of thousands of entries: the breadth tell
Enable the '15 Field Engineering' diagnostic so the DC logs Event 1644 at all. This is defender telemetry, not a command: it is what the sweep leaves behind.
HACKTHESOCLesson 41LDAP and BloodHound16 / 59

Benign LDAP versus a collection sweep

Same protocol, opposite shape. Baseline the left so the right stands out.

Line-of-business app

A known service account, steady low volume, predictable filters an app developer wrote once.

  • Narrow filter: one user or one group
  • base or one scope, few entries
  • Repeats the same small query all day

Collector

One principal reading users, groups and security descriptors in minutes, from a host that never did before.

  • Wildcard (objectClass=*) subtree sweep
  • Thousands of entries in one burst
  • SPN, UAC-bitwise and ACL filters together

The filter alone is never the verdict: an admin tool can issue a broad query too. Volume plus breadth plus a new source is.

UAC = userAccountControl. Stress that the honest read on the left is why you tune on volume and novelty, not on the presence of a wildcard.

LDAP under the hood

Ports, binds, scopes, and the Global Catalog, so you know exactly which traffic to hunt.

HACKTHESOCLesson 41LDAP and BloodHound19 / 59

The four LDAP ports

PortProtocolScope and note for the hunter
389LDAP (cleartext or STARTTLS)This domain partition; creds sniffable if the bind is unencrypted
636LDAPS (over TLS)This domain partition, encrypted from the first byte
3268Global CatalogWhole forest, partial attributes: a collector favourite
3269Global Catalog over SSLWhole forest, encrypted
A single GC query on 3268 returns a partial view of every object in the entire forest, so one foothold enumerates across domain boundaries without touching each DC. Watch 3268 from non-app hosts.
HACKTHESOCLesson 41LDAP and BloodHound20 / 59

Binds and scopes: proving who you are, and how much you ask

Bind types

A simple bind on 389 leaks the password to a sniffer, which is why apps should use LDAPS.

  • Anonymous: no creds; often restricted
  • Simple: user + password, cleartext on 389
  • SASL (Kerberos/NTLM): the legitimate path

Search scope

Collectors use subtree searches from the domain root, reading thousands of objects at once.

  • base: that one object only
  • one: its immediate children
  • sub: the base and everything beneath

Cleartext simple binds, anonymous binds, GC subtree sweeps and wildcard filters are rare for apps, constant for a collector.

SASL = Simple Authentication and Security Layer. Tie each exotic option back to a hunt: anonymous binds and cleartext simple binds each deserve their own alert.
HACKTHESOCLesson 41LDAP and BloodHound21 / 59
The subtree sweep from the domain root is the single most telling LDAP shape.

A sub scope over (objectClass=*) starting at DC=corp,DC=local asks for everything. Almost no application needs that; a collector cannot work without it.

Use this breather to fix the one shape worth memorising. Everything in the detection sections is a way of catching this pattern on different channels.

Section 5 of 10

The attack-path graph

Collected AD data becomes a graph; shortest-path queries turn relationships into a route to Domain Admins.

HACKTHESOCLesson 41LDAP and BloodHound23 / 59

Two halves of the graph tooling

The collector

Runs inside the domain and reads AD: objects, memberships, ACLs, sessions, delegation. This is the half that makes the telemetry you hunt (1644, 4662, 5145). Its noise level depends on which methods it uses.

The graph database

Ingests the collected data into a graph (nodes = principals, edges = rights) and answers shortest-path questions like 'how do I reach Domain Admins'. It runs offline, so it produces no telemetry on your network.

Only the collection half touches your environment, so that is where detection lives.

Keep it conceptual and defender-side: you detect the collector reading AD, never the offline graphing step. Naming tools is unnecessary here.
HACKTHESOCLesson 41LDAP and BloodHound24 / 59

Collection breadth tells you how aggressive the sweep was

The methods a collector uses decide which telemetry fires, and how loud it is.

Method classWhere it reachesTelemetry it produces
DC-only (LDAP)Domain controllers only1644 LDAP searches, 4662 object reads; quiet
Default (LDAP + host)Every reachable host over SMB/RPCAdds 5145 and 4624 Type 3 fan-out; noisy
All / GPO local groupHosts plus GPO-assigned local adminWidest footprint across both channels
Reading the breadth from the telemetry tells you whether the operator was trying to stay quiet (DC-only) or grabbing everything (Default/All). That shapes your urgency.
HACKTHESOCLesson 41LDAP and BloodHound25 / 59

What a collector gathers from AD

  • Domain objectsUsers, groups, computers, OUs, GPOs, trusts.
  • Group membershipsIncluding nested chains, resolved recursively.
  • ACLs and object rightsGenericAll, WriteDACL, AddMember: the escalation edges.
  • SessionsWhich users are logged on where, exposing credentials.
  • Local accessAdminTo, CanRDP, ExecuteDCOM on each host.
  • DelegationConstrained and unconstrained delegation settings.
Sessions and local access are the host-based half: they need SMB/RPC reach, which is why Default/All collection lights up 5145 and 4624 and a DC-only hunt misses them.
HACKTHESOCLesson 41LDAP and BloodHound26 / 59
Transitive pathfinding is the power, and it cuts both ways.

Chaining ForceChangePassword, MemberOf and AdminTo walks a helpdesk account to a DC in seconds. Run the same graph defensively and you see the path first, and can cut it.

This sets up section 10: the graph is a remediation tool. Any path an attacker's graph finds, a defender's graph finds first.

The attack-path edges you must recognise

Each edge is a concrete AD right. Knowing them lets you read a path and pick the link to remove.

HACKTHESOCLesson 41LDAP and BloodHound28 / 59

DACL and object-control edges: the escalation workhorses

EdgeMeaningWhy it is dangerous
GenericAllFull control over the objectReset password, set an SPN, add a shadow credential
GenericWriteWrite any non-protected attributeSet an SPN (Kerberoast) or the preauth flag (AS-REP roast)
WriteDACL / WriteOwnerRewrite permissions, or become ownerGrant yourself GenericAll, then escalate
ForceChangePasswordReset the password without the old oneTake over the account and log in as it
AddKeyCredentialLinkWrite msDS-KeyCredentialLinkShadow Credentials: impersonate via PKINIT
GenericWrite is the common targeted pivot: write an SPN for a Kerberoast or the DONT_REQ_PREAUTH flag for a targeted AS-REP roast. DACL = Discretionary Access Control List.
HACKTHESOCLesson 41LDAP and BloodHound29 / 59
An attack path is just these edges chained, so the defender's job is to find the one unnecessary edge and cut it.

No single edge is an alarm; the chain is the attack. Remove one link, usually a stray GenericAll or ForceChangePassword, and every path through it breaks.

Placed between the two edge tables so students read the second already knowing the goal is not to memorise edges but to spot the removable one.
HACKTHESOCLesson 41LDAP and BloodHound30 / 59

Replication, delegation, and access edges

EdgeMeaningWhy it is dangerous
GetChanges + GetChangesAllThe two replication rights on the domainTogether they enable DCSync, stealing krbtgt
AllowedToDelegate / AllowedToActConstrained / resource-based delegationImpersonate users to a service (S4U abuse)
AdminToLocal admin on the target computerDump LSASS there for cached credentials
HasSessionUser has a live session on that hostIf you are admin there, steal their credentials
CanRDP / ExecuteDCOMRDP or DCOM access to the targetInteractive foothold or remote code execution
GetChanges + GetChangesAll held together by a non-DC principal is a red flag on its own: outside DCs and a few sync accounts, nothing legitimate needs both.
HACKTHESOCLesson 41LDAP and BloodHound31 / 59

How a defender cuts each class of edge

Object-control (ACL)

GenericAll, WriteDACL, ForceChangePassword: strip the delegated right from the principal that should never have had it. One ACL change breaks every path through it.

Replication

GetChanges + GetChangesAll: audit who holds both on the domain head and remove it from anything that is not a DC or an approved sync account.

Host and session

AdminTo, HasSession: enforce tiering so privileged sessions never land on low-tier hosts, and trim local-admin group membership.

Every cut is surgical and reversible: you remove a grant, you do not rebuild the directory.

This turns the edge catalog into action: for each edge an analyst reads in a graph, they now know the specific control that removes it.

Worked example: a path to Domain Admins

Four ordinary facts strung into one devastating outcome, read edge by edge.

HACKTHESOCLesson 41LDAP and BloodHound33 / 59

Reading the shortest path edge by edge

  1. 1The pathsvc-helpdesk02 -[ForceChangePassword]-> it-support-lead -[MemberOf]-> IT-ADMINS -[AdminTo]-> DC01.
  2. 2ForceChangePasswordHelpdesk was delegated the right to reset it-support-lead's password without knowing the old one.
  3. 3MemberOfit-support-lead belongs to the IT-ADMINS group.
  4. 4AdminToIT-ADMINS has local admin on DC01, a Domain Controller.
No step is alarming alone: helpdesk resets passwords, memberships are normal, admin groups administer servers. The chain is the attack.
HACKTHESOCLesson 41LDAP and BloodHound34 / 59

Each edge has an innocent reason, and that is the trap

Why nothing in the path trips a per-event rule, and what actually makes it dangerous.

EdgeInnocent reason it existsWhat makes the chain dangerous
ForceChangePasswordHelpdesk resets forgotten passwords all dayThe target is not an ordinary user but a group member with admin reach
MemberOfPeople belong to teams and role groupsThe group quietly carries local admin on a DC
AdminToAdmin groups must administer their serversThat server is a Domain Controller, so admin = domain
This is the heart of the lesson: you cannot alert on any one edge. You find the chain by graphing, then you judge the weakest, most needless link.
HACKTHESOCLesson 41LDAP and BloodHound35 / 59
Reset the password, log in, and you land with local admin on a Domain Controller.

A direct route to dumping credentials or running DCSync as a fully privileged identity. Cut it by removing one edge: helpdesk's ForceChangePassword right over that account.

Close the example on the defensive move, not the attack: one surgical ACL change collapses the whole path, and the graph showed you exactly which one.

Detection: volume, breadth, rights-focused reads

Reading the directory is normal, so hunt the shape, not any single query.

HACKTHESOCLesson 41LDAP and BloodHound37 / 59
Hunt the shape of the reading, never the fact of it.

One principal, many objects, wide scope, security-descriptor reads, from a host that never did this before. That combination is the signal; each part alone is daily noise.

State the thesis of the whole detection block up front so every query and alert that follows is read as one more way to measure this same shape.
HACKTHESOCLesson 41LDAP and BloodHound38 / 59

Event 4662: a security-descriptor read burst

  • One account touching thousands of objects, reading security descriptors, is strong enumeration
  • 4662 needs SACL auditing on the objects, or it never fires
Event 4661 (handle requested) can spike similarly. The repetition line is the point: a single 4662 is nothing; thousands from one principal in minutes is the sweep.
HACKTHESOCLesson 41LDAP and BloodHound39 / 59

FieldValueWhat it tells the analyst
SubjectUserNamesvc-helpdesk02The reading principal; baseline its normal object count
ObjectNameCN=Domain Admins,...A privileged target; thousands of distinct names means a sweep
AccessMask0x20000READ_CONTROL: reading the security descriptor (the ACL)

0x20000 is READ_CONTROL, reading an object's ACL. That is exactly what ACL collection does to every object, which is why this mask in bulk is the ACL-enumeration tell.
HACKTHESOCLesson 41LDAP and BloodHound40 / 59

Behavioural tells and the best tripwire

  • Collection burstsConnections from one host to many machines, plus rapid sequential LDAP binds.
  • Defender for IdentityFlags LDAP reconnaissance and enumeration patterns on DC traffic directly.
  • Honeytoken accountsA decoy like svc_backup_admin with no legitimate use. Any read or Kerberos request touching it is almost certainly malicious.

A honeytoken appears in any broad collection output, catching the sweep itself, not only later exploitation.

The honeytoken is the highest-fidelity tell because nothing legitimate ever queries it, so it needs no baseline and almost never false-positives.
HACKTHESOCLesson 41LDAP and BloodHound41 / 59

Two channels a DC-only LDAP hunt misses

Host-based collection

Default/All reach over SMB/RPC to every host for sessions (srvsvc, NetSessionEnum) and local groups (samr). Watch Event 5145 for repeated IPC$ plus \PIPE\srvsvc / \PIPE\samr.

AD Web Services (9389)

The AD PowerShell module and SOAPHound query ADWS on TCP 9389, not raw 389. A hunt on 389/636 alone misses it entirely.

The host tell is one source opening short-lived SMB sessions to dozens or hundreds of hosts in minutes, plus a 4624 Type 3 fan-out from one account.

ADWS proxies to LDAP, so 1644 LDAP-search logging still captures the resulting searches on the DC. Still, monitor 9389 connections from non-admin hosts directly.
HACKTHESOCLesson 41LDAP and BloodHound42 / 59

Event 5145: the host-based session sweep

  • IPC$ plus srvsvc is the session-enumeration pipe (NetSessionEnum)
  • samr in the same position is local-group enumeration
This is the 5145 counterpart to the 4662 LDAP tell. The fan-out of one account to hundreds of hosts is what distinguishes it from an admin touching one or two servers.
HACKTHESOCLesson 41LDAP and BloodHound43 / 59

Benign session enumeration versus a collection fan-out

Admin tools and inventory scanners enumerate sessions too, so judge the spread and the source.

Benign

Recognisable source, predictable cadence, and a business reason you can name.

  • An inventory server polling on a schedule
  • A known management host or SCCM-style agent
  • Steady, from a consistent source, few targets at a time

Malicious

A source with no inventory role suddenly enumerating the whole estate, once, fast.

  • A workstation or user account, not a mgmt host
  • Hundreds of hosts in a single tight burst
  • Paired with an LDAP sweep from the same principal

The clincher is correlation: the same principal lighting up 1644/4662 on the DC and 5145 across hosts in the same window.

Name the real false positives: vuln scanners and asset-inventory tools do fan out. Allow-list those sources so the alert means something when it fires.

Honeytoken confirmation: the zero-baseline signal

  • Place the decoy wellA privileged-looking account in a monitored OU, with an enticing name and no real use.
  • Alert on any touchAny 4662, 4769 Kerberos request, or LDAP read against it is near-certainly malicious.
  • It catches collection itselfA sweep reads every object, so it reads the decoy, confirming the activity before exploitation.

Unlike the statistical hunts, a honeytoken needs no learning period and almost never false-positives.

This is the pairing that covers the MDI learning window: statistical hunts calibrate while the honeytoken fires immediately on any collection.

Section 10 of 10

Defence: reducing exposure and payoff

Shrink what an attacker can find, and what a found path is worth.

HACKTHESOCLesson 41LDAP and BloodHound51 / 59

Four defensive levers

ACL hygiene

Remove dangerous delegated rights like GenericAll and WriteDACL. Graph your own domain on a schedule and remediate the shortest paths first.

Administrative tiering

Tier the model so enumerating a workstation never reveals Tier 0 sessions, and a lower-tier ACL mistake cannot chain up to Domain Admin.

Reduce readable surface

Clear secrets from description fields, retire stale accounts flagged by old lastLogonTimestamp, constrain sensitive-attribute visibility.

Behavioural monitoring

Deploy Defender for Identity on DCs and baseline normal LDAP volume per account so bulk enumeration stands out from app traffic.

Defence reduces both exposure (what is findable) and payoff (what a path is worth).

Running the path graph against your own domain, the same way an attacker would, finds the removable edges first. Defence is proactive here, not just monitoring.
HACKTHESOCLesson 41LDAP and BloodHound52 / 59
Defence runs on two fronts: shrink what is findable on-prem, and extend the same path-thinking to the cloud.

On-prem you cut dangerous edges and blind host-based collection; in Entra you model the same control relationships and watch for bulk Graph reads.

An attacker runs one continuous graph across on-prem and cloud, so the defender must too. This bridges the on-prem levers to the cloud ones.
HACKTHESOCLesson 41LDAP and BloodHound53 / 59

Blind the host half, and cover the cloud graph

Restrict remote SAM

The GPO 'Network access: Restrict clients allowed to make remote calls to SAM' (RestrictRemoteSam), scoped to admins only, blinds the host-based AdminTo / local-group half of a collection.

Cover Entra ID

The graph also ingests Entra ID (Azure AD) data, modelling AZGlobalAdmin, AZOwner, AZResetPassword and app-control edges. Run it on your tenant and watch MicrosoftGraphActivityLogs for bulk reads.

The Graph-activity burst is the Entra parallel to the on-prem 4662 burst.

A cloud escalation path is often an app owner who can add credentials to a privileged service principal: the exact attack-path thinking, extended to the cloud.

Key takeaways

  1. 01AD is queryable over LDAP, and most of it is readable by any authenticated user by design, so recon is reading, not exploiting.
  2. 02Recon precedes every attack: it enumerates users, groups, trusts, SPNs, and especially ACLs where escalation edges hide.
  3. 03Hunt the shape, never the query: one principal, many objects, wide scope, security-descriptor reads, from a new source.
  4. 04A DC-only LDAP hunt misses the host channel (5145 SMB session/group sweeps) and ADWS on 9389; cover all three.
  5. 05No single edge is an alarm; graph the paths, cut the one needless edge, and pair statistical hunts with a zero-baseline honeytoken.

Check your understanding, question 1 of 3

One standard user account generates thousands of Event ID 4662 object-read operations across users, groups, and computers within a few minutes. What is the most likely explanation?

A burst of thousands of 4662 reads spanning many object types and touching security descriptors is the hallmark of a collection sweep. A backup reads a narrow set, a normal logon reads only the user's own memberships, and replication is DC-to-DC.

Choose an answer, or press R to reveal it.

Why do defenders treat Active Directory ACLs, not just group memberships, as a critical part of the attack surface?
