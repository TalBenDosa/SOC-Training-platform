Section 1 of 9

Kerberos login and why pre-auth exists

What happens the first moment someone logs in to a domain.

HACKTHESOCLesson 40AS-REP Roasting6 / 51

The four-message front-gate exchange

  1. 1AS-REQThe client asks the KDC: 'I am this user, give me a TGT', the token proving a successful logon.
  2. 2Pre-authenticationBy default the client must first prove it knows the password, by encrypting a current timestamp with a password-derived key.
  3. 3KDC verifiesThe KDC decrypts that timestamp with the account's key. If it works and is recent, knowledge of the password is proven.
  4. 4AS-REPOnly then does the KDC reply with the TGT. Pre-auth is a bouncer's whisper-check, done cryptographically.
The client never sends the password itself. Pre-auth is the gate that normally stands before the KDC says anything back.
HACKTHESOCLesson 40AS-REP Roasting7 / 51

The words you need before anything else

  • KDCKey Distribution Center: the domain controller service that issues Kerberos tickets.
  • TGTTicket Granting Ticket: the proof-of-logon token the KDC returns after the first exchange.
  • AS-REQ / AS-REPThe request for that first ticket, and the KDC's reply to it.
  • Pre-authenticationProving you know the password, by encrypting a current timestamp, before the KDC replies.
Define every acronym up front. Students cannot read a 4768 later if KDC, TGT and AS-REP are still fuzzy.
HACKTHESOCLesson 40AS-REP Roasting8 / 51

Pre-authentication on versus off

Turning it off is what makes crackable ciphertext reach a stranger.

Pre-auth enforced

The client must first prove the password by encrypting a timestamp the KDC can decrypt. Only then does the KDC reply, so no useful ciphertext reaches an unauthenticated requester.

Pre-auth disabled

The KDC skips that check and returns the AS-REP to anyone who asks, while still encrypting part of it with the password key: ciphertext a stranger can take away and attack.

DONT_REQ_PREAUTH is bit 0x400000 (decimal 4194304) in the account's userAccountControl attribute.

Colour encodes meaning here: green is the safe default, red is the exposed state. Lead with the contrast, not the flag name.
HACKTHESOCLesson 40AS-REP Roasting9 / 51
The flag removes the check, not the encryption.

The KDC stops verifying the key before it replies, but never stops using that key to encrypt part of the reply. That gap is the entire exposure.

Spell the paradox out slowly. This single idea is what the whole lesson rests on.

Section 2 of 9

What AS-REP Roasting is

The attack in one sentence, and why accounts end up exposed.

HACKTHESOCLesson 40AS-REP Roasting11 / 51
AS-REP Roasting is taking the password-encrypted AS-REP from a pre-auth-disabled account and guessing its password offline.

That is the whole technique, MITRE ATT&CK T1558.004. No steps follow on this slide: the defender's interest is the exposure and the telemetry, not the procedure.

Keep it to one plain sentence. The rest of the deck is about what it leaves behind and how to shut it down.
HACKTHESOCLesson 40AS-REP Roasting12 / 51

Why would any account have pre-auth disabled?

  • Legacy compatibilityOlder or non-Windows Kerberos clients could not perform pre-auth, so an admin disabled it to make login work.
  • Copy-paste provisioningAn admin clones an old service-account template that already had the flag set, not realising what it does.
  • Forgotten testingThe flag was toggled once for troubleshooting and never reverted.

Nobody sets this maliciously; it becomes a weakness through misconfiguration or legacy interoperability.

The flag is low-visibility: nothing looks broken, which is exactly why it survives for years and why hunts find it.
HACKTHESOCLesson 40AS-REP Roasting13 / 51
No lockout, no rate limit, no failed-logon event: the guessing never touches the domain.

A copy of the ciphertext is attacked on separate hardware, so Event 4625 (failed logon) counters and lockout thresholds see nothing at all.

Contrast with typing a wrong password at a login screen, where the domain sees every attempt. Here it sees none of the guessing.
HACKTHESOCLesson 40AS-REP Roasting14 / 51

Where the damage lands

Impact scales with what the exposed account can do.

Ordinary user

A cracked standard-user password still gives a foothold and a starting point for lateral movement and recon.

Privileged or service account

A pre-auth-exempt admin or service account with a weak password is one of the cheapest routes to full-domain compromise there is.

Priority when you find exposed accounts follows privilege: fix the admin and service accounts first.

When triaging a hit later, the account's group membership is what turns a low into a critical.

Section 3 of 9

The telemetry the exchange leaves behind

What the domain controller records, even when pre-auth is skipped.

HACKTHESOCLesson 40AS-REP Roasting16 / 51
Every AS-REQ is logged on the domain controller, whether pre-auth happened or not.

That is why the defender has a signal at all: a request against a pre-auth-disabled account still writes an Event 4768 on the DC that issued the reply.

Reassure students that even a 'stealthy' technique produces a record. The stealth is only in the offline guessing, not the request.
HACKTHESOCLesson 40AS-REP Roasting17 / 51

What fires, in order

  1. 1A directory read (quiet)The account is located by a query for the pre-auth-disabled flag, a normal-looking directory read that rarely stands out.
  2. 2The AS request (the signal)The exempt account is requested with no pre-auth, which the DC records as Event 4768 with Pre-Authentication Type 0.
  3. 3The guessing (invisible)A copy of the encrypted reply is attacked elsewhere. The domain sees nothing of this stage.

The defender's reliable signal is the middle stage: the 4768 with Pre-Authentication Type 0.

Map each stage to visibility: quiet, then the one clear signal, then invisible. Detection anchors on the middle.
HACKTHESOCLesson 40AS-REP Roasting18 / 51

The three fields that tell the story

Everything you need to decide is inside one 4768, plus how often it repeats.

SignalBenign lookRoasting look
Pre-Authentication TypeNon-zero, commonly 20, no pre-auth performed
Ticket Encryption Type0x12 or 0x11 (AES)0x17 (RC4), fast to crack
Requests per sourceOne user, usual hostMany usernames, one source IP
These three are the whole detection. Everything later just operationalises reading them at scale.
HACKTHESOCLesson 40AS-REP Roasting19 / 51

One exempt account versus a burst

Volume separates a legitimate exemption from bulk roasting.

Legitimate exemption

A single known-exempt account requested from its usual host, matching your approved baseline. Expected, documented, closed.

Bulk roasting

A burst of Type 0 requests for many different usernames from one source IP in a short window. Nobody logs in as fifty accounts from one workstation in ten seconds.

Volume per source is the discriminator a human finds obvious and a rule can encode cheaply.

Section 4 of 9

Targeted roasting: a flag toggle you can see

When the exemption is created on purpose, it leaves its own trail.

HACKTHESOCLesson 40AS-REP Roasting21 / 51
Write access over a user object removes the need for the flag to already be set.

Rights such as GenericAll, GenericWrite or WriteProperty, often from sloppy helpdesk delegation, let an attacker change an account's userAccountControl.

This is why auditing who can write to user objects belongs in the same conversation as password strength.
HACKTHESOCLesson 40AS-REP Roasting22 / 51

The event sequence a defender observes

  1. 1A 4738 adds the flagDirectory records a user account changed, with DONT_REQ_PREAUTH newly set on an account that never had it.
  2. 2A 4768 with Type 0Moments later the now-exempt account is requested, logged as an AS ticket request with no pre-auth.
  3. 3A 4738 reverts the flagThe flag is removed again so the account looks untouched. The paired 4738s around the 4768 are the tell.

Even a strong password does not save an account whose flag can be flipped by a compromised delegated account.

Frame this as what the DC logs, not as a how-to. The learning point is the recognisable 4738 to 4768 to 4738 shape.
HACKTHESOCLesson 40AS-REP Roasting23 / 51

Events to correlate for a targeted roast

EventWhat it recordsWhy it matters
4738A user account was changedA new DONT_REQ_PREAUTH here is the setup for a targeted roast
5136A directory object was modifiedCaptures the exact attribute change when object auditing is on
4768An AS ticket was requestedType 0 between two 4738s completes the signature
5136 requires Directory Service Changes auditing to be enabled; without it you lean on the paired 4738s and the 4768.

AS-REP Roasting vs Kerberoasting

Two offline-cracking techniques that are easy to confuse for detection.

HACKTHESOCLesson 40AS-REP Roasting25 / 51

Know the distinction cold

AspectAS-REP Roasting (T1558.004)Kerberoasting (T1558.003)
Targeted accountsUsers with pre-auth disabledAccounts with a Service Principal Name
Kerberos messageAS-REP, the initial auth exchangeTGS-REP, a service ticket
Needs valid domain creds?No, can be unauthenticatedYes, must be able to request a TGS
Key event ID4768, AS ticket requested4769, service ticket requested
The two anchor events, 4768 versus 4769, are the fastest way to tell which technique a captured pattern belongs to.
HACKTHESOCLesson 40AS-REP Roasting26 / 51

What defeats each one

They share a weakness but not a cure.

Shared defences

Strong, long passwords and AES-only Kerberos defeat both, because both ultimately depend on cracking RC4 ciphertext of a weak password offline.

AS-REP only

Re-enabling pre-authentication cures AS-REP Roasting outright. There is no equivalent single switch for Kerberoasting.

gMSA (Group Managed Service Accounts) remove the human weak-password risk for service accounts entirely.

The asymmetry matters: AS-REP has a clean root-cause fix, Kerberoasting does not, which shapes how you prioritise remediation.
HACKTHESOCLesson 40AS-REP Roasting27 / 51
You cannot remove the Service Principal Names that services legitimately need.

So Kerberoasting defence leans on password strength and AES, while AS-REP Roasting can be removed at the source by requiring pre-auth again.

This is why re-enabling pre-auth is such a satisfying fix: it deletes the exposure rather than making cracking slower.

Detection: reading Event 4768

The DC-side record of every AS-REQ/AS-REP exchange.

HACKTHESOCLesson 40AS-REP Roasting29 / 51

The fingerprint inside a single 4768

  • Pre-Authentication Type 0 means no pre-auth was performed: the direct signal
  • 0x17 is RC4; in an AES environment that alone is worth flagging
A normal interactive logon shows a non-zero pre-auth type, commonly 2. Type 0 for an account that should be a normal user is the single strongest indicator.
HACKTHESOCLesson 40AS-REP Roasting30 / 51

Read it field by field

What each line means and what the analyst does with it.

FieldValueWhat it means / do
Account Namesvc-legacyThe exposed principal; check its privilege and baseline
Pre-Authentication Type0No pre-auth: the defining trait of this technique
Ticket Encryption Type0x17RC4, the fast-crack path; anomalous in an AES domain
Client Address10.14.22.108Pivot here for other accounts and follow-on logons
The highlighted row is the one that confirms the technique. The client address is what turns one event into a hunt.
HACKTHESOCLesson 40AS-REP Roasting31 / 51

Benign 4768 versus malicious 4768

Same event, three things pull it one way or the other.

Benign

Type 2 pre-auth, AES encryption, one account, from the host it always uses, inside normal hours. This is just someone logging in.

Malicious

Type 0, RC4 (0x17), many usernames from one source in seconds, often an account with no business reason to be exempt. This is roasting.

Give students the full benign shape so Type 0 does not become a blanket alarm: legitimate exemptions do exist and must be baselined.
HACKTHESOCLesson 40AS-REP Roasting32 / 51

Build the rule, baseline, and bait

  • Watch the patternA burst of 4768s for many usernames from one source IP in a short window is bulk roasting, not a person.
  • Correlation ruleAlert on EventID 4768 with Pre-Authentication Type 0, enriched with encryption type and grouped by source IP.
  • Baseline exemptionsKeep a list of accounts that legitimately have pre-auth disabled, so requests outside that list stand out at once.
  • HoneytokenA pre-auth-disabled decoy with a tempting name and a long random password. Any Type 0 4768 for it is a near-zero-false-positive hit.
The honeytoken has no legitimate reason to ever be queried, so it is one of the highest-fidelity tripwires you can deploy.

Triage and response

From a single 4768 alert to a closed, documented ticket.

HACKTHESOCLesson 40AS-REP Roasting34 / 51

Triage a Type 0 4768 alert

  1. 1Confirm the signalVerify Pre-Authentication Type is 0, and note the ticket encryption type and client address.
  2. 2Identify the accountWhich principal was requested, and what privilege does it hold in the domain?
  3. 3Check the baselineIs the account on your approved exempt list, or is the exemption unexpected?
  4. 4Measure the spreadOne account from a known host, or many usernames from one source in a short window?
  5. 5Rate severityAn unexpected exemption on a privileged account, with RC4, is your worst case.
Severity is privilege times surprise times spread. A baselined exempt account from its own host is routine; the opposite is an incident.
HACKTHESOCLesson 40AS-REP Roasting35 / 51
The decision hinges on one question: is this account supposed to be exempt?

A documented, baselined exemption from its usual host is noise you close. An exemption nobody approved, or one that just appeared, is the real finding.

Push students to answer this before they escalate. Most Type 0 events in a healthy domain resolve to a small, known list.
HACKTHESOCLesson 40AS-REP Roasting36 / 51

Respond when it is real

  1. 1Reset the passwordForce a long random reset so any offline guessing already underway is wasted.
  2. 2Re-enable pre-authenticationRemove the DONT_REQ_PREAUTH bit unless a retired legacy client genuinely still needs it.
  3. 3Hunt the sourcePivot on the client address for 4768s against other accounts and for successful follow-on logons.
  4. 4Check for reuseLook for the account authenticating from new hosts, a sign the password was already cracked and used.
Resetting the password alone is incomplete: without re-enabling pre-auth the new password is just as roastable as the old one.
HACKTHESOCLesson 40AS-REP Roasting37 / 51

Escalate or close

Match the response to what you actually found.

Close

A single request for a known-exempt account from its usual host, matching the approved baseline. Document and close.

Remediate

An unexpected exemption or an off-baseline source, with no spread yet. Reset, re-enable pre-auth, and keep watching the source.

Escalate

A burst across many accounts from one source, or RC4 on a privileged account. Treat as active roasting and start incident response.

Three clear outcomes keep the analyst decisive. Most alerts land in close; the rare red one is unmistakable.

The audit query that finds exposed accounts

1.2.840.113556.1.4.803 is the LDAP bitwise-AND matching rule; changing only the decimal reuses it for any risky flag. This is a defender audit, not an attack.
HACKTHESOCLesson 40AS-REP Roasting41 / 51

What to do with the results

  • Each hit is an exposureEvery account returned can be roasted today. Treat the list as a live attack surface, not an inventory.
  • Sort by privilegeA pre-auth-exempt account in an admin or service group is the highest priority to fix first.
  • Compare to baselineAnything not on your documented-exemption list is configuration drift to remove now.
Run this on a schedule. A migration or template copy can silently re-add the flag long after you first cleaned it up.

Section 9 of 9

Hardening: closing the gap

Attack the root cause, not just the request you happened to catch.

HACKTHESOCLesson 40AS-REP Roasting43 / 51

Four defensive moves

Require pre-auth

Remove the 0x400000 flag wherever legacy needs do not truly require it. This alone neutralises the attack.

Strong passwords / gMSA

Any account that must stay exempt needs 25+ random characters. Prefer gMSA so passwords are machine-generated and rotated.

Disable RC4, use AES

Force Kerberos onto AES, removing the fast-crack type and making any RC4 request an anomaly in its own right.

Continuously audit

A migration or template copy can silently re-add the flag. Alert on any newly appearing exemption.

Pair the recurring audit with the honeytoken so a missed window still has a tripwire.

Re-enabling pre-auth returns the KDC to demanding the whisper-check. Confirm the legacy client that forced the exemption is truly retired first.
HACKTHESOCLesson 40AS-REP Roasting44 / 51

Encryption types you want to see

The etype in a 4768 tells you how healthy the exchange is.

Encryption typeMeaningWhat a defender wants
0x17RC4-HMAC, legacy and fast to crackRare; flag it in an AES domain
0x11AES128-CTS-HMAC-SHA1-96Expected modern value
0x12AES256-CTS-HMAC-SHA1-96Preferred, strongest
Disabling RC4 does not make roasting impossible; it removes the fast path, so any lingering 0x17 is worth a second look.
HACKTHESOCLesson 40AS-REP Roasting45 / 51
Flip the bit back carefully: a live legacy client that needed the exemption will stop logging in.

Confirm the interoperability reason for every exemption is genuinely retired before re-requiring pre-auth, or you trade an exposure for an outage.

This is the one place re-enabling pre-auth can bite. Verify first, then remove, and watch for broken logons afterwards.
HACKTHESOCLesson 40AS-REP Roasting46 / 51

Layer the tripwires so nothing slips

  • Recurring auditA scheduled directory query that alerts on any new pre-auth exemption catches drift between manual reviews.
  • HoneytokenA pre-auth-disabled decoy means even an exemption you missed still trips an alert the moment it is roasted.
  • Block reconDisable anonymous LDAP and SAMR enumeration and alert on repeated KDC error codes from one source to catch target-list building.
Defence in depth: the audit prevents, the honeytoken detects, and blocking anonymous enumeration raises the cost of even finding targets.
HACKTHESOCLesson 40AS-REP Roasting47 / 51

Key takeaways

  1. 01Kerberos normally requires pre-auth: proving you know the password before the KDC issues a TGT, like a bouncer's whisper-check.
  2. 02DONT_REQ_PREAUTH (bit 0x400000) tells the KDC to skip that check, handing out an AS-REP whose key is still password-encrypted.
  3. 03Guessing is fully offline, so there is no lockout, rate limit, or failed-logon event for the domain to see.
  4. 04AS-REP Roasting (4768, can be unauthenticated) is easy to confuse with Kerberoasting (4769, SPN accounts, authenticated).
  5. 05Detect on 4768 with Pre-Auth Type 0 and RC4 (0x17); defend by re-enabling pre-auth, strong passwords or gMSA, and AES.
