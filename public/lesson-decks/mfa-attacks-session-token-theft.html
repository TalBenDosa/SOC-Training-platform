Section 1 of 6

Sessions, tokens, and what MFA actually proves

Two facts are the whole foundation, plus one sentence that governs the lesson.

HACKTHESOCLesson 93MFA attacks and token theft6 / 35

Two facts are the whole foundation

Bearer credential

A session cookie (and the tokens issued with it) is treated as the logged-in user with no re-proof of identity. A stolen session inherits every permission the real user had.

Three distinct tokens

An IdP such as Microsoft Entra ID or Okta issues three different tokens after login. Confusing them is a common mistake.

This lesson assumes students already know what authentication, a session cookie and an identity provider are. Do not re-teach them.
HACKTHESOCLesson 93MFA attacks and token theft7 / 35

Three token types junior analysts mix up

Token typeWhat it provesTypical lifetime
ID tokenWho the user is: name, email, tenantMinutes to an hour
Access tokenWhich resource or API the holder may call, and scopeMinutes to an hour
Refresh tokenSilently obtains new access tokens without re-authenticatingDays to months

Under single sign-on (SSO), one stolen IdP session opens many connected apps: one theft, many doors.

A refresh token is the highest-value artifact to steal: it mints fresh access tokens indefinitely unless explicitly revoked, even after a password rotation.

The mental model for the whole lesson

MFA is checked once and its result is baked into the session or token. Every attack that follows exploits exactly that trust.

MFA fatigue and push bombing (T1621)

Multi-Factor Authentication Request Generation, Credential Access tactic: wearing down the human.

HACKTHESOCLesson 93MFA attacks and token theft10 / 35
The attacker can trigger pushes as often as they like, for free, with no lockout. The victim only has to make one mistake, ever.

The password already comes from credential stuffing, a phishing kit, or an infostealer log bought on a criminal marketplace.

It needs no exploit, only a password and a tired, distracted or annoyed human. That is why it is the most practiced technique in this family.
HACKTHESOCLesson 93MFA attacks and token theft11 / 35

How a push-bombing takeover runs

  1. 1Submit the correct passwordFirst factor passes every time; each attempt sends a new push.
  2. 2Burst or spread the pushesDozens in minutes, or over hours, often late at night when the victim is groggy.
  3. 3Pose as IT supportUber 2022: a WhatsApp message claiming to be IT after a barrage of pushes.
  4. 4Victim approves oneOut of habit, confusion, or belief they are helping IT. A fully MFA-satisfied session.
CISA/FBI advisory AA23-320A on Scattered Spider names push bombing alongside SIM swapping and help-desk social engineering.
HACKTHESOCLesson 93MFA attacks and token theft12 / 35

Simplified timeline of a push-bombing takeover

  • Every push is legitimate: a real login attempt against the real IdP
  • Volume and pattern over time is the only tell: a detection problem
You cannot refuse to send a push just because a password was entered correctly. No malware, no exploit, nothing for traditional tools to block.

Detecting MFA fatigue

Signs, fields, and the mitigation that removes the attack surface.

HACKTHESOCLesson 93MFA attacks and token theft14 / 35

Signs an analyst looks for

  • Burst then acceptSeveral denials then one acceptance, same source IP or same login attempt.
  • Off-hours timingUber-style incidents often land at 1-3 AM victim time.
  • Network mismatchIP, country or autonomous system (AS) inconsistent with the user, e.g. a hosting or VPN provider.
  • New device right afterAttackers enroll their own device within seconds of getting in.
The new-device enrollment guarantees the attacker can complete MFA again even if the password is reset. Always check for it.
HACKTHESOCLesson 93MFA attacks and token theft15 / 35

Reading it in the Okta System Log

  • One record proves nothing; the detection is a correlation rule
  • Count DENIED per actor in ~15 min, threshold commonly 5-10
  • Alert when the burst is immediately followed by SUCCESS from the same source
False-positive discipline: a single denied push is common and usually benign. Never escalate on one denial alone; escalate on the pattern and check for an acceptance.
HACKTHESOCLesson 93MFA attacks and token theft16 / 35

Number matching stops fatigue, and only fatigue

What it does

The sign-in screen shows a two-digit number the user must type into the app. A reflex tap can no longer succeed. Mandatory in Microsoft; Okta calls it Number Challenge.

What it does not stop

A reverse-proxy kit shows the victim the correct number in real time. A stolen session cookie never triggers a push at all.

Treating number matching as a complete MFA-attack solution is the most common false sense of security.

Plant this now; it comes back in the defense ladder in section 6.

Section 4 of 6

AiTM phishing and session-cookie theft

T1539 Steal Web Session Cookie, then T1550.004 Web Session Cookie reuse.

HACKTHESOCLesson 93MFA attacks and token theft18 / 35
AiTM defeats MFA even when the user does everything right.

Real password, real push, approved deliberately, and the account is still taken over. Two ATT&CK IDs for two moments: the theft, then the replay.

T1539 sits under Credential Access; T1550.004 under Lateral Movement. Known reverse-proxy kits: Evilginx, Tycoon 2FA, EvilProxy.
HACKTHESOCLesson 93MFA attacks and token theft19 / 35

How a reverse-proxy phishing kit steals the cookie

  1. 1Look-alike linklogin.company-sso-secure.com instead of login.microsoftonline.com.
  2. 2Proxy, not fake pageIt forwards every request and response to the real IdP in real time.
  3. 3Real password relayedThe real IdP sends a real push to the victim's real phone.
  4. 4Victim approvesFunctionally they are logging into the real service; the proxy just watches.
  5. 5Real cookie issuedThe IdP returns a valid, MFA-satisfied session cookie through the proxy.
  6. 6Proxy copies itThen passes it on, so the victim's login 'succeeds' normally.
CISA's 2025 Scattered Spider update names this class of tooling as the mechanism behind the group's 'near-universal' MFA bypass.
HACKTHESOCLesson 93MFA attacks and token theft20 / 35

MFA bypassed vs MFA made irrelevant

MFA bypassed

MFA fatigue (T1621)

The attacker never had the second factor and tricked the human into supplying it.

MFA made irrelevant

AiTM theft (T1539 + T1550.004)

MFA was performed correctly by the real, willing user. The failure is what happened to the artifact afterwards.

On replay: no password, no push, no authentication step at all on the attacker's side, often from another country, AS and browser.

This is the single most important conceptual distinction in the lesson. Make students say it back.

Section 5 of 6

Detecting replay, token theft, and forged SAML

None involves a bad login. Each is an anomaly in what happens to an artifact after authentication.

HACKTHESOCLesson 93MFA attacks and token theft22 / 35

Session replay: the same sessionId from two places

  • One sessionId should never appear from two IPs, ASes and browsers
  • 'Satisfied by claim in the token' admits no fresh authentication ran
  • Clean riskLevelDuringSignIn and CA 'success' do not prove a clean sign-in
The risk engine and Conditional Access both see a valid, MFA-satisfied token and have no way to know it was stolen. That is why the sessionId correlation beats any single risk field.
HACKTHESOCLesson 93MFA attacks and token theft23 / 35

Hunt one SessionId seen from two networks

  • Same session token seen from 2+ networks
  • Run the same logic over AADNonInteractiveUserSignInLogs: replay lands there
  • Corroborate with ID Protection: Anomalous token, AiTM, Token issuer anomaly
The replayed token is used non-interactively, so much of the activity never appears in interactive SigninLogs. Correlate the Entra ID Protection risk events with a matching duplicated SessionId.

Other token-theft vectors, and where each is seen

Endpoint cookie theft

Infostealer

RedLine, Lumma, Raccoon take cookies from the browser store. EDR: non-browser process reading the cookie database, then sign-ins from a new location.

T1528-adjacent

Device-code phishing

The victim enters the attacker's device code and tokens are issued. Seen as device-code-flow sign-ins the user did not initiate.

T1550

PRT theft

Malware on a joined Windows device extracts the PRT. ID Protection: 'Possible attempt to access Primary Refresh Token'.

T1528

OAuth consent

A malicious app gets access and refresh tokens via a real consent screen. Audit Entra 'Consent to application' events.

T1134.001 is a Windows endpoint token technique seen in Sysmon and Security events, never in an IdP sign-in log.

Infostealer replay looks identical in the IdP to AiTM replay; only EDR shows the endpoint origin. For consent grants, flag unusual publishers, new apps and overly broad scopes such as full mailbox read.
HACKTHESOCLesson 93MFA attacks and token theft25 / 35
Golden SAML (T1606.002): a federated sign-in claims AD FS issued it, but AD FS has no matching issuance event.

A Global Administrator federated sign-in in Entra with no AD FS Event ID 1200 ('issued a valid token') on any federation server is the tell.

With the stolen signing key, tokens are minted entirely offline. Watch the precursor too: abnormal access to the AD FS service account and DKM container, or the token-signing certificate being read or exported. 1202 is 'validated a new credential'.

From number matching to phishing-resistant MFA

A ladder of mitigations, and what each rung does not cover.

The defense ladder

RungWhat it stopsWhat it leaves open
1. Number matchingFatigue reflex tapsAiTM relays the number; replay has no push
2. Conditional AccessCompliant device blocks proxied sign-in: device identity can't be relayedCoverage gaps: BYOD, exclusions, tokens minted earlier

Token Protection native-app coverage (Windows, iOS/iPadOS, macOS) is GA for Exchange Online, SharePoint Online and Teams.
HACKTHESOCLesson 93MFA attacks and token theft28 / 35
FIDO2 signs the challenge together with the exact origin, so a look-alike proxy domain fails at the protocol level.

CISA names only FIDO2/WebAuthn and PIV/CAC as phishing-resistant. NIST SP 800-63B calls it verifier impersonation resistance.

SMS, app push and push with number matching are all named by CISA as still vulnerable to AiTM relay. FIDO2 needs no human vigilance at all.

Response playbook: a password reset alone does nothing

  1. 1Revoke sessions and tokensRevoke-MgUserSignInSession (Graph revokeSignInSessions); confirm non-interactive sign-ins stop.
  2. 2Remove attacker MFA methodsAudit 'User registered security info' events, delete unknown methods, then reset the password.
  3. 3Golden SAML: roll the certRoll the AD FS token-signing certificate and revoke tokens for all potentially affected identities.
  4. 4Review and feed backWhat the artifact accessed (mailbox rules, file downloads); feed indicators into detections.
Golden SAML has no MFA rung at all: it forges the output of authentication. Protect the signing key like a domain controller and restrict DKM (Distributed Key Manager) access tightly.
HACKTHESOCLesson 93MFA attacks and token theft30 / 35

The analyst decision framework

  1. 1Did MFA actually run?Or does the detail say 'satisfied by a claim in the token'? Fatigue vs artifact theft.
  2. 2Who saw the challenge?If a fresh challenge ran, could a reverse proxy have relayed it?
  3. 3Which artifact was reused?Cookie T1550.004, OAuth token T1528, Windows token T1134.001, or forged SAML T1606.002.
Each answer points to a different log source, containment action and root cause. 'The account was compromised' is not a professional finding.

Key takeaways

  1. 01MFA is checked once at login and baked into a cookie or token; stealing the artifact defeats MFA.
  2. 02MFA fatigue (T1621) tricks the human into approving; number matching closes this specific hole.
  3. 03AiTM (T1539 + T1550.004) steals the cookie after a correct login and replays it with no authentication.
  4. 04Prove replay with one SessionId from 2+ ASNs, in SigninLogs and AADNonInteractiveUserSignInLogs.
  5. 05T1134.001 is a Windows endpoint technique; T1528 is cloud OAuth abuse. Different logs, different layers.
  6. 06Only FIDO2/WebAuthn or PIV/CAC structurally stop AiTM, by binding the response to the origin domain.

Check your understanding, question 1 of 3

A junior analyst says: 'The session cookie was stolen, but the account still has MFA enabled, so the attacker's access is limited to what MFA allows.' What is wrong with this reasoning?

MFA is checked once at authentication and baked into the artifact. The app never re-invokes it for a valid cookie. CA is evaluated at sign-in or token issuance, so without Token Protection or CAE a new IP does not force fresh MFA.

Check your understanding, question 3 of 3

Why does push with number matching fail to stop a reverse-proxy AiTM attack, while FIDO2/WebAuthn stops it?

Number matching has no cryptographic binding to the domain the browser visits, so a proxy passes the number through. FIDO2 signs with the negotiated origin and refuses a mismatched domain. The number is relayed, not intercepted or guessed.
