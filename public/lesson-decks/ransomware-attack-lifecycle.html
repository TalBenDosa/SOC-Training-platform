By the time files start encrypting, the actionable window has already closed.

Encryption is the last, loudest step of a chain that has run for hours to weeks. Catching that chain is the whole game.

Contrast a SOC that catches ransomware in the pre-encryption window with one that discovers it the morning half the fleet will not boot. Define encryption, RaaS, IAB and double extortion up front.

Three terms that recur through the whole lesson

Each describes a role or a business model, not a technique. They explain why the chain looks the way it does.

  • Initial Access Broker (IAB)A criminal who specialises in breaking in, then sells the working foothold on criminal marketplaces.
  • Ransomware-as-a-Service (RaaS)Core operators build and license the encryptor and leak site; independent affiliates rent it and run the intrusions.
  • Double extortionData is stolen before anything is encrypted, so the victim faces a leak threat even with perfect backups.
All three are unpacked properly in section 7. Here the goal is that students recognise the words when they hear them in sections 1 and 2.
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle6 / 86

The chain runs for hours to weeks before encryption

Seven stages, in order. Every stage before encryption is a chance for the SOC to catch it.

  1. 1Initial accessPhishing, exposed remote access or an edge-device exploit gives a foothold.
  2. 2Dwell and discoveryThe attacker maps Active Directory: domain controllers, privileged groups, key servers.
  3. 3Credential accessLSASS dumping and stored credentials, aiming for a domain admin or equivalent.
  4. 4Lateral movementReach and privilege broad enough to push the encryptor everywhere at once.
  5. 5Pre-encryption sabotageShadow copies, security tools and logs are destroyed. The last real chance.
  6. 6Encryption and extortionFleet-wide encryption in minutes, then the leak-site and payment pressure.
This matches the timeline figure in section 4. Ask the class which stages are quiet and which are loud: the early ones are quiet, sabotage and encryption are loud.

Section 1 of 12

Initial access: phishing, exposed services, edge devices

Almost never a novel technique: well-known weaknesses not closed in time.

HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle8 / 86

Three well-documented ways in

Phishing

Rarely delivers the ransomware itself. Delivers an initial-access loader or a credential-harvesting page that hands over working corporate credentials.

Exposed remote access

RDP on the internet with a weak or reused password, or a VPN with an unpatched flaw or a breached credential. IABs sell exactly this access.

Increasingly dominant

Edge device exploits

VPN gateways, firewalls and file-transfer appliances (Citrix, Fortinet, Ivanti) with a disclosed CVE giving remote code execution at the perimeter.

Edge-device logs are often not forwarded to the SIEM with the rigour of Windows logs: a blind spot exactly where attackers land.

Because of Initial Access Brokers, the group that deploys the ransomware frequently is not the group that broke in. They bought it.
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle9 / 86

How a bought foothold reaches a ransomware crew

Exposed remote access is often found and sold by one group, then used by another.

  1. 1Find the weaknessRDP listening on the internet with a weak or reused password, or a VPN with a known unpatched flaw.
  2. 2Or reuse a breachA VPN credential obtained in a prior breach works just as well as an exploit.
  3. 3Sell pre-validated accessThe IAB lists the working access on a criminal marketplace.
  4. 4The affiliate buys inA different group logs in with valid access and starts the dwell period.

At the console this looks like a valid login, not an exploit. The intruder you are chasing may not be the one who broke in.

This is why attribution by entry vector is weak: the entry and the encryption can belong to two different criminal businesses.
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle10 / 86

Why edge devices are the increasingly dominant way in

VPN gateways, firewalls and secure file-transfer appliances from vendors like Citrix, Fortinet and Ivanti.

PropertyWhy the attacker likes itWhat it means for the SOC
PositionSits at the perimeter with privileged visibility into everything behind itOne compromised appliance is a view into the whole network
Disclosed CVEUnauthenticated or low-friction remote code execution on the devicePatch latency on edge devices is attack surface
MonitoringOften trusted to the vendor's own logging, not forwarded to the SIEMA blind spot exactly where the attacker chooses to land
Contrast with Windows Event Logs, which most teams forward rigorously. Ask: does our SIEM actually receive the VPN gateway logs?

Section 2 of 12

Dwell, discovery and credential access

Hours to weeks spent building toward enterprise-wide encryption.

HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle12 / 86
A competent affiliate does not encrypt immediately.

Encrypting one host would waste the access. The dwell period, a few hours in an aggressive intrusion to several weeks in a deliberate one, builds toward fleet-wide encryption.

This is good news for defenders: the dwell period is time in which the attacker is generating telemetry before anything breaks.
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle13 / 86

What the dwell period is spent on

Discovery

Enumerate AD: domain controllers, privileged groups, high-value servers. Native net group, nltest, the PowerShell AD module, and BloodHound.

  • Telemetry: many LDAP queries on groups and computers
  • From one account or workstation, in a short period
  • Well outside that account's normal pattern

Credential access

LSASS memory dumping (T1003) for cached admin credentials, plus password managers and browser-stored credentials (T1555).

  • Goal: a domain admin or equivalent credential
  • That is what makes fleet-wide encryption possible
A competent affiliate does not encrypt immediately: that would squander the access before extracting maximum value. The LDAP burst is distinctive but easy to miss.
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle14 / 86

Reading an AD discovery burst in the telemetry

Discovery uses built-in tools, so no single command is the alert. The shape of the activity is.

DimensionWhat the discovery burst looks like
VolumeA large number of LDAP queries against group memberships and computer objects
SourceOne account or one workstation generating all of it
TimingConcentrated in a short period, not spread across a working day
BaselineWell outside that account's normal usage pattern
Toolingnet group, nltest, the PowerShell AD module, or BloodHound building an attack-path graph
Distinctive but easy to miss: each query is legitimate on its own. The detection is volume plus source plus baseline deviation.
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle15 / 86

Credential access: the hunt for one domain admin credential

  • LSASS memory dumping (T1003)Harvests cached credentials of any administrator who has logged into the compromised host.
  • Stored credentials (T1555)Password managers and browser-stored credentials give a broader initial credential set.
  • The objectiveA domain administrator or equivalent. That credential turns a handful of compromised hosts into enterprise-wide encryption.
  • Why it matters to youAdmins logging into a compromised workstation leave reusable credentials behind. Every admin logon there widens the blast radius.
Covered in depth in the credential-attacks lesson; here the point is purpose. The attacker is not collecting credentials for their own sake, they are collecting reach.

Section 3 of 12

Lateral movement toward domain admin

The goal is reach: enough access to push the encryptor everywhere at once.

HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle17 / 86

Familiar lateral techniques, each with a job to do

TechniqueWhat it does for the attacker
SMB admin-share abuseStages tools on newly reached hosts
RDP and WinRMInteractive and scripted access to each new host
Pass-the-hash / pass-the-ticketA harvested NTLM hash or Kerberos ticket replaces the plaintext password on each hop
Narrow privileged roleIn mature estates, reaches backups and hypervisor management without full domain compromise
The techniques are the same ones from the lateral-movement lesson. What changes in ransomware is the objective, on the next slide.
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle18 / 86

Same techniques, different objective

Espionage or data theft

Lateral movement toward one specific server or data set. Minimal footprint for as long as possible.

Ransomware

Breadth: enough reach and privilege that one push delivers the encryptor to as many hosts as possible at the same time.

Breadth is what you can hunt: one account suddenly reaching many hosts it never touched before.

Ask the class what "breadth" looks like in logs: a new admin session fanning out to dozens of servers, many of which that account has never logged into.
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle19 / 86

Building the single push

  1. 1Build the target listFrom discovery: every server and workstation the encryptor should reach.
  2. 2Secure the access chainDomain admin, or a role that reaches backups and hypervisor management.
  3. 3Pick a delivery channelGroup Policy, the organisation's own software deployment tool (T1072-adjacent), or remote execution over the list.
  4. 4Push simultaneouslyThe ransomware binary lands on as many hosts as possible at once.
Abuse of legitimate deployment infrastructure is the reason a change in GPO or deployment-tool jobs by an unexpected account deserves a look.
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle20 / 86
Ransomware lateral movement accelerates and broadens in its final days.

The attacker shifts from minimal-footprint discovery to building the target list and access chain the deployment step needs.

Individually quiet techniques become noticeably wider in scope. That widening is itself a signal worth hunting.

The pre-encryption window: your last real chance

T1490, T1685 and T1685.005: loud, specific, and the last point to prevent an outage.

HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle22 / 86

Sabotage is the last stage where you can stop an outage

Unlike the often quiet dwell period, this phase is comparatively loud. After it, encryption runs across the fleet within minutes.
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle23 / 86

Three sabotage actions and the signature each leaves

Sabotage actionATT&CK IDSignature the SOC sees
Shadow copy and backup destructionT1490vssadmin delete shadows /all /quiet, wmic shadowcopy delete fleet-wide; backup repos wiped
Security tool tamperingT1685 (formerly T1562.001)EDR, AV and logging agents stopped or uninstalled on many hosts, sometimes via BYOVD
Log clearingT1685.005 (formerly T1070.001)Security log cleared: Event ID 1102; System or other logs cleared: System Event ID 104
ATT&CK v19 IDs: T1685 (Disable or Modify Tools, formerly T1562.001) and T1685.005 (Clear Windows Event Logs, formerly T1070.001), now under Defense Impairment. BYOVD loads a signed but vulnerable driver to kill a protected EDR process; mass EDR agents going offline outside a known change is top severity.
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle24 / 86

T1490: destroying the way back without paying

Inhibit System Recovery is close to universal, because it decides whether the victim can simply restore.

  • Local recovery pointsvssadmin delete shadows /all /quiet or the WMI equivalent wmic shadowcopy delete, run on as many hosts as access allows.
  • Dedicated backup platformsVeeam, Commvault and similar are hunted down: backup repositories and jobs are deleted directly.
  • Why it is high fidelityVanishingly few legitimate business reasons exist for that exact command to run broadly across a fleet in a short window.
  • What it tells youThe attacker is removing your option to refuse payment. Encryption is likely close.
A victim with an intact, isolated backup has far more leverage to refuse payment. That is exactly why backups are targeted.
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle25 / 86

T1685: how security tools get switched off

Disable or Modify Tools runs in parallel with backup destruction, on as many hosts as possible.

  1. 1Simple: stop the serviceWhere local admin rights allow, the EDR, AV or logging agent is stopped or uninstalled.
  2. 2The obstacleA well-protected EDR process resists a normal termination attempt.
  3. 3BYOVDBring Your Own Vulnerable Driver: load a legitimately signed but vulnerable kernel driver.
  4. 4Kernel privilegeThe driver grants privilege no normal process gets, so the EDR process can be killed.
  5. 5What you seeEDR agents going offline or uninstalled across many hosts in a short window.

Outside a known maintenance change, that is one of the highest-severity signals available, not a routine agent-health issue.

Students often file mass agent-offline alerts under IT hygiene. Warn them: several sensors dropping together is the attacker blinding you.
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle26 / 86

T1685.005: clearing the logs leaves its own record

Clear Windows Event Logs, done with wevtutil cl or the PowerShell Clear-EventLog equivalent.

What was clearedEvent writtenHow to treat it
Security logEvent ID 1102: the audit log was clearedNear-perfect detection: almost nothing legitimate clears it outright
System or other logsSystem Event ID 104: the log file was clearedSame severity when it clusters with other sabotage
Either, alongside T1490 or T1685Correlated by time and host countA near-certain pre-encryption signal, not a routine anomaly
The irony students enjoy: clearing the log writes a log. Make sure 1102 and 104 are forwarded and alerted on centrally, because the local copy is gone.
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle27 / 86

Correlate shadow deletion and log clearing across hosts

  • Two signal sets: shadow-copy deletion (T1490) and log clearing (T1685.005)
  • union puts both on one timeline, labelled by Signal
  • The fleet view: more than three distinct hosts in one 30-minute bin
The power is the fleet view: more than three hosts in a 30-minute bin. Event ID 1102 (and System Event ID 104) is itself a near-perfect detection, because almost nothing legitimate clears the security log outright.
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle28 / 86

The narrow benign case, and how to rule it out

Can be benign

Backup and imaging products create and remove shadow copies. A storage admin reclaiming space or a DR test can delete one on a single host.

Almost never benign

The same destructive command on many hosts within minutes, by an account with no business running it, right after credential access and lateral movement.

Check scope, the executing account, and what preceded it before you escalate or dismiss.

The qualifier 'broadly, across a fleet, in a short window' is carrying real weight in this detection.
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle29 / 86

Three checks before you escalate or dismiss

  1. 1ScopeOne host, or the same destructive command on many hosts within minutes?
  2. 2Executing accountDoes this account have any business running it? A backup product, or a service account acting interactively?
  3. 3What preceded itCredential access and lateral movement on the same hosts just before is what turns maintenance into an incident.
These three checks separate a storage admin reclaiming space from an affiliate preparing to encrypt. Write them into the triage note.

Worked example: a pre-encryption timeline in raw logs

FIN-SQL02, four log sources, eleven minutes. Watch which account keeps recurring.

HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle31 / 86

03:14 to 03:17: driver, cleared log, shadow copies gone

  • A kernel driver loading from Temp under a generic name: the BYOVD mechanism
  • 1102 two minutes later: the Security log is cleared
  • vssadmin one minute after that destroys the local recovery point
Each line could be innocent on a busy file server. Point at svc-backup01 appearing again and again.
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle32 / 86

Reading each line on its own

Each event could be innocent on a busy file server. Read what each one means first.

Time and eventWhat it recordsWhy it stands out
03:14 System 7045A service was installed: kernel driver RTCore64 from C:\Windows\TempA driver from Temp under a generic name is the BYOVD mechanism
03:16 Security 1102The audit log was cleared, by svc-backup01Almost nothing legitimate clears an audit log outright
03:17 Sysmon 1Process creation: vssadmin delete shadows /all /quietThe host's local recovery point is destroyed
03:22 Sysmon 11File create: a .lockbit file and a ransom noteEncryption has started; the outage has begun
Sysmon Event ID 1 is process creation and 11 is file create. Have students say the meaning of each line before you reveal the next slide.
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle33 / 86

03:22: encryption begins on the shared drive

  • Files reappear with a new extension
  • A ransom note dropped alongside them
  • Tens of thousands of writes in six minutes, same account
Sysmon Event ID 11 is file create. By this point the outage has begun; the earlier three lines were the window.
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle34 / 86

What makes the sequence unambiguous

Same account

svc-backup01 on every line. A service account has no reason to install kernel drivers or interactively delete shadow copies.

Same host

Everything happens on FIN-SQL02, a finance file server, so the events build one story.

Tight window

Driver install to full-blown encryption in under eleven minutes.

This is the shape to recognise: not each ATT&CK technique as an isolated fact, but same account, same host, short window.
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle35 / 86
Same account, same host, under eleven minutes: the correlation is the detection.

A service account has no reason to install kernel drivers or delete shadow copies. Isolate the host and disable the account now.

Do not wait for a file-encryption alert to confirm it. Four individually explainable events become a near-certain detonation in progress.

Section 6 of 12

Precursor detections: RMM, bulk cloud copy, service stops

T1219, T1567.002 and T1489: quieter signals hours to days earlier, worth hunting as a set.

HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle37 / 86

Three quieter precursors, each explainable alone

T1219

RMM tool appears

AnyDesk, ScreenConnect, Atera, Splashtop, TeamViewer: one you do not license, or first seen on a server, installed by a non-IT account from C:\Users\Public, C:\ProgramData or Temp.

T1567.002

Bulk cloud copy

rclone (often renamed) or the MEGA client. Match the command line, not the file name; a new rclone.conf is a finding. Hundreds of MB to GB out from a file server at night.

T1489, T1490

Mass service stops

Bursts of System 7036 (stopped) and 7040 (start type changed) across SQL, Exchange, backup and security services, plus recovery switched off beside shadow deletion.

Signed, often allow-listed RMM gives a persistent channel that survives a password reset. Byte counts for the cloud copy come from the firewall or proxy (bytes_out, sentbyte), not endpoint network events.
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle38 / 86

RMM tool (T1219): IT tool or intruder channel?

Affiliates like RMM because it is signed, often allow-listed, and survives a password reset. The detection is context, not existence.

CheckExpected IT useFinding
ProductOne your organisation licensesOne you do not license
HostWhere it has always runA server, for the first time
Installed byAn IT accountAn account that is not IT
LocationProgram FilesPortable binary in C:\Users\Public, C:\ProgramData or Temp
NetworkHosts that already use itVendor relay domains looked up from hosts that never contacted them

Telemetry: process creation (Sysmon 1, DeviceProcessEvents), service installs (System 7045), DNS or proxy lookups.

AnyDesk, ScreenConnect (ConnectWise Control), Atera, Splashtop and TeamViewer all appear in incident reports. Your licensed one is the baseline.
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle39 / 86

Bulk cloud copy (T1567.002): the double-extortion theft

Usually rclone, an open-source sync tool, or the MEGA client.

  • Match the command linerclone is often renamed to look like a system binary. copy or sync with --transfers, --multi-thread-streams or a mega: remote is the tell.
  • A config file appearsA new rclone.conf on a server is a finding on its own.
  • Volume and destinationHundreds of MB to GB outbound to mega.nz, a consumer cloud provider or a raw VPS IP.
  • Wrong host, wrong hourFrom a file server that normally only receives traffic, typically at night.
  • Where bytes come fromFirewall or proxy fields such as bytes_out or sentbyte, not endpoint network events.
This is the same detection as insider exfiltration. Who is copying does not change what the transfer looks like.
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle40 / 86

Mass service stops (T1489) and recovery switched off (T1490)

  1. 1The reasonA file held open with a lock cannot be encrypted, and running backup agents could restore it.
  2. 2Stop the servicesSQL Server, Exchange, backup (Veeam, Backup Exec) and security agents: net stop, taskkill /f.
  3. 3Keep them downsc config sets the start type to disabled, logged as System Event ID 7040.
  4. 4The log trailBursts of System Event ID 7036 (stopped state) across several services and hosts.
  5. 5Recovery offA bcdedit change with recoveryenabled no, beside the shadow-copy deletion.
One stopped service is patch night. Many critical services across several hosts in minutes, by an unexpected account, is the final playbook.
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle41 / 86

Trimmed from the lesson's hunt, which adds two more stages (recovery inhibited, log cleared) and account and first/last-seen columns. Three or more stages on one host, or two on several hosts within the same hours, means escalate and consider isolation before the shadow copies go.

Encryption, the hypervisor layer, and the RaaS model

T1486 and T1489, data theft first, ESXi as a multiplier, and brands that are toolkits.

HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle45 / 86

The final stage and its last tripwires

T1489

Service stop

Stop SQL Server, Exchange, backup agents: a locked file cannot be encrypted. Signature: a cluster of 7036 stopped-state events across critical services.

T1486

Encryption

Data Encrypted for Impact: the step the entire preceding chain was preparing for.

Double extortion

Exfiltrate first, so a victim with perfect backups still faces a leak-site threat. Detectable before encryption as large outbound transfers.

Exfiltration monitoring is mechanically identical whether the thief is an insider or an affiliate, which is why it is a ransomware control too. Watch for the rclone and MEGA patterns from the precursor section.
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle46 / 86

Double extortion: the theft happens first

  1. 1Steal during the dwellFinancial records, customer PII, internal communications leave during lateral movement and pre-encryption.
  2. 2Then encryptT1486 runs across the fleet as usual.
  3. 3Victim restoresEven perfect, untouched backups bring the files back.
  4. 4Leak-site pressureRefusing to pay now means the data is published or sold. The pressure survives the restore.

Step 1 is detectable before encryption begins: large outbound transfers to unfamiliar cloud or file-sharing destinations.

Link back to the rclone and MEGA patterns in section 6. That precursor is the double-extortion step.
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle47 / 86

The hypervisor layer multiplies the blast radius

Windows guests

The encryptor works one operating system at a time, one physical or virtual server after another.

ESXi hypervisor

An ESXi-targeting encryptor goes after the datastore files behind every VM on the host in one pass. Fifty VMs fall in the time one server would.

Hypervisors need the same rigorous patching and access control as a domain controller.

Unpatched, internet-facing or weakly credentialed hypervisor management interfaces are among the highest-value targets in an affiliate playbook.
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle48 / 86

One ESXi host, fifty VMs: its own logs are the only witness

No EDR on ESXi in most estates, and logs stay on the host unless Syslog.global.logHost forwards them.

SourceWhat to watch
/var/log/auth.logSSH and ESXi Shell logins; SSH to a host nobody administers that way is the first alert
/var/log/shell.logAll shell commands: VM listing, VMs killed or powered off, unsigned binaries allowed, /tmp runs
hostd.log / vobd.logHost agent and audit events, including esx.audit.ssh.enabled and mass VM power-state changes
vCenter eventsSSH enabled on hosts, many VMs powered off in minutes, AD-integrated admin group changes

An ESXi encryptor goes after the datastore files behind every VM, so fifty servers fall in the time one physical server would take. Hypervisors need domain-controller-grade patching and access control.
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle49 / 86

The pre-encryption sequence you can read in shell.log

Every command typed in the ESXi Shell or over SSH lands in /var/log/shell.log, if it is forwarded.

  • Inventory the VMsesxcli vm process list or vim-cmd vmsvc/getallvms from an unexpected session.
  • Stop the VMsesxcli vm process kill or vim-cmd vmsvc/power.off, so the VM disk files unlock.
  • Allow unsigned codeThe execInstalledOnly advanced setting, which blocks unsigned binaries, being turned off.
  • Run from /tmpA binary launched from /tmp right after that setting changed.
Each command is normal for an administrator. The sequence, from a session nobody expected over SSH, is the encryptor being prepared.
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle50 / 86

Ransomware is an industry with specialised roles

  1. 1Initial Access BrokerBreaks in via phishing, exposed RDP or an unpatched VPN, then sells the foothold on criminal markets.
  2. 2RaaS core operatorsBuild the encryptor, leak site and negotiation platform, and license the toolkit.
  3. 3AffiliatesRun the intrusions with the rented toolkit, commonly keeping around 70-80% of any ransom.
  4. 4Leak sitesThe enforcement arm of double extortion: stolen data is published if the victim refuses to pay.
The group that encrypts you may not be the group that got in.
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle51 / 86

Two consequences at the console

Big game hunting

Ransoms scale with ability to pay, so affiliates spend days hands-on-keyboard in one valuable organisation. That is why dwell and lateral movement exist.

Slippery attribution

LockBit, BlackCat/ALPHV or Akira name a toolkit and affiliate program, not one group. Two intrusions under one brand can differ in entry and tempo.

Treat the tradecraft in your own logs, not the brand name, as your evidence.

Triple extortion and beyond

DDoS pressure and direct stakeholder harassment added on top.

HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle53 / 86

Each extortion layer adds a pressure point

Extortion layerMechanismWhat it adds
SingleT1486: files encryptedAvailability loss; recoverable if backups are intact
Double+ data theft before encryption, leak-site threatPressure survives even a clean backup restore
Triple+ DDoS on public-facing infrastructure during negotiationA second, visible outage when pressure is highest

Triple extortion was pioneered in late 2020 by SunCrypt and RagnarLocker; Avaddon adopted it from January 2021. The DDoS does not need to be sustained, only visible at the worst moment.
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle54 / 86

How the triple-extortion DDoS is timed

  1. 1Encryption and leak threatThe first two levers are already in play.
  2. 2Negotiation stallsThe victim is not paying on the attacker's schedule.
  3. 3Short DDoS burstAgainst the website or a VPN gateway. It does not need to be sophisticated or sustained.
  4. 4A visible second outageCustomers see the site down, remote staff lose VPN, exactly when leadership is under most pressure.
SunCrypt and RagnarLocker pioneered this in the second half of 2020; Avaddon publicly announced from January 2021 that ignored deadlines would bring a DDoS.
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle55 / 86
The fourth lever takes disclosure out of the victim's hands.

Some operators contact the victim's own customers, partners, employees or regulators directly, forcing disclosure on the attacker's schedule.

Several vendors describe this informally as a fourth lever. It bypasses the organisation's crisis-communication timeline entirely.
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle56 / 86

Paying ends nothing, and the DDoS belongs to the same case

CISA AA25-071A

No guarantee

A Medusa victim who had paid was then contacted by a separate actor claiming the payment was stolen, demanding a second payment for the "real" decryptor.

SOC implication

A DDoS alert on a public asset during an active ransomware incident is a probable extension of the same extortion. Link it to that incident, not a new ticket.

Triage it in context. Treating the DDoS as unrelated loses the picture of what the attacker is doing to the negotiation.

Backups and recovery: the defence that works

Can you get the business back without the attacker's key?

HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle58 / 86

3-2-1, and why only one copy survives

3 copies (live plus two backups), 2 media or systems, 1 off-site and offline or immutable. That last word decides the outcome.
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle59 / 86

The 3-2-1 rule, digit by digit

DigitMeaningWhat it protects against
3 copiesThe live copy plus two backupsOne corrupted or failed backup
2 media or systemsTwo different types of media or systemsOne platform failing or being reached
1 off-site and offlineOff-site and offline, or immutableAn attacker with domain admin: the copy that decides the outcome
A competent attacker who reaches domain admin deliberately hunts backups. Only the last digit is designed with that attacker in mind.
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle60 / 86

Reachability decides which backup survives

Connected backups

Domain backup server, mapped drive, online share: reachable, so encrypted or deleted too, often after vssadmin delete shadows.

Offline or immutable

Air-gapped tape, or cloud object storage with immutability or object-lock that even an admin cannot alter. This copy restores you.

A service account suddenly touching the backup server is both an alarm and a race. Catching it is the difference between a clean restore and a catastrophe.
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle61 / 86

Two truths that make backups a capability

  • Untested is not a backupUntested backups fail silently. Recovery is a rehearsed drill measured by RTO (time to operational) and RPO (data you can afford to lose).
  • Beats encryption, not extortionRestoring does nothing to un-leak stolen data, which is why DLP and egress monitoring matter even with flawless backups.
RTO is Recovery Time Objective; RPO is Recovery Point Objective.
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle62 / 86
An attack on backups is both an alarm and a race.

Mass shadow-copy deletion or a service account suddenly touching the backup server: catching it is often the difference between a clean restore and a catastrophe.

This reframes the pre-encryption window from section 4 in business terms: what you protect by catching T1490 is the recovery itself.

Section 10 of 12

Intermittent encryption and exfiltration-only extortion

Two trends built specifically to blunt the detections above.

HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle64 / 86

Two trends that evade encryption-centric detection

TrendWhat changedWhy it matters to detection
Intermittent encryptionOnly a fraction of each file's bytes are encryptedEvades entropy-based and full-rewrite canary detections; files still unusable
Exfiltration-only extortionNo encryption step at all, just the data-leak threatT1486/T1490/T1685/T1685.005 never fire; exfil monitoring catches it
Intermittent encryption first appeared with LockFile in mid-2021 and is now marketed by BlackCat/ALPHV, LockBit, Black Basta, Akira, Play and Agenda for speed and stealth. Re-tune canary and entropy detections for partial writes.
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle65 / 86

How intermittent encryption works

  1. 1Skip, do not rewriteThe encryptor encrypts alternating blocks, for example the first block of every few kilobytes.
  2. 2Faster across the fleetLess work per file shrinks the window between first file and full-fleet impact.
  3. 3Still unusableTo the user, the file is corrupted and will not open.
  4. 4Looks like a sparse writeThe change pattern is a partial write, not a full read-then-overwrite.
First seen with LockFile in mid-2021. Several RaaS groups market it to affiliates as a toolkit feature, each with its own byte-skipping configuration.
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle66 / 86

Why entropy and canary detections can miss it

Entropy is a statistical measure of how random a file's bytes look, and therefore how likely it is to be encrypted.

Full encryption

Every byte rewritten: high entropy, a clear read-then-overwrite pattern. Classic canary and entropy detections score it well.

Intermittent encryption

Most bytes untouched: the file can under-score on entropy and never show a full rewrite.

Analyst action

Re-tune canary, honeypot-file and entropy detections for partial-file modification. Do not assume they catch every variant.

These detections have historically been a reliable last line. The trend was built specifically to blunt them.
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle67 / 86
Some crews now skip encryption entirely.

Exfiltration-only extortion: steal the data, threaten to publish it, and never run an encryptor.

This attacks the model the lesson built so far. Everything in sections 4 to 6 assumed an encryption event was coming.
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle68 / 86

Exfiltration-only in practice

BianLian

Shifted primarily to exfiltration-based extortion around January 2023 and exclusively by around January 2024, dropping its encryptor.

Cl0p

2023 mass exploitation of GoAnywhere MFT and MOVEit Transfer (CVE-2023-34362): exploit the app, take the data, extort. No lateral movement phase.

To the SOC the whole attack can look like one large outbound transfer. Exfiltration monitoring is a co-equal detection layer.

This is the Achilles heel of a SOC that over-indexed on encryption and sabotage signals.
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle69 / 86

SignalFires?Why

The same holds for T1685.005 log clearing in this pattern. Exfiltration monitoring is a mandatory co-equal layer, not a secondary one.

Section 11 of 12

The decision to pay

Not a clean way out, and never a technician's call.

HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle71 / 86

The case against paying, unpacked

  • Not a guaranteeDecryptors are often slow, buggy or fail on some files; a meaningful share of paying victims never fully recover.
  • Funds the next attackEvery ransom finances the RaaS ecosystem and marks the payer as someone who pays, inviting repeat targeting.
  • It can be illegalPaying sanctioned groups or individuals can itself be a legal violation, which is why legal counsel joins early.
  • Double extortion breaks itThere is no reliable way to prove the attacker deleted the stolen copy, so paying does not end the leak exposure.
An analyst is rarely the decision-maker, but understanding these trade-offs makes you a credible voice when a business leader asks "should we just pay?".
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle72 / 86

When paying is considered, and who decides

The narrow case for

Raw survival: backups failed or were destroyed, lives or critical services are at risk, and downtime outweighs everything else.

Who decides

A business-continuity decision with legal, executive and often law-enforcement input. Never a technician's call.

The SOC's role is to supply facts: scope, whether backups survived, whether data left the network.
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle73 / 86
The decision to pay is decided long before the incident, by the preparation.

Tested offline backups, a written IR plan, pre-arranged legal and IR contacts, and a rehearsed scenario rarely leave you cornered.

Prevention closes the door; resilience means an attacker who gets in still cannot force your hand.

The ransomware IR playbook

Containment, negotiation and recovery, following CISA's #StopRansomware Guide.

HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle75 / 86

Six phases and who owns each

PhaseCore actionWho owns it
1. ContainmentIsolate hosts (disconnect, do not power off), disable compromised and new accountsSOC / IR team
2. Evidence preservationForensic images or memory captures, full log export for the dwell periodSOC / forensics
3. Stakeholder engagementLegal, external IR firm, cyber-insurance carrier, law enforcement reportLegal / executive leadership
4. Negotiation (if pursued)Sanctions check, proof-of-life decryption test, then price and logisticsProfessional negotiator, not the SOC
5. Eradication before restoreClose the entry point, rotate credentials, then restore from offline or immutable backupIR team / infrastructure

Disconnect from wired and wireless networks rather than power off: powering off destroys memory, running processes and live connections. Power down only if you cannot isolate any other way. Insurers may need to approve the IR firm and negotiator.
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle76 / 86

Containment in the first minutes

  1. 1Isolate, do not power offDisconnect from wired and wireless networks. Isolation stops the spread and keeps evidence.
  2. 2Why not power offIt destroys memory, running processes and live connections that show how the attacker got in.
  3. 3Power down only as last resortOnly if the host cannot be isolated any other way.
  4. 4Disable accountsThe compromised accounts and any new accounts the attacker created for persistence.
  5. 5Find every touched hostSweep the pre-encryption indicators, not just hosts already showing encrypted files.
A host left connected keeps spreading the encryptor or keeps an attacker session alive. Isolation is the middle path between that and pulling the plug.
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle77 / 86

Preserve evidence, then bring in the right people

Evidence first

Forensic images or memory captures of a sample of affected hosts, plus SIEM, EDR and authentication logs for the whole dwell period.

  • Establishes the initial access vector
  • Without it, restores get re-compromised

People, from hour one

Legal (notification, insurance, sanctions), an external IR firm, the cyber-insurance carrier, and a law-enforcement report.

  • Insurers may approve or select the IR firm
  • Reporting does not oblige any course of action
Per CISA and FBI guidance, reporting gives investigators visibility that can help identify the actor, recover funds in some cases and support other victims.
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle78 / 86

Ransom-note triage: which family, and is there a decryptor?

  • The identifiersNote file name (e.g. README.txt), the appended extension, the Tor leak-site or chat URL, any Tox or email contact.
  • Identify the familyID Ransomware or No More Ransom's Crypto Sheriff return the likely family: hunt its public IOCs and TTPs.
  • Check for a decryptorNo More Ransom lists free decryptors for broken or seized families. Most current families have none: a bonus, not a plan.
  • Three cautionsCopy from an isolated host or image; check with the IR lead before uploading a victim-specific ID; nobody in the SOC contacts the attacker.
No More Ransom (nomoreransom.org) is run by Europol and partners. A note's victim ID can let anyone holding it open the negotiation portal, which is why uploading it is the IR lead's call.
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle79 / 86

If negotiation happens, specialists run it

  1. 1Sanctions checkConfirm the actor or wallet is not on a government sanctions list, which can make payment illegal.
  2. 2Proof-of-lifeThe attacker decrypts one or two non-sensitive sample files to prove the key works.
  3. 3Price and logisticsOnly then. Handled by a professional negotiator, never an executive messaging the attacker.

This reduces the risk of paying for nothing, but as the Medusa case showed, does not eliminate it.

Nobody in the SOC contacts the attacker. That boundary matters as much as any technical step.
HACKTHESOCLesson 31Ransomware lifecycle80 / 86
Restore before eradication and you hand the attacker a fresh environment to re-encrypt.

Close the entry point, rotate every credential the attacker may have touched, then restore from offline or immutable copies.

An unpatched VPN, a phished credential never rotated, or a leftover web shell all survive a restore. The same affiliate can return through the same door.

Lessons learned: the questions that close the loop

  • Initial accessWhich initial-access control failed?
  • DetectionWhich pre-encryption signal fired late, or not at all?
  • ResilienceWhich backup or segmentation gap turned a contained intrusion into an outage?
  • If you skip itThe same gap is exploited again, sometimes by the same affiliate returning through the same unpatched door.
Treat lessons learned as mandatory, owned by SOC leadership, and feeding new detections back into sections 4 and 6.

Key takeaways

  1. 01Encryption is the end of the chain; the last real chance is pre-encryption sabotage: T1490, T1685, T1685.005.
  2. 02Fleet-wide vssadmin delete shadows, mass EDR disconnection and Event ID 1102 have almost no benign explanation.
  3. 03Hunt precursors as a set (RMM tool, bulk cloud copy, service stops) and score hosts by distinct stages in 24h.
  4. 04Double extortion and exfiltration-only groups make outbound-transfer monitoring a co-equal ransomware detection.
  5. 05ESXi has no EDR: forward its syslog. RaaS brands are rented tools; treat tradecraft as the evidence.
  6. 06Only offline or immutable, tested backups survive; eradicate the entry point before you restore.
