TCP/IP stack fundamentals for detection

Every header field is a hunting opportunity.

HACKTHESOCLesson 16Protocols for detection6 / 92

Headers are metadata, the payload is the message

Four practical layers: Link (Ethernet, ARP), Internet (IP, ICMP), Transport (TCP, UDP), Application (HTTP, DNS, SMB). Each adds a header you can query.
HACKTHESOCLesson 16Protocols for detection7 / 92

What each layer gives a detection engineer

  • TransportTCP handshake and state: half-open connections, RST floods, odd flag combos. UDP is fire-and-forget, loved by DNS and beacons.
  • Internetsource.ip and destination.ip to pivot on; TTL hints at OS; fragmentation can evade IDS.
  • LinkARP spoofing and gratuitous ARP precede man-in-the-middle (T1557.002).
  • Across layersnetwork.protocol=ssh on port 443 is SSH tunneled to evade egress filtering.
An RST from your host right after an external connection attempt means egress filtering blocked it, which tells you something on that host tried to call out.
HACKTHESOCLesson 16Protocols for detection8 / 92

Same port, different verdict: correlate the layers

destination.port is what the traffic claims. network.protocol is what Zeek or the NGFW actually saw.

destination.portnetwork.protocol observedVerdict
443ssl (TLS)Agrees: ordinary HTTPS
443sshSSH tunnelled over 443 to slip past egress filtering

443 alone tells you almost nothing: it is the most common destination port on the internet. The value comes from comparing it with the protocol actually observed.
HACKTHESOCLesson 16Protocols for detection9 / 92

Byte ratio: the exfiltration tell

Browsing is bytes_in much greater than bytes_out. 4 GB out and 50 KB back is screaming exfiltration, even on 443. You want session records, which pair both directions, not one-way flows.
HACKTHESOCLesson 16Protocols for detection10 / 92

Flows, sessions and the shape of exfiltration

Flow

A one-way summary of packets between two endpoints. You only see one direction, so you cannot compare what went out with what came back.

Session

Pairs client-to-server and server-to-client. Now bytes_out and bytes_in sit on one row, and their ratio becomes evidence.

The two shapes

Browsing: bytes_in far larger than bytes_out. Exfiltration: 4 GB out, 50 KB back, even on port 443.

  • Query: bytes_out > 50 MB and ratio > 10
Backups and large uploads to approved cloud storage also have a high ratio. Check the destination against known services before escalating.

TCP vs UDP: flags, reliability, port numbers

Concrete enough to use in a detection rule.

HACKTHESOCLesson 16Protocols for detection12 / 92

Six TCP flags and what they signal

FlagMeaningWhat it signals
SYNStart a new connectionAlone, never answered: the fingerprint of a port scan
ACKConfirms receipt of prior dataMissing on an established session is abnormal
FINGracefully closeA clean FIN/ACK exchange ends a normal session
RSTAbort immediatelyNothing listening, or a firewall killed it
PSHDeliver buffered data nowPackets carrying payload, like the TLS Client Hello
URGUrgent dataRare today; unexpected URG can mean evasive packet crafting
A host that consistently RSTs its own outbound connections a few packets in is worth baselining: it can be a C2 client aborting on a sinkholed or seized server.
HACKTHESOCLesson 16Protocols for detection13 / 92

How TCP stays reliable, and how it ends

  1. 1Sequence numbersEvery byte is numbered, so the receiver can detect gaps and reorder packets.
  2. 2Acknowledgment numbersThe receiver says which byte it expects next, so missing data is resent.
  3. 3Sliding windowEach side advertises how much unacknowledged data it can buffer.
  4. 4Graceful closeFIN, ACK, FIN, ACK: four packets, both sides agree to stop.
  5. 5Abrupt closeOne RST, from either side, with no negotiation.
None of the first three exists in UDP. That missing state is exactly why UDP beacons and amplification floods are harder to fingerprint with stateful rules.
HACKTHESOCLesson 16Protocols for detection14 / 92

Reliable TCP vs lightweight UDP

TCP

Sequence numbers, acknowledgment numbers and a sliding window. Minimum 20-byte header. State a firewall can track.

UDP

Four fields: ports, length, checksum. No handshake, no ack, no retransmit. DNS, NTP, SNMP, many C2 frameworks and DDoS tools favor it.

No connection state means UDP beacons and amplification floods are harder to fingerprint with stateful rules.

Graceful close is four packets (FIN, ACK, FIN, ACK); an abrupt close is a single RST.
HACKTHESOCLesson 16Protocols for detection15 / 92

Scanners and UDP floods, read from conn.log

  • Zeek's history letters: S = SYN, R = RST, F = FIN seen
  • Scan: many RSTs across many ports from one source
  • Flood or amplification: huge UDP volume to very few targets
The 10-minute bucket separates the two failure modes per source. Tune the thresholds to your own baseline before alerting.
HACKTHESOCLesson 16Protocols for detection16 / 92

Port numbers as evidence: the doors attackers use

Servers sit on well-known (0-1023) or registered (1024-49151) ports; the client uses an ephemeral port.

PortProtocolWhy it matters
22, 53, 853SSH, DNS, DNS over TLSRemote access, name resolution and its encrypted form
88KerberosTicket requests: the credential-attack protocol
135RPC endpoint mapperThen a dynamic high port for WMI and DCOM
389/636, 3268/3269LDAP, LDAPS, Global CatalogActive Directory queries and reconnaissance
443, 445HTTPS, SMBC2 over the web; file shares and lateral movement
3389, 5985/5986RDP (TCP and UDP), WinRMInteractive and PowerShell remoting

Ephemeral ranges differ: Windows 49152-65535, Linux 32768-60999 (net.ipv4.ip_local_port_range). A Windows-tuned rule misfires on Linux.

Database ports such as 1433, 3306 and 6379 matter when they appear from a source outside the database tier. A host listening on an ephemeral port, or a workstation accepting 445, 3389 or 5985, is the role-reversal anomaly covered later.
HACKTHESOCLesson 16Protocols for detection17 / 92

Two port facts that break naive rules

Ephemeral ranges differ

Windows clients use 49152-65535; Linux defaults to 32768-60999 (net.ipv4.ip_local_port_range). A 'high port' rule tuned on Windows misfires on Linux.

Role reversal

A host that starts listening on an ephemeral port, or a workstation that accepts connections on 445, 3389 or 5985, is acting as a server it was never meant to be.

Client on the ephemeral port, server on the well-known one: that is normal. Any reversal of that pattern deserves a look.

A TCP handshake, packet by packet

Client 10.14.22.108:51422 to server 93.184.216.34:443, captured with tcpdump.

  1. 1[S] SYNClient: I want to start a conversation.
  2. 2[S.] SYN-ACKServer from 443: got it, and I want to too.
  3. 3[.] ACKClient confirms. Only now can real data flow.
  4. 4[P.] PSH-ACK, length 517First data: on 443, almost always the TLS Client Hello.
A port scan in the same format is line 1 alone, a lone [S] repeated against port after port, with no SYN-ACK ever. Visible from shape alone, no payload needed.

ICMP: diagnostics, recon and covert channels

No ports, IP protocol number 1, and rarely inspected.

HACKTHESOCLesson 16Protocols for detection24 / 92
ICMP has no ports. It rides directly inside IP as protocol number 1, every host must speak it, and few inspect it.

ICMP (Internet Control Message Protocol, RFC 792) exists so routers and hosts can report errors and diagnostics back to a sender.

No ports means no port-based firewall rule to key on, which is part of why ICMP is one of the least-inspected protocols on many networks.
HACKTHESOCLesson 16Protocols for detection25 / 92

ICMP types to recognize

Type / codeNameMeaning
8 / 0Echo RequestA ping
0 / 0Echo ReplyThe response to a ping
3 / 0, 1Network or Host UnreachableNo route to the network, or the host cannot be reached
3 / 3Port UnreachableUDP hit a closed port
3 / 13Administratively ProhibitedA firewall explicitly blocked it: a rule fired
5 / 0 to 3RedirectA router says use a different next hop
11 / 0Time ExceededTTL hit zero in transit: how traceroute works
A ping sweep across a subnet maps live hosts: T1018 Remote System Discovery. Traceroute chains Type 11 replies with TTL 1, 2, 3 to map the path.
HACKTHESOCLesson 16Protocols for detection26 / 92

Diagnostics that double as reconnaissance

  1. 1PingA Type 8 Echo Request answered by a Type 0 Echo Reply.
  2. 2Ping sweepEcho Requests to every address in a subnet map live hosts: T1018 Remote System Discovery.
  3. 3Traceroute sends TTL 1, 2, 3...Each router decrements the TTL; where it hits zero, that router answers.
  4. 4Type 11 replies map the pathChaining the Time Exceeded replies reveals every hop to the destination.
A sweep from a workstation that never does network diagnostics is the reconnaissance signal; from a monitoring server it is routine.
HACKTHESOCLesson 16Protocols for detection27 / 92

ICMP tunneling: the payload is the tell

  • Default ping: Windows 32 bytes, Linux 56 bytes
  • Hundreds of high-entropy bytes in a sustained exchange: not a ping
  • T1095 Non-Application Layer Protocol: icmpsh, ptunnel, Anchor
Add a count of Echo Requests with no matching reply, or unsolicited replies. ICMP volume is normally tiny, so this costs almost nothing to run.
HACKTHESOCLesson 16Protocols for detection28 / 92

A real ping vs a tunnel inside ping

Diagnostic ping

Small, fixed payload: Windows sends 32 bytes of a repeating alphabet, Linux 56 bytes of incrementing values. Each request gets one reply.

ICMP tunnel (T1095)

Hundreds of bytes of high-entropy or structured data, in a sustained back-and-forth. Requests with no reply, or unsolicited replies.

  • Seen in icmpsh, ptunnel
  • Used by the Anchor malware family
RFC 792 allows Echo packets to carry an arbitrary payload, which is the gap these tools use. Payload size is the cheapest, strongest tell.

DNS abuse and tunneling (T1071.004)

Allowed outbound everywhere, rarely inspected, carries arbitrary data.

HACKTHESOCLesson 16Protocols for detection30 / 92

Why attackers love DNS, and the three jobs it does for them

  • Always allowedOutbound DNS works from virtually every network, and it is rarely inspected at the application layer.
  • Carries dataArbitrary data fits in subdomain labels and TXT records.
  • C2 channelT1071.004 Application Layer Protocol: DNS.
  • ExfiltrationT1048.003 over an alternative protocol, or T1041 over the C2 channel.
  • Finding infrastructureT1568 Dynamic Resolution, including DGA domains.
If your SOC is not actively hunting DNS, ransomware operators, APTs and commodity malware all share that blind spot with you.

Four DNS hunts every SOC should run

  • Long query namesdnscat2 and Iodine encode data in labels: 100+ characters. Flag p95 over 50 or max over 100, plus many unique subdomains.
  • Unusual query typesHundreds of TXT, NULL or CNAME queries per hour from one workstation. dnscat2 prefers TXT and MX.
  • New and DGA domainsEmotet, Qakbot, Trickbot rotate pseudo-random domains. Even 8+ consonants in a row catches some DGA.
  • Resolver bypassInternal source, port 53, destination not an approved resolver: catches malware hardcoding 8.8.8.8 or 1.1.1.1.
The resolver-bypass rule is cheap and catches a surprising amount of malware that avoids corporate DNS logging.
HACKTHESOCLesson 16Protocols for detection33 / 92

What a tunnel query looks like next to a normal one

  • Normal names are short and human-typable
  • Tunnel labels are long, unique per query, under one parent domain
  • TXT and MX carry more payload, so tunnels prefer them
Thresholds from the lesson: 95th-percentile query length over 50 characters, or a maximum over 100, plus many unique subdomains in a short window.
HACKTHESOCLesson 16Protocols for detection34 / 92

HACKTHESOCLesson 16Protocols for detection35 / 92

Hunting DNS tunneling in Zeek

  • Many unique long subdomains under one parent domain
  • Check query_types for TXT-heavy patterns
  • Aggregate per asking host, not per query
DGA produces many different parent domains; tunneling produces many subdomains under one parent. That difference matters in the quiz.
HACKTHESOCLesson 16Protocols for detection36 / 92

The cheapest DNS rule: resolver bypass

  1. 1Source is internalsource.ip inside your address space.
  2. 2Destination port is 53Plain DNS, UDP or TCP.
  3. 3Destination is not approveddestination.ip not in your allow-list of corporate resolvers.
  4. 4AlertCatches malware that hardcodes 8.8.8.8 or 1.1.1.1 to avoid corporate DNS logging.
TCP/53 with a long payload straight to an external nameserver is worse still. Either it is misconfigured or it is compromised; both need fixing.

HTTP and HTTPS analysis for C2

Think probabilistically: behavior browsers do not exhibit.

HACKTHESOCLesson 16Protocols for detection38 / 92

Fingerprints C2 tooling leaves in web traffic

Cobalt Strike, Sliver, Mythic, Brute Ratel, Havoc (T1071.001).

  • User-AgentRare or new strings, empty UAs, curl or Python-urllib from non-developers, Chrome claims over HTTP/1.0.
  • BeaconingPolling every 58 to 62 seconds for 8 hours, with tightly clustered small requests and responses.
  • JA3 / JA3STLS Client Hello hashes. Match known-bad lists like abuse.ch SSLBL, and flag rare JA3 values.
  • Response codesMany 404s: scanning. Many 200s with tiny bodies to one external IP: beaconing.
  • Originating processJoin Sysmon Event ID 3: is it chrome.exe, or rundll32.exe?
Key fields: http.request.method, url.full, http.response.status_code, user agent, tls.client.ja3. Many commodity families share a Go TLS stack, so one JA3 can cover several.
HACKTHESOCLesson 16Protocols for detection39 / 92

Browser traffic vs an implant's traffic

SignalA real browserA C2 implant
User-AgentCommon, consistent with the requestRare, empty, curl, or 'Chrome' over HTTP/1.0
TimingBursty and human-pacedEvery 58-62 seconds for 8 hours
SizesVaried pages and assetsSmall request, small response, tightly clustered
Status codesMixedMany 200s with tiny bodies to one external IP
Process (Sysmon Event ID 3)chrome.exerundll32.exe or another non-browser
No single row is proof. Two or three rows agreeing, plus the originating process, is what moves a connection from odd to malicious.
HACKTHESOCLesson 16Protocols for detection40 / 92

HTTPS beaconing by interval regularity

  • streamstats last(ts) finds each connection's gap to the previous one, per (src, dst, port)
  • Low stdev relative to mean is machine-paced
  • Browsing is bursty and human-paced
Pair interval regularity with tightly clustered byte sizes and the false-positive rate drops dramatically.
HACKTHESOCLesson 16Protocols for detection41 / 92

How the beacon query scores regularity

  1. 1Sort by tuple and timeGroup connections by source, destination and port, oldest first.
  2. 2Measure each gapstreamstats last(ts) gives the previous connection; delta is the gap.
  3. 3Summarise the gapsAverage and standard deviation per tuple, with over 30 connections.
  4. 4Score itbeacon_score = stdev / avg; under 0.2 is machine-paced.
  5. 5Read an exampleEvery 58-62 s: average about 60, deviation about 1.4, score about 0.02.
Human browsing produces a high score because gaps vary wildly. Add tightly clustered byte sizes and the false-positive rate drops sharply.

What encryption hides: DoH, DoT, QUIC, TLS metadata

Three changes take away the payload; the metadata that remains is what you hunt.

HACKTHESOCLesson 16Protocols for detection43 / 92

Encrypted DNS and QUIC blind the classic hunts

TCP 853

DoT

DNS over TLS runs on its own port, so it is easy to see and easy to block.

HTTPS to a resolver

DoH

Queries go as HTTPS to dns.google, cloudflare-dns.com or dns.quad9.net, path /dns-query. Bypasses the corporate resolver, so the DNS hunts see nothing.

UDP 443

QUIC and HTTP/3

TLS 1.3 built into the transport. TCP-intercepting inspection sees none of it. Zeek quic.log keeps version, server_name, client_protocol (h3), history.

Controls: block 853, block or proxy public DoH resolvers, set browser policy (DnsOverHttpsMode, Firefox canary use-application-dns.net).

What is left for DoH is the SNI naming a public resolver, the resolver's IP, and the process from EDR. A DoH connection from a process that is not a browser is worth a look. Many organisations block outbound UDP 443 so browsers fall back to TCP and TLS.
HACKTHESOCLesson 16Protocols for detection44 / 92

Who is using DoH or DoT instead of your resolver?

  • DoH is only visible as TLS to a known resolver name
  • DoT is easy: its own port, TCP 853
  • Next pivot: the process from EDR. Not a browser? Look closer
The resolver-bypass rule from the DNS section never fires for DoH, because no packet goes to port 53. This query fills that gap.
HACKTHESOCLesson 16Protocols for detection45 / 92

When the payload is encrypted, the handshake still talks

Zeek ssl.log: version, cipher, server_name, next_protocol, established; the certificate links to x509.log.

  • No SNI at allTo an external IP. Browsers always send it; a lot of malware that connects by raw IP does not.
  • Self-signed or very new certSubject equal to issuer, validity that started days ago, or san.dns that does not cover the SNI.
  • SNI and Host disagreeDomain fronting, or a well-known CDN's SNI paired with a destination IP outside that CDN's ranges.
  • ALPN missing or oddA browser negotiates h2; a C2 implant often negotiates nothing.
JA3 got noisier after Chrome began randomising TLS extension order in 2023; JA4 sorts extensions first and is more stable. Encrypted Client Hello hides the real SNI where deployed, so SNI is strong evidence today and a shrinking one tomorrow.
HACKTHESOCLesson 16Protocols for detection46 / 92

External TLS with no SNI or a self-signed certificate

subject and issuer sit in ssl.log only on Zeek versions that still log them there; on newer versions join x509.log through the certificate fingerprint. The lesson pairs this with a hunt for sessions to public DoH resolvers or port 853.
HACKTHESOCLesson 16Protocols for detection47 / 92

EvidenceWhat changedWhat to do

Plaintext vs encrypted protocol pairs

When the old protocol shows up at all, that is the finding.

HACKTHESOCLesson 16Protocols for detection49 / 92

The classic pairs

PlaintextEncrypted replacementKey distinction
Telnet 23/tcpSSH 22/tcpNot encrypted Telnet: a different suite replacing its use case
FTP 21 control, 20 dataFTPS 21 or 990/tcpFTP wrapped in TLS: same commands, same two channels
FTP 21/tcpSFTP 22/tcpNot FTP at all: a file subsystem inside an SSH session
HTTP 80/tcpHTTPS 443/tcpThe same pattern for the web

The SFTP name is a trap: it shares no wire protocol with FTP or FTPS.
HACKTHESOCLesson 16Protocols for detection50 / 92

Why the plaintext version is a standing risk

What is exposed

FTP logs in with a two-line USER then PASS exchange in plain ASCII. Telnet exposes every keystroke of the whole session, including the login.

Who can read it

Anyone on the path: a compromised switch, an AiTM position from ARP or DHCP spoofing, a rogue access point. No cracking needed: T1040 Network Sniffing.

This links forward to section 11: rogue DHCP puts the attacker in the path, and any plaintext protocol is then readable. The final quiz uses that chain.
HACKTHESOCLesson 16Protocols for detection51 / 92

FTP sends credentials in the clear

  • No decryption or cracking needed: this is T1040 Network Sniffing
  • Telnet exposes every keystroke the same way
  • Alert on presence: Zeek service=ftp or service=telnet
There is essentially no legitimate reason for Telnet or plain FTP on a modern network. It is either forgotten legacy kit or a planted listener.
HACKTHESOCLesson 16Protocols for detection52 / 92
For Telnet and plain FTP, alert on presence, not misuse.

Confirm with Zeek service=ftp or service=telnet from protocol inspection, not port 21 or 23 alone. Either it is legacy kit to retire, or a planted listener.

SSH, SFTP, FTPS and HTTPS have been standard for years, so a single confirmed session is worth a ticket on its own.

SMB and lateral movement (T1021.002)

Catch ransomware operators in the dwell time before encryption.

HACKTHESOCLesson 16Protocols for detection54 / 92

How SMB carries an intrusion from host to host

  1. 1Enumerate sharesT1135 Network Share Discovery: find data and pivot targets.
  2. 2Reach admin sharesC$, ADMIN$, IPC$: T1021.002 SMB/Windows Admin Shares.
  3. 3Copy the payloadT1570 Lateral Tool Transfer onto the target.
  4. 4Execute remotelyPsExec, smbexec or wmiexec start it as a service or process.
  5. 5Spread before encryptingThis is the dwell time where ransomware operators can be caught.
Each step leaves different evidence; the next slides show where each one is logged.
HACKTHESOCLesson 16Protocols for detection55 / 92

Where SMB evidence comes from

  • Zeeksmb_files.log, smb_mapping.log: path, name, action, share_type.
  • Windows Security5140 share accessed, 5145 detailed: ShareName, AccessMask, IpAddress.
  • SysmonEvent ID 3 network connection to port 445.
  • NDRsmb.command, smb.share, smb.filename, smb.dialect.
Adversaries use SMB to enumerate shares (T1135), reach admin shares (T1021.002), copy payloads (T1570) and execute via PsExec, smbexec or wmiexec.
HACKTHESOCLesson 16Protocols for detection56 / 92

Four SMB detections

Admin share access

Workstation-to-workstation C$, ADMIN$, IPC$ is almost never legit. Baseline server tooling; pair with 4624 LogonType=3.

Enumeration bursts

Normal: 2 to 5 SMB servers a day. 50 in 10 minutes: SharpHound, CrackMapExec, smbclient.

Suspicious files

PSEXESVC.exe on ADMIN$, or .exe, .bat, .vbs, .ps1 written to admin shares. Correlate with 7045 service creation.

Dialect downgrade

SMBv1 should not exist (EternalBlue). Alert below SMBv2, and on signing disabled (NTLM relay, T1557.001).

Layering share access, file write and service creation on the destination builds a multi-stage correlation rule.

Spray on 445, confirmed by network logons

HACKTHESOCLesson 16Protocols for detection67 / 92

How ARP works, and how spoofing bends it

  1. 1Who has 10.14.22.1?A host broadcasts an ARP request to the whole local segment.
  2. 2Owner replies with its MAC'10.14.22.1 is at aa:bb:cc:dd:ee:ff', and every host caches the answer.
  3. 3Attacker sends a gratuitous ARPUnsolicited, claiming the gateway's IP is at the attacker's MAC.
  4. 4Caches update without checksARP has no authentication, so hosts believe it.
  5. 5Traffic flows via the attackerAdversary-in-the-middle from a couple of forged broadcasts.
Detection tell: one IP mapped to two MACs, especially when the new MAC's vendor OUI (first three bytes) does not match your gateway hardware.
HACKTHESOCLesson 16Protocols for detection68 / 92

Two protocols with no authentication

T1557.002

ARP spoofing

Any host can send a gratuitous ARP claiming the gateway's IP. Tell: one IP mapped to two MACs, or a MAC whose vendor OUI is not your gateway's.

T1557.003

Rogue DHCP

A rogue server races the real one and hands out an attacker gateway and DNS. Starvation exhausts the real pool first.

ARP (RFC 826) resolves IP to MAC on the local segment. DHCP runs on UDP 67 server and 68 client, starting with a broadcast because a new host has no address yet.
HACKTHESOCLesson 16Protocols for detection69 / 92

DHCP's four-step DORA handshake

  1. 1DiscoverClient broadcasts: is any DHCP server out there?
  2. 2OfferA server offers an IP address, gateway and DNS server.
  3. 3RequestClient requests the lease it chose.
  4. 4AcknowledgeThe chosen server confirms the lease.
The first Offer a client accepts sets its gateway and DNS, and nothing authenticates which server sent it. Detection: two servers answering one transaction, or a gateway or DNS outside approved infrastructure.

Email: SMTP, SPF, DKIM, DMARC

SMTP was built with no way to verify the sender.

HACKTHESOCLesson 16Protocols for detection77 / 92

Three checks, three angles

SPF

Is the sending server's IP on the domain's published DNS list? Validates the server, not the message.

DKIM

Is the message signed by the domain and unaltered? Public key in DNS. Validates integrity and origin.

DMARC

Do SPF or DKIM align with the visible From? Sets policy: p=none, p=quarantine or p=reject.

Anyone can put any address in the SMTP From field. These three layers were bolted on to fix that.
HACKTHESOCLesson 16Protocols for detection78 / 92

How a receiving server checks one message

  1. 1Mail arrives over SMTPFrom a server IP, claiming a domain in the visible From.
  2. 2SPF checkIs that IP on the list the domain publishes in DNS?
  3. 3DKIM checkDoes the signature verify with the domain's public key from DNS, unaltered?
  4. 4DMARC checkDoes SPF or DKIM pass AND align with the visible From domain?
  5. 5Apply the policyp=none monitor, p=quarantine spam, p=reject block.
SPF (Sender Policy Framework), DKIM (DomainKeys Identified Mail), DMARC (Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting and Conformance).

Protocol anomalies: the wrong door

The port is a claim. Content and behavior reveal the truth.

HACKTHESOCLesson 16Protocols for detection86 / 92

Four shapes of protocol mismatch

Port-service mismatch

Non-TLS data or an SSH handshake on 443, a raw tunnel on 53.

Right protocol, wrong role

A workstation serving SMB to peers, or suddenly acting as a DNS resolver.

Non-standard port

SSH on 2222, RDP on a high port, to dodge port-based rules.

Volume and rhythm

DNS carrying far too much data, ICMP carrying a payload.

Baseline and deviation: know normal for this port and host. Tools like Zeek identify the actual protocol regardless of port.

Attackers run C2 on 443 because it is almost always allowed, but the content often betrays that it is not web traffic.
HACKTHESOCLesson 16Protocols for detection87 / 92

Baseline and deviation, in practice

  1. 1Know the expected protocol443 carries HTTPS, 22 SSH, 53 DNS.
  2. 2Identify the real protocolUse Zeek or an NGFW that classifies by content, not by port number.
  3. 3Know the host's roleClients should not serve SMB or answer DNS for peers.
  4. 4Know normal volume and rhythmHow much DNS data, how much ICMP payload, is usual here.
  5. 5Investigate the gapWhere the claim and the observed behavior differ.
The port tells you what traffic is supposed to be; content and behavior tell you what it is. The gap is where evasion hides.

Key takeaways

  1. 01Correlate source.ip, destination.port, network.transport and network.protocol: the port is only a claim.
  2. 02Hunt DNS tunneling by query length, query-type mix and unique subdomains per source, not blocklists alone.
  3. 03Find HTTPS beacons by interval regularity; when payload is encrypted, hunt missing SNI, odd certificates and ALPN.
