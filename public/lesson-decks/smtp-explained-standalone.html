Section 1 of 11

What SMTP is and its one job

Simple Mail Transfer Protocol: sending and relaying, not reading.

HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained6 / 76

Sending versus reading mail

The postal service

SMTP

Sends email and relays it between mail servers until it reaches the recipient's mailbox.

Opening your mailbox

IMAP / POP3

Retrieves stored messages when you open your inbox. IMAP (Internet Message Access Protocol) syncs mail on the server; POP3 (Post Office Protocol v3) downloads it off.

SMTP servers are the post offices that hand the letter along until it arrives.

Keep this split clear; mixing SMTP up with IMAP is a common beginner confusion. One property of SMTP, how easy it is to lie about the sender, is the root of a whole category of attacks.
HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained7 / 76

Every attack email is an SMTP delivery

  • PhishingA lure with a link or attachment, handed from server to server by SMTP like any other letter.
  • Malicious attachmentThe file rides inside the same message; SMTP moves it without looking at it.
  • Business email compromiseBEC: a fake request from a boss or supplier, often with no payload at all, only a believable sender.
  • The common rootSMTP lets the sender state who they are. Later sections show how analysts check that claim.
Tie the protocol to the alert queue: most of what a SOC calls phishing is simply SMTP doing its job for the wrong person.

Section 2 of 11

How SMTP works: the journey and the ports

A relay across several servers, and where security checks happen.

HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained9 / 76

The journey of one email

  1. 1SubmissionYour client hands the message to your organisation's outgoing mail server.
  2. 2MX lookupThat server finds the recipient domain's mail server via DNS MX records.
  3. 3RelayIt connects to the recipient's mail server and relays the message via SMTP.
  4. 4DeliveryRecipient server stores it; IMAP/POP3 later fetches it for reading.
MX means Mail Exchange: 'this server handles mail for example.com'.
HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained10 / 76

Step 2 up close: the MX lookup that picks the next hop

  • MX = Mail Exchange: the record that names a domain's inbound mail servers
  • Lower number = preferred: 10 is tried before 20, which is the backup
  • The A record turns the MX name into the IP the relay connects to on port 25
This is why DNS matters to mail security: whoever controls a domain's MX records controls where its inbound mail goes.
HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained11 / 76

The ports to know

PortUse
25Classic SMTP, server-to-server relay
587Modern submission from mail clients, with authentication and STARTTLS
465SMTP over TLS (SMTPS), another secure submission option
143 / 993IMAP, for retrieval
110 / 995POP3, for retrieval
The first three are SMTP; the last two are the retrieval companions.
HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained12 / 76

Port 25 from the wrong host is a signal

Mail servers

Expected

Your MX and outbound relay talk port 25 to other mail servers all day. Clients submit on 587 or 465 to your own server.

A workstation on 25

Suspicious

A laptop opening outbound port 25 straight to internet mail servers bypasses your relay: typical of spam malware or a script sending mail out.

Many networks block outbound 25 from everything except the mail servers, so a firewall deny on 25 is worth a look.

Firewall or NetFlow data showing a user subnet hitting tcp/25 externally is a classic outbound-abuse lead, and it pairs with the outbound volume signals in section 9.
HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained13 / 76
MAIL FROM and the From: header are separate, and both are simply stated by the sender.

Nothing in basic SMTP verifies either is truthful. A design choice from a small, trusted internet, and why forgery is so easy.

MAIL FROM is the envelope sender. From: is the header the user sees.
HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained14 / 76

Envelope versus letter: two sender fields

MAIL FROM

Envelope sender

Given in the SMTP conversation. Used for bounces, recorded as the Return-Path: header on delivery. The user rarely sees it.

From:

Header sender

Written inside the message in DATA. This is what the mail client shows the user, display name included.

The gap

The two can differ completely, and neither is checked by basic SMTP. Spoofing lives in that gap.

The postal analogy: the envelope address goes to the post office, the letterhead inside goes to the reader. A forger can write anything on either.

The full SMTP command set, recon included

About a dozen commands; a few are gifts to attackers.

HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained16 / 76

A full ESMTP submission, command by command

  • EHLO (ESMTP = Extended SMTP) greets and makes the server list every extension it supports
  • The visible From: lives inside DATA, separate from MAIL FROM
  • A lone dot on its own line ends the message
Plain SMTP greets with HELO; Extended SMTP uses EHLO, which is both a greeting and capability discovery.
HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained17 / 76

Commands and what they mean to an analyst

CommandPurposeAnalyst note

RCPT TODeclare a recipient (repeatable)Accepted for external domains from anyone = open relay
VRFYAsk whether a mailbox existsUsername enumeration; often disabled

AUTHAuthenticate the client (RFC 4954)Failed AUTH floods = credential guessing

EHLO's reply reveals STARTTLS and AUTH support, which attackers read to plan.
HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained18 / 76

Why VRFY and EXPN deserve attention

  • VRFY bobAsks the server to confirm whether bob is a real mailbox.
  • EXPN salesAsks it to list every member of the sales distribution list.
  • The riskA valid-username map before a phishing or password-spray campaign.
  • Good answer252 2.1.5 Cannot VRFY user: the server declines, recon denied.

A burst of VRFY/EXPN in mail logs is a recognisable reconnaissance signal.

Convenience features from the trusting early internet. Well-run servers disable them or return deliberately uninformative answers.
HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained19 / 76

What recon looks like in a mail transcript

  • No MAIL FROM, no DATA: the session exists only to ask questions
  • 252 = server declines to confirm the user: recon denied
  • 502 = command disabled. Both are the hardened answers you want
RFC 5321 defines 252 for exactly this: the server will not verify the user but would still try delivery. Point out how the EHLO reply also told the prober that AUTH is on offer.
HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained20 / 76

One VRFY is noise; a burst is reconnaissance

Usually benign

A single VRFY from a known monitoring host, or a mail admin testing one address from inside the network.

Reconnaissance

One external IP sending VRFY or EXPN for many names in seconds, then disconnecting without sending mail. Mapping valid users.

Next pivot: do the probed names later appear as phishing recipients or password-spray targets?

The value of spotting enumeration is the warning it gives: the same address list usually comes back as recipients of a campaign.
HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained21 / 76

AUTH guessing as it appears in a Postfix log

  • SASL is the framework behind the SMTP AUTH command
  • Same source IP, rapid repeats: guessing passwords against your mail server
  • A success line from that IP afterwards means a mailbox may be compromised
Failed AUTH floods are credential guessing. The interesting event is the first success after many failures, because that account can now send mail as an insider.
HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained22 / 76

Triage an AUTH failure flood

  1. 1Count and scopeHow many failures, from how many source IPs, against how many usernames?
  2. 2Look for a successAny successful AUTH from the same IP or for a targeted account is the escalation trigger.
  3. 3Check what it sentIf an account authenticated, review its outbound mail volume and recipients.
  4. 4ContainReset the password, enforce MFA where possible, and block or rate-limit the source.
Many sources and many usernames suggests spraying; one username with many passwords is brute force. The response priority is the same: find any success first.

Section 4 of 11

Reading SMTP reply codes

The first digit tells you whether to worry; the full code tells you what happened.

HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained24 / 76

Four reply categories by first digit

2yz Success

Accepted and completed: 250 Ok, 220 ready, 221 bye.

3yz Send more

Waiting for input: 354 after DATA.

4yz Try later

Transient: 421, 450/451/452. Basis of greylisting.

5yz Give up

Permanent: 550, 551, 552, 554. Retrying will not help.

Greylisting = reply 4yz to an unknown sender on purpose, betting real servers retry and most spam tooling gives up.

Greylisting deliberately returns a 4yz to unknown senders, betting that real mail servers retry and much spam tooling does not.
HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained25 / 76

Greylisting, step by step

  1. 1Unknown sender arrivesThe receiving server has never seen this combination of sending IP, sender and recipient.
  2. 2Deliberate 4yzIt replies with a temporary failure, such as 451, instead of accepting the mail.
  3. 3Real servers retryA standards-compliant mail server queues the message and tries again minutes later.
  4. 4Retry is acceptedThe second attempt gets 250. Much spam tooling never retries, so its mail is simply lost.
Greylisting explains why legitimate mail sometimes arrives late: a delay of several minutes on first contact is the cost of the filter, not a fault.
HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained26 / 76

Codes that tell an abuse story

CodeMeaningWhat it may point to
530Must issue STARTTLS / auth requiredA correctly locked-down submission server
550Rejected: bad recipient, blocklist, 'Relaying denied'Policy refusal or a closed relay working
554Transaction failedCatch-all permanent rejection, often a spam filter
421Service not availableOverload, or throttling of abusive senders
250 to external RCPT TOAccepted for a domain it should not relayOpen relay: urgent finding
A wave of outbound connections earning 550 and 554 can mean an internal host became a spam cannon. Repeated 530/421 to your own users usually means TLS or throttling policy, not an attack.
HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained27 / 76

Same codes, two very different stories

530 / 421 to your users

Policy at work

Your own users repeatedly hit 530 or 421: usually a TLS requirement or throttling policy doing its job, not an attack.

550 / 554 outbound wave

Host turned spam cannon

One internal host suddenly opens many outbound sessions and remote servers answer 550 and 554: it is being rejected for spam.

The first digit tells you whether to worry; the full code plus the direction of traffic tells you what happened.

Section 5 of 11

STARTTLS and open relays

Encryption that can be stripped, and trust that can be borrowed.

HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained29 / 76

Two ways SMTP gets encrypted

SMTPS, port 465

Implicit TLS

Encrypted from the very first byte, before any SMTP command. Needs a dedicated port.

Ports 25 and 587

STARTTLS

Starts in plaintext, then the client asks to upgrade the connection to TLS (RFC 3207).

After the upgrade, authentication and the message itself are encrypted.
HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained30 / 76

STARTTLS and how it gets stripped

  • An on-path attacker removes the 250-STARTTLS line: a downgrade
  • The client never sees TLS offered and continues in plaintext
  • RFC 8689 REQUIRETLS lets a sender refuse delivery instead of falling back
The weakness is in the name: opportunistic. SMTP traditionally prioritises delivery over guaranteed encryption.
HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained31 / 76

How a STARTTLS downgrade unfolds

  1. 1Plaintext startEvery STARTTLS session begins unencrypted, so the capability list travels in the clear.
  2. 2Line removed on the pathAn attacker between the servers deletes 250-STARTTLS from the server's reply.
  3. 3Client sees no TLSThe sender believes TLS is unavailable and, preferring delivery, continues in plaintext.
  4. 4Contents exposedCredentials for AUTH and the message itself cross the network readable. Neither side may notice.
The attacker never breaks encryption; they stop it from starting. That is why the fix is a policy that forbids fallback, not stronger ciphers.
HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained32 / 76

MTA-STS turns a stripped STARTTLS into a failed delivery

RFC 8461

MTA-STS

TXT record _mta-sts.example.com plus a policy file listing legitimate MX hosts and a mode of testing or enforce.

Sender with enforce cached

Refuses to deliver unless TLS succeeds with a certificate matching a listed MX. No silent plaintext fallback.

RFC 8460

TLS-RPT

_smtp._tls.example.com asks senders for daily JSON reports of TLS failures: where a SOC sees a downgrade attempt or a broken MX certificate.

MTA-STS is the fix most domains actually deploy for server-to-server stripping. The policy file lives at https://mta-sts.example.com/.well-known/mta-sts.txt.
HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained33 / 76

The DNS records behind MTA-STS and TLS-RPT

  • id= changes whenever the policy changes, telling senders to fetch it again
  • mode: enforce means no TLS with a matching certificate, no delivery
  • rua= is where daily TLS failure reports arrive for your SOC to read
max_age is how long senders cache the policy, in seconds; 604800 is one week. A domain in mode testing only reports failures and still delivers.
HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained34 / 76

SMTP smuggling: one session, a hidden second message

  • What it isDisclosed December 2023. Outbound and inbound servers disagree on where DATA ends, so a hidden second message rides along.
  • Why it mattersThe hidden message inherits the outbound server's SPF pass for a domain the attacker does not control.
  • CVEsCVE-2023-51764 (Postfix), CVE-2023-51765 (Sendmail), CVE-2023-51766 (Exim).
  • DefencePatching and strict end-of-data handling, such as Postfix's smtpd_forbid_bare_newline.
  • Investigative tellTwo messages logged from one SMTP session, the second with a different From: domain.
This is a transport-level issue: SPF passing does not prove the message is legitimate when the sending server itself was tricked into carrying it.
HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained35 / 76

Why smuggled mail passes SPF

  1. 1Outbound accepts the sessionThe attacker submits through a real outbound server that is allowed to send for its domain.
  2. 2Servers disagree on the endThe outbound server treats an unusual line ending as text; the inbound server treats it as end of DATA.
  3. 3Two messages arriveThe inbound server sees a second message with its own From:, carried on the same connection.
  4. 4SPF passesSPF checks the connecting IP, which belongs to the real outbound server, so the hidden message inherits its pass.
Describe the mechanism, not the bytes. The analyst takeaway is that an SPF pass proves which server connected, not that the message is honest.
HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained36 / 76

Testing for an open relay

  • 550 Relaying denied or 530 Must issue a STARTTLS command first: correctly configured
  • 250 OK and the mail is forwarded: open relay, treat as urgent
Open relays let mail appear to come from a reputable, uninvolved third party, and are regularly abused within hours of discovery.
HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained37 / 76

Closed relay versus open relay

Closed relay

Relays only mail it originated or mail for domains it hosts. External-to-external RCPT TO gets 550 or 530.

Open relay

Relays for anyone, to anywhere. Spam and phishing leave from your reputable IP, and your domain lands on blocklists.

Finding one of your own servers answering 250 to an external RCPT TO is an urgent configuration incident.

Attackers scan for open relays because the mail appears to come from an uninvolved third party, which hides their own infrastructure.

Section 6 of 11

Why email is so easy to forge

Basic SMTP believes whatever sender address it is told.

HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained39 / 76
An attacker can write From: ceo@yourcompany.com, and nothing in core SMTP stops it.

The engine behind most phishing and business email compromise. Architectural, not a bug.

SMTP was designed for a small, trusting network and never built in sender verification.
HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained40 / 76

Trust bolted on afterwards, published in DNS

Sender Policy Framework

SPF

The domain owner lists servers allowed to send for the domain; the receiver checks the sending server.

DomainKeys Identified Mail

DKIM

The sending server signs the message; the receiver verifies with a public key in DNS: authentic and unaltered.

Alignment and policy

DMARC

Ties SPF and DKIM to the visible From: and tells receivers what to do on failure: nothing, quarantine or reject.

Be sceptical of the From line. The real verification lives in the authentication results.
HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained41 / 76

How a receiving server checks one message

  1. 1SPFIs the connecting IP listed in the SPF record of the MAIL FROM domain?
  2. 2DKIMDoes the DKIM-Signature verify with the public key in the signing domain's DNS?
  3. 3DMARC alignmentDoes a passing SPF or DKIM domain match the visible From: domain?
  4. 4Apply the policyIf DMARC fails, follow the From: domain's policy: none, quarantine or reject.
  5. 5Write the verdictRecord all results in the Authentication-Results header for analysts to read.
Each check answers a different question. SPF and DKIM alone can pass for the attacker's own domain; DMARC is what ties them to the name the user sees.

SPF, DKIM and DMARC: reading the DNS records

The raw evidence behind every spf=pass or fail verdict.

HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained43 / 76

The three records side by side

All three are DNS TXT records. Walk each one left to right on the next slide.
HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained44 / 76

What each part of the records means

TokenMeaning
include:_spf.google.comAlso trust the servers Google's own SPF record authorises
-all vs ~allHard fail: unlisted servers are not authorised. Soft fail only marks them suspicious
s20250601._domainkeyDKIM selector: lets a domain rotate or run multiple signing keys
DKIM-Signature: d= s= b=Signature in the email's headers, verified against the message exactly as received

A domain still on ~all has weaker protection than one using -all.
HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained45 / 76
Alignment closes the gap: the domain that passed SPF or DKIM must match the visible From: domain.

Otherwise an attacker passes SPF for a domain they control while forging ceo@example.com.

Authentication-Results is the receiver's summary of checking all three records. spf=fail dkim=none dmarc=fail is a precise statement, not a vague warning.
HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained46 / 76

Passing is not the same as aligned

The forgery alignment stops

SPF pass, DMARC fail

Envelope from attacker-spf-domain.com, which the attacker controls, so SPF passes. Visible From: says ceo@example.com. Domains differ: DMARC fails.

Aligned

SPF pass, DMARC pass

Envelope and visible From: are both example.com, and the sending IP is in example.com's SPF record. The pass is about the name the user sees.

Without alignment the first message would technically pass SPF while still being a convincing forgery, which is exactly the gap DMARC was designed to close.
HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained47 / 76

Reading the Authentication-Results verdicts

ResultWhat it meansAnalyst reading
spf=passThe sending IP is allowed for the envelope domainSays which domain, not that it is yours: check alignment
spf=softfailNot listed, but the domain uses ~allSuspicious; weaker than a hard fail
dkim=noneThe message carried no DKIM signatureNo proof of origin or integrity at all
dkim=failA signature exists but does not verifyAltered in transit, forwarded, or forged
dmarc=failNo aligned SPF or DKIM pass for the From: domainThe visible sender is not proven: treat as spoofed until shown otherwise
Forwarding is the classic benign cause of dkim=fail or dmarc=fail, which is why ARC exists. Check the ARC i=1 result before calling a forwarded message forged.

MIME, attachments and links: what SMTP carries

SMTP only moves text lines; MIME (Multipurpose Internet Mail Extensions) layers everything richer on top.

HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained49 / 76

MIME structure is how you find the payload in a raw .eml

  • multipart/mixedboundary="b1_7f3c" splits parts at lines starting --b1_7f3c; multipart/alternative holds text and HTML bodies.
  • Content-Dispositionattachment; filename="Invoice_4471.docm" names the file; its Content-Type should describe it.
  • Type mismatchAn application/pdf type on a .html or .iso filename is a classic evasion tell.
  • base64 transfer encodingDecode the block to recover the file and hash it, then pivot to EDR and threat-intel lookups.
MIME is Multipurpose Internet Mail Extensions. All of these parts travel inside the same DATA transmission described earlier.
HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained50 / 76

A raw .eml with an attachment, as the analyst sees it

  • Each --b1_7f3c line starts a new part; the trailing -- ends the message
  • Type says PDF, filename says .html: the mismatch evasion tell
  • The base64 decodes to <html><body>: an HTML file, not a PDF
Decode the base64 block, save the file, hash it, and pivot: EDR for anyone who opened it, threat intel for the hash.
HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained51 / 76

Reading the parts: what each header tells you

HeaderTells youCheck
Content-Type: multipart/mixedThe message has several partsFind the boundary= value first
multipart/alternativeText and HTML versions of one bodyCompare them: hidden content can differ
Content-DispositionAttachment or inline, and the filenameIs the extension risky: .docm, .iso, .html?
Content-Type of the partWhat the sender claims the file isDoes it match the filename and real bytes?
Content-Transfer-EncodingHow bytes became text: usually base64Decode to recover and hash the file
Mail clients hide all of this. Opening the raw source is the only way to see the structure the attacker actually built.
HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained52 / 76

Two payload forms, carried with indifference to intent

Malicious attachment

Macro-enabled Office document, script with a document icon, or an archive or disk image (.zip, .iso, .img) hiding an executable.

Malicious link

In the HTML body, often a lookalike: micros0ft-support.com or login.microsoft.com.attacker-domain.com. Compare link text with the real href.

SMTP does not inspect either one, so detection happens at the mail gateway before delivery or at the endpoint when the file is opened.
HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained53 / 76

Link text versus the real target in the HTML body

  • The visible text shows a genuine Microsoft address
  • The href host ends in attacker-domain.com; the Microsoft part is a subdomain
  • Read hosts right to left: the registered domain is the last two labels
Users see the link text; the browser follows the href. Analysts should always read the href, and extract it for URL reputation checks.
HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained54 / 76

Triage question: which layer caught it, or missed it?

  1. 1AuthenticationRejected on SPF, DKIM or DMARC.
  2. 2Content scanningStopped by attachment or link scanning.
  3. 3The userDelivered, then reported by the user.
Asking which layer blocked or missed a given message is a routine part of triage.

Section 9 of 11

Why SMTP matters to a SOC analyst

Headers are the evidence trail; authentication results are the verdict.

HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained56 / 76

Phishing and BEC triage moves

  1. 1Read the authentication resultsCEO mail with spf=fail dkim=fail dmarc=fail is very likely spoofed.
  2. 2Trace the pathReceived: lines show which servers handled it and the originating IP.
  3. 3Spot mismatchesDisplay name vs From: vs envelope MAIL FROM: a common spoofing tell.
  4. 4Scope the campaignSearch the sender, domain and subject across all mailboxes to find every copy.
Reading authentication results is one of the fastest, highest-value moves in phishing triage. Scoping comes next because a reported email is rarely the only copy.
HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained57 / 76

Outbound volume: business as usual or abuse?

Expected

Newsletters from an approved marketing platform, sent from IPs listed in your SPF record, at a steady, scheduled volume.

Suspicious

A normal user account or a server that rarely sends mail suddenly emitting hundreds of messages to external domains, often with bounces.

A compromised mailbox is often used to phish the victim's own contacts, because those recipients trust the sender. The volume jump is the signal.
HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained58 / 76

Malicious mail activity in logs and traffic

  • Inbound threatsPhishing waves, attachments and links; gateway logs record senders, subjects, verdicts.
  • Outbound abuseAn internal account or server suddenly sending large volumes: spam, phishing, exfil.
  • Exfiltration by emailLarge or unusual outbound messages to external domains.
  • Relay abuse and spoofingTraffic patterns and auth failures reveal abused servers and impersonated domains.
SMTP itself is trusting and forgeable; the analyst applies the verification the protocol lacks.
HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained59 / 76

Hunting outbound bursts in Microsoft Defender

  • Count outbound mail per sender per hour
  • dcount of recipients separates one big thread from a spray
  • The threshold is a starting point: tune it to your normal senders
Run it, then exclude known bulk senders. What remains is a short list of accounts to check for compromise or exfiltration by mail.

The Received chain, Message-ID and MTA logs

Read the hops bottom-up, trust them top-down, and check what the mail server itself logged.

HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained61 / 76

The first header your own MX wrote is the trust boundary

  • Top hop is internal; the second is YOUR edge: real origin = 203.0.113.90
  • The bottom hop sits below the boundary: forgeable, and claims your own server
  • The Message-ID domain is the origin platform, not corp-example.com
Every server prepends a Received header. Attackers can add fake lower lines before sending. Timestamps should move forward; a big gap means queueing, going backwards means forgery or a bad clock.
HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained62 / 76

How to walk a Received chain

  1. 1Start at the topThe newest header was written by your own mailbox server: trusted.
  2. 2Find your edgeGo down to the first header by your MX with an external from.
  3. 3Take the originThat from host and IP is the real source of the message.
  4. 4Distrust what is belowLower lines were written by systems you do not control and can be forged.
  5. 5Check the clockTimes should move forward going up; going backwards suggests forgery.
Read bottom-up to tell the story, trust top-down to find the truth. Students often grab the bottom line as the origin, which is the one an attacker controls.
HACKTHESOCLesson 85SMTP explained63 / 76

Other headers that carry evidence

  • Message-IDSet by the originating system; its domain often reveals the real platform. In Microsoft 365 it is InternetMessageId.
  • X-Originating-IPAdded by some webmail platforms: the submitting client's IP. Many no longer add it, so absence proves nothing.
  • ARC = Authenticated Received ChainARC-Seal, ARC-Message-Signature, ARC-Authentication-Results, stamped i=1, i=2 per intermediary.
  • Why ARC existsForwarders and mailing lists rewrite mail and break SPF/DKIM; ARC preserves the first hop's pass, so i=1 shows it passed originally.
InternetMessageId is the key for finding every copy of one message across mailboxes. ARC exists because mailing lists and forwarders break SPF or DKIM.

Key takeaways

  1. 01SMTP sends and relays mail; IMAP and POP3 read it. MAIL FROM and From: are stated, never verified: the root of spoofing.
  2. 02VRFY and EXPN bursts are username enumeration; 250 to an external RCPT TO means an open relay.
  3. 03STARTTLS can be stripped; MTA-STS with TLS-RPT makes senders refuse plaintext, and patching fixes SMTP smuggling.
  4. 04SPF lists senders, DKIM signs, DMARC enforces alignment; in triage read Authentication-Results, not the From: line.
  5. 05Trust Received top-down: the first header your MX wrote is the real origin; MTA logs show what servers actually did.
  6. 06MIME boundaries, filenames and base64 let you extract and hash an attachment; a mismatched Content-Type is a tell.

Check your understanding, question 3 of 3

One external source issues a rapid series of VRFY and EXPN commands against many usernames before any message is sent. What is this, and why do hardened servers disable these commands?

A burst of VRFY and EXPN with no mail sent is recon for phishing or spraying, so hardened servers disable them. A relay test uses MAIL FROM and RCPT TO, greylisted senders just retry, and clients send RCPT TO, not VRFY.
