Every control protects systems. None of them directly protects the person who answers the phone, opens the email, or approves the prompt.

Social engineering manipulates that person into doing something they would not do if they had a moment to stop and think.

Stress it is not about careless people: the tactics are designed to short-circuit careful thinking. Ask of every incident: what human instinct did this rely on, and what process gap let it work?

Section 1 of 13

The psychology: authority, urgency, reciprocity, fear

Learn the levers, not the scam of the week.

HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering6 / 85

Four words this lesson uses constantly

  • PhishingThe umbrella term for tricking someone into handing over information, credentials, money or access. Email is only one channel.
  • PretextThe false but plausible story or identity: "I'm from IT", "I'm the CEO", "I'm a new vendor".
  • BECBusiness Email Compromise: impersonating an executive or vendor by email to move money. No malware at all.
  • OSINTOpen-Source Intelligence: LinkedIn, company sites, social media, old breaches, gathered to make a pretext convincing.
Every technique later in the lesson is a combination of these four. A pretext is the constant; only the costume changes.
HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering7 / 85

Four levers sit under every pretext

Psychological leverHow the attacker uses it
AuthorityImpersonates a manager, executive, IT or law enforcement so questioning the request feels risky
UrgencyCompresses the window to think critically with deadlines and imminent consequences
Reciprocity / helpfulnessOpens with a small, plausible favour to prime the target to reciprocate a bigger one
FearThreatens being fired, reported or blamed so compliance feels like the safer option
Urgency is why attacks cluster at end of day, end of week and quarter-close. Reciprocity is the most exploitable in a SOC context, because it is exactly the instinct help-desk staff are trained to have.
HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering8 / 85

Reading the lever in a reported message, in four questions

The triage habit this whole lesson builds, applied to any channel.

  1. 1What action is being asked?Pay, reset, approve, reply, scan, consent. The action is what the attacker wants; everything else is wrapping.
  2. 2Which levers are pulled?Name them: authority, urgency, reciprocity, fear. Two or more stacked on one request is the first warning.
  3. 3Which channel, and can it be checked?Email has headers and auth results; a phone call or SMS leaves far less. Know what evidence you will and will not have.
  4. 4Is there an independent way to verify?A number on file, a known approver, a ticket. A request that forbids verification has told you what it is.
Put the answers in the case notes. 'Authority plus urgency, wire request, no callback' is a better triage line than 'looks phishy'.
HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering9 / 85

Six channels, the same four levers

Attackers keep inventing new delivery mechanisms for the same manipulation, which is why recognising the lever beats memorising any specific scam.
HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering10 / 85

Real urgency tolerates a callback, manufactured urgency forbids one

Benign pressure

A real deadline from a known person through a normal channel. It survives a short delay to verify, and the requester expects you to check.

  • Arrives where such requests normally arrive
  • Payee or process unchanged
  • Requester happy to be called back

Manufactured pressure

The deadline exists to stop you thinking. The message pre-empts verification and asks for something outside the usual process.

  • "Don't call, I'm in meetings"
  • Secrecy plus a new payee or new device
  • Consequences threatened for delay
Both kinds of message use urgency, so urgency alone is not the signal. The signal is urgency combined with resistance to verification.

Section 2 of 13

Spear phishing and vishing

T1566.001, T1566.002 and T1598: same manipulation, different channel.

HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering12 / 85

Two email techniques, only one carries a payload

T1566.001 / .002

Spear phishing

Phishing tailored to one target: attachment (.001) or link (.002). Five minutes of LinkedIn lets it cite a real project, colleague or invoice number.

  • No spelling errors, no odd sender
  • Relies on the levers instead

T1598

Phishing for information

Reconnaissance only: get a reply confirming a job title, an extension, or that the mailbox is active and monitored.

  • No payload, so no malware or URL detection
  • Only defence: the target declines
A well-researched spear phish defeats the generic 'look for spelling errors' advice. T1598 is pure conversation and is easy to dismiss as a false positive, which is the mistake.
HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering13 / 85

A T1598 probe looks clean in EmailEvents, and that is the point

Fields from the Defender EmailEvents table for an information-gathering email.

FieldTypical valueHow to read it
UrlCount0No link: URL reputation and detonation have nothing to check
AttachmentCount0No file: sandboxing and AV have nothing to check
ThreatTypesemptyThe filter found no threat because there is no payload
IsFirstContact1First ever message from this sender to this person: worth a look
DeliveryActionDeliveredIt reached the inbox, so only the user or the SOC can stop the reply
When a user reports one, search for the same sender or subject across all recipients: reconnaissance is rarely sent to one person only.
HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering14 / 85

Vishing wins by keeping the target on the line

The same manipulation by phone, now helped by caller-ID spoofing and AI voice cloning.

  • Authority plus urgencyClaims to be IT, a bank or an executive, with a problem that needs fixing right now.
  • No time to verifyGuides the target step by step, because hanging up to call back the real number breaks the attack.
  • Voice is not proofA cloned voice from a short public sample can imitate a real CEO or board member.
Teach the instinct: a target who hangs up and calls the known number defeats the whole attack, however convincing the voice.
HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering15 / 85

A vishing call leaves little telemetry, so work from the follow-on

  1. 1The user reportOften the only record of the call: time, claimed identity, what was asked, what the user did.
  2. 2The phone system recordCaller number, called number, time and duration. Caller ID can be spoofed, so it shows when, not who.
  3. 3The action that followedLook minutes later: a password change, an MFA approval, a remote-support tool installed, a new sign-in.
  4. 4Contain and widenReset what the caller obtained, revoke sessions, then ask the service desk who else got a similar call.
The call itself is invisible to the SIEM. What the caller got the user to do is not: that is where the evidence is.

Section 3 of 13

Help-desk impersonation and the MFA reset

The Scattered Spider pattern, documented in a CISA joint advisory.

HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering17 / 85

How the help-desk MFA reset attack runs

  1. 1Research a real employeeName, department, sometimes the employee's real phone number, spoofed as caller ID.
  2. 2Call the help deskReport a lost phone or lockout that blocks the MFA challenge.
  3. 3Ask for a resetReset the MFA enrolment or add a new device to the account.
  4. 4Sign in as the employeeFully authenticated session: no malware, no CVE, frequently no alert of any kind.
No password guessed, no hash stolen. To every technical system a legitimate MFA reset just happened through the legitimate process.
HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering18 / 85

The signal is who reset the factor, and what happened next

  • Admin security-info operations: a factor registered, updated or deleted for a user
  • Join forward to a successful sign-in by the same target within the hour
  • Incident = reset, then first-ever sign-in from a new country or unmanaged device
An MFA reset alone is a perfectly normal help-desk action. The pairing is what makes it an incident, so never alert on the reset by itself.
HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering19 / 85

Reading the reset-then-sign-in pair field by field

FieldExample valueWhat it tells you
OperationNameAdmin registered security infoSomeone with admin rights added an MFA method for another user

Every row alone is routine. The story is the sequence: admin reset, then a successful sign-in from a new place on an unknown device.
HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering20 / 85

Same audit event, two very different stories

Legitimate reset

Ticket shows a callback to the number on file. The next sign-in comes from the user's usual city on a managed device.

  • Ticket and callback recorded
  • Usual location and device
  • User confirms when asked

Impersonation

Ticket says the caller was in a hurry and verified by name and department only. The new factor is used within the hour from a new country.

  • No callback, or a number the caller gave
  • New country, unmanaged device
  • Real user knows nothing about it
The help-desk ticket is evidence. Pull it before you decide, and record what verification was actually done.
HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering21 / 85

Responding to a suspected help-desk impersonation

  1. 1Confirm with the real employeeCall them on the number already on file. No request made? Treat the account as compromised.
  2. 2Cut the sessionRevoke sign-in sessions, remove the method the caller registered, and reset the password.
  3. 3Scope what the session didMailbox rules, file access, new app consents, further MFA changes, admin actions.
  4. 4Hunt for repeatsOther admin security-info resets in the same window, and other desk calls with the same story.
  5. 5Feed back to the deskShare the call pattern so technicians recognise the next attempt.
Removing the attacker's registered method matters: resetting the password alone leaves their factor on the account.
HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering22 / 85

The fix is process, not a technology purchase

Callback verification

Call the employee back on a number already on file, never one the caller gives, before any change to authentication settings.

Second channel

Independent verification: a manager confirms, or a video call where the employee is recognised.

Authority to say no

Explicit cover for staff to refuse or escalate a request that feels off, without being blamed for poor service.

A help desk measured purely on speed and friendliness is structurally set up for this attack to succeed.

The technician's incentives (tickets closed fast, users happy) work against interrogating callers. A counter-incentive for caution is part of the control.

Section 4 of 13

Smishing and QR-code phishing (quishing)

Still T1566, with the payload moved where email security cannot see it.

HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering24 / 85

Both route around the corporate email stack

SMS phishing

Smishing

Read on a phone where URLs are hard to inspect, often on a personal device outside the email gateway and URL filtering.

  • Delivery, bank, or fake IT / payroll notice
  • Personal number from a data broker or breach

QR-code phishing

Quishing

The URL is an image, not text a reputation scanner or secure email gateway can read. A phone camera takes the session off corporate monitoring and DNS filtering.

  • "Scan to complete mandatory training"
  • "Scan to view this secure document"
The gap is analyst muscle memory: someone trained only on headers and URLs has no reflex for a payload that arrived as an image scanned by a device that never touched our network.
HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering25 / 85

What a quishing email still reveals to the mail stack

EvidenceWhat to look for
UrlCount low or 0No clickable link in the body, yet the message asks the user to go somewhere
Image or PDF attachmentThe QR code is a picture: the destination is not text in the message
EmailUrlInfo UrlLocationWhere Defender extracts it, a URL found in a QR code is recorded as QRCode
Subject and senderMandatory training, document share or MFA renewal, from a first-time sender
The smish itself never reaches your logs if it went to a personal phone. Your evidence then starts at the sign-in that follows.
HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering26 / 85

Following a smish or quish from the report to the sign-in

  1. 1Collect the reportScreenshot of the SMS or the email with the QR code, the time, and whether the user entered anything.
  2. 2Find every recipientSame sender, subject or attachment across mailboxes. Smishing waves often hit many staff at once.
  3. 3Check the sign-insFor those users: successful sign-ins from a new IP, a mobile browser, or an unmanaged device soon after.
  4. 4ContainRevoke sessions, reset the password, block the destination domain in proxy and DNS filtering, purge the email.
A user who scanned but entered nothing is low risk. A user who entered credentials is a compromised account until sessions are revoked.
HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering27 / 85

Benign and malicious QR codes look identical: context decides

Benign

Published by the organisation through a known channel and resolving to a known domain. It never leads to a sign-in prompt for corporate credentials.

  • Posted in the office or intranet
  • Known company domain

Suspicious

Arrives by email or SMS from an unknown sender, adds urgency, and lands on a page asking for corporate credentials or an MFA code.

  • Unsolicited, first-time sender
  • Ends at a login page

Awareness rule: a QR code that ends at a login page is treated like a link in an email, and reported.

Teach users to check where a code leads before signing in, and that the corporate login is reached by typing the known address, not by scanning.

MFA fatigue and pretexting (T1621)

Manipulation that targets an MFA prompt, not a technical exploit of MFA.

HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering29 / 85
MFA fatigue does not break MFA. The attacker already has the password and spams push prompts until the user taps approve.

T1621, Multi-Factor Authentication Request Generation, sits under Credential Access, in the same family as vishing, not pass-the-hash.

Repetition manufactures urgency (make it stop) and borrows authority (the system is asking), and there is no human on the other end to question.
HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering30 / 85

Many denied pushes, then one approval

  • 500121 = the MFA challenge failed or was refused by the user
  • 0 = sign-in succeeded
  • Five or more refusals and at least one approval in 15 minutes is the tell
Read it as behaviour, not error. The password already worked on every one of these attempts.
HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering31 / 85

The sign-in result codes that tell the MFA fatigue story

ValueMeaningWhat it tells the analyst
ResultType 50126Invalid username or passwordAbsent here: the attacker already has the right password
ResultType 50074Strong authentication is requiredFirst factor passed, MFA challenge triggered
ResultType 500121Strong authentication failedThe push was denied or not completed
ResultType 0SuccessAfter many 500121s, this is where the user gave in
AuthenticationRequirementmultiFactorAuthenticationThe policy demanded MFA, so the password alone was not enough
The missing 50126 failures matter: a burst of MFA denials with no password failures means the credential is already stolen. Reset it even if no push was approved.
HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering32 / 85

One denied record: the user saying no

  • The refusals ARE the user defending the account
  • IPAddress is not their usual egress
  • Minutes apart, overnight. The final approval is where they gave up
Interview the user: approving 'because it would not stop' with no login attempt of their own confirms the pattern.
HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering33 / 85

A few denials are normal. A burst ending in approval is not

Benign

One or two failures from the user's usual IP while they sign in on a new laptop, followed by success from the same place.

  • Usual IP and location
  • Working hours
  • User confirms the sign-in

MFA fatigue

Many denials minutes apart, overnight, from an IP the user never uses, then one success from that same IP.

  • Unfamiliar IP or country
  • Out of hours, rapid repeats
  • User did not try to sign in
Check the IP of the final success. If it matches the IP of the denied attempts and not the user's own, the attacker holds the session.
HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering34 / 85

Triage and response for a suspected MFA fatigue attack

  1. 1Ask the userWere you signing in? Did you approve a prompt to make it stop? Use a channel other than the account.
  2. 2Revoke and resetRevoke sessions and refresh tokens, reset the password: the password is compromised whatever happened.
  3. 3Review the sessionIf one was approved: mailbox rules, new MFA methods, app consents, file access from that IP.
  4. 4Find the password sourceLeaked credential, earlier phish, reuse. Otherwise the attacker simply tries again.
Thank the user for every denial. They defended the account many times before the one mistake.
HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering35 / 85

Countermeasures, and the structural gap behind pretexting

Number matching

The user must enter the number shown on the login screen into the authenticator, so they have to be looking at the real login attempt.

Rate limiting

Cap how many MFA challenges can be sent to one account in a short window.

Pretexting as a category

No callback or second-channel check for sensitive requests, plus a culture that rewards fast and helpful unconditionally.

Pretexting is the umbrella: a fabricated identity, situation or justification built to obtain what would otherwise be refused. Every technique here is a pretext in a different costume.
HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering36 / 85

What users are told, and what they should do

  • An unexpected prompt is an alarmIf you did not just try to sign in, the password is already known to someone else. Deny, then report.
  • Report it, do not just ignore itMicrosoft Authenticator offers "Report suspicious activity", which Entra ID Protection records as a user-risk detection.
  • Read the contextWith number matching and additional context, the prompt shows the app and location: check them before entering the number.
  • Nobody legitimate asks for a codeIT never phones to ask you to approve a prompt or read out a code. That call is the attack.
Pair the attack with a call: attackers often phone during the push storm posing as IT. The awareness rule closes both halves.

Section 6 of 13

Business Email Compromise: no malware at all

The costliest category the FBI's IC3 tracks, with nothing for antivirus to catch.

HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering38 / 85

BEC is a pure social-engineering chain

The most expensive attacks often have the least hacking in them. That is the lesson to land here.
HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering39 / 85

Four steps from research to an unrecoverable wire

  1. 1Research (OSINT)Who approves payments, the CFO's name, an active supplier, the finance team's rhythm.
  2. 2ImpersonateLookalike domain (swapped character, rn posing as m), spoofed display name or hijacked mailbox. Reply-To redirects.
  3. 3PressureAuthority, urgency and secrecy: "keep it confidential, don't call, just wire the funds".
  4. 4The transferFunds go to the attacker's "new banking details", often overseas and unrecoverable within hours.
Variants recur: CEO fraud, vendor or invoice fraud (VEC), payroll diversion, and gift-card scams.
HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering40 / 85

Spoofed sender or hijacked mailbox: different evidence

EvidenceLookalike or spoofedCompromised real mailbox
SPF / DKIM / DMARCFail, or pass for the lookalike domainPass: it came from the real server
Sender addressSwapped character or different domainExactly right
Where to lookHeaders and IsFirstContactSign-ins and audit log of the real sender
TelltaleReply-To to an outside mailboxNew inbox rules hiding replies from the owner
Do not close a BEC report because authentication passed. Passing auth with a payment-change request points to a compromised mailbox, which is worse.
HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering41 / 85

A hijacked mailbox hides the conversation with an inbox rule

Unified audit log record for a rule created from the attacker's session (simplified).

  • Near-empty name and an unused folder: built to be invisible to the owner
  • Payment keywords: replies from finance are hidden from the real CFO
  • ClientIP not the CFO's usual address: pivot to SigninLogs from it
Legitimate rules have readable names and sort newsletters. A rule that hides payment mail from its own owner is a strong BEC indicator.
HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering42 / 85

Where BEC detection lives, and what actually stops it

Email signals

Metadata and the story it tells.

  • Lookalike or newly-registered domain
  • Reply-To differs from From
  • External banner on an "internal" exec
  • First-time sender, urgency + secrecy + new payee

The control

Out-of-band verification: confirm any banking change or urgent wire by calling a known number, never one supplied in the email.

With no malware, the human is the sensor. The process is the defence.
HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering43 / 85

BEC response: the money first, then the mailbox

  1. 1Stop or recall the paymentFinance contacts the bank immediately. Recovery chances fall fast after the first hours.
  2. 2Verify out of bandCall the executive or vendor on a known number to confirm the request is false.
  3. 3Preserve the evidenceFull headers, the message, the payment instruction, and audit logs for the sender if internal.
  4. 4Check for compromiseIf the sender is internal: sign-ins, inbox rules, forwarding, app consents. Revoke and reset.
  5. 5Find other targets and reportSame sender or bank details sent elsewhere; report to law enforcement, e.g. FBI IC3.
The SOC's first call in BEC is often to finance, not to a tool. Agree that contact path before the incident.

Section 7 of 13

Worked example: reading a spoofed email's headers

Thirty seconds with "view original" often closes a BEC case before money moves.

HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering45 / 85

Three fields and three checks do the real work

Field or checkWhat it tells you
FromWhat the client displays. Often freely set unless the receiver enforces authentication
Reply-ToSeparate from From. If it differs, every reply goes to the attacker
Received chainRead bottom to top: the servers the message physically passed through
SPFWas the sending server authorised to send mail for that domain?
DKIMCryptographic signature proving the message was not altered after sending
DMARCWhat to do when SPF or DKIM fail. p=reject means refuse it outright
The display name is just text the sender chose. Read the address underneath character by character, because a swapped character looks identical at normal reading size.
HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering46 / 85

A "CEO" email that never touched company.co

  • The a in the From domain is a lookalike character, not company.co
  • Reply-To sends every answer to an outside mailbox
  • SPF fail, no DKIM, DMARC fail: confirmation, not suspicion
Read the Received chain bottom-up: the message came from an unrelated relay and never touched company.co's own mail infrastructure.
HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering47 / 85

Not every authentication failure is an attack

Benign failure

Forwarded or mailing-list mail can break SPF while DKIM still passes. A small vendor with a misconfigured SPF record fails on every message.

  • Consistent with the sender's history
  • No new payee, no pressure

Malicious failure

DMARC fails for a domain claiming to be your own CEO, the From domain is a lookalike, and Reply-To points to a free mailbox.

  • Lookalike or new domain
  • Reply-To differs from From
  • Request to move money or data
Weigh the failure against the request. A failed check on a newsletter is noise; the same failure on a wire request is decisive.
HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering48 / 85

The thirty-second header read, in order

  1. 1Address, not display nameRead the From address character by character. The display name is free text.
  2. 2Reply-To and Return-PathDo they point somewhere other than the From domain? Where do replies and bounces go?
  3. 3Received, bottom to topWhich server first handled it? Did it ever touch your own domain's mail servers?
  4. 4Authentication-ResultsSPF, DKIM and DMARC as your receiving server judged them.
  5. 5Decide and recordNote each finding in the case, then block the sender and search for other recipients.
Doing it in the same order every time is what makes it fast. Students should be able to do this blind.

Section 8 of 13

OSINT: the reconnaissance behind the pretext

"They knew my manager's name" is usually diligent public research, not an insider.

HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering50 / 85

Ordinary, public sources attackers mine

  • LinkedIn and company siteNames, roles, reporting lines, org charts, who sits in finance, and job posts that reveal your SIEM.
  • Social mediaA holiday post says when someone is unreachable and a stand-in might rush their approvals.
  • Breach dumpsEmail formats, real credentials to try, and which staff reuse passwords.
  • Technical footprintsDocument metadata, DNS records, job details, and out-of-office replies naming a backup contact.
This is the Reconnaissance tactic: T1589 Gather Victim Identity Information, T1591 Gather Victim Org Information, T1598 Phishing for Information.
HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering51 / 85

Where reconnaissance becomes visible to the defender

Recon activityWhat you may seeAction
Probing emails (T1598)Payload-free first-contact emails asking for detailsSearch all recipients, block the sender
Calls fishing for detailsService-desk notes of callers asking who handles paymentsLog and share the pattern with the desk
Breached credentialsEntra ID Protection "Leaked credentials" risk detectionForce a password reset
Auto-replies to outsidersOut-of-office replies naming a backup contactLimit external auto-replies
Most OSINT is invisible because it happens on other people's websites. These are the few places where it touches your telemetry.
HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering52 / 85

Two consequences for the analyst

Assume OSINT first

A personalised lure is not evidence of an insider. Escalate to an insider hypothesis only when specifically non-public information was used.

Deny the payoff

Trim what job posts and social accounts reveal, but mainly make the wire, password or MFA approval impossible on information alone.

The recon is unavoidable. What you deny them is the action at the end of it.

Section 9 of 13

Beyond email: tailgating, baiting, deepfakes

Vectors that never touch an email gateway at all.

HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering54 / 85

Physical and synthetic vectors

Tailgating

Following an authorised person through a secured door, carrying boxes or in a delivery uniform. Exploits the awkwardness of refusing.

T1091, T1200

Baiting

A USB drive labelled "Salaries 2026" left in a car park. Defeated by culture: an unknown USB goes to security, never into a port.

Deepfake voice and video

A cloned executive voice, or a staged video call of deepfaked colleagues: the 2024 case cost roughly US$25 million.

Deepfakes are BEC's evolution: they defeat the 'just call to verify' instinct because the voice on the line sounds real.
HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering55 / 85

Tailgating shows up in access-control logs, if you look

SignalBenign explanationSuspicious pattern
Door held open alarmDeliveries or moving furnitureRepeated at a secure door, no one badged in
Anti-passback violationBadge not read on the way inBadge used to leave with no recorded entry

Correlating physical access with logons is a powerful insider and tailgating check, but only if the two systems' logs reach the SIEM.
HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering56 / 85

Baiting: the found USB drive leaves an audit trail

Illustrative, trimmed 6416: the real event also carries Subject, Device ID, Class ID and Vendor IDs.

  • 6416 needs "Audit PNP Activity" enabled; EDR device control adds more detail
  • A finance workstation, early morning: who plugged it in, and why?
  • Next look: process and file events from the drive in the following minutes
Response: isolate the host if anything executed, collect the drive for security, and ask the user where it came from. Device control policy blocks most of this.
HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering57 / 85

Triage for a suspected deepfake voice or video request

  1. 1Pause the actionHold the payment or access change. Real executives accept a short delay; a fake depends on speed.
  2. 2Verify on a trusted channelCall back a known number, or use the pre-agreed code word. Never the number or link in the request.
  3. 3Collect the evidenceMeeting invite and join logs, external or guest participants, call records, the payment instruction.
  4. 4Check the real executiveIs their mailbox or account compromised too? A fake meeting invite often comes from one.
The evidence of a deepfake is rarely in the audio. It is in the request being outside the normal process, and in the invite metadata.
HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering58 / 85

Defence rests on a process the fake cannot satisfy

  • Independent channelVerify through a pre-agreed channel, not the one the request arrived on, e.g. a callback to a number you already trust.
  • Shared code wordFor high-value voice or video requests, so identity rests on a secret, not a face or a voice.
  • Process over impressionDual authorisation on payments, badge-in discipline, USB restrictions: none depend on judging if a face is genuine.
Convincing evidence, a header, a name, now a face and a voice, can all be manufactured. Robust defence never rests on recognising the fake.

Whaling and watering-hole attacks (T1189)

Narrow the target list to one executive, or target nobody and wait on the path.

HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering60 / 85

Opposite directions, different detection

T1566.001 / .002

Whaling

Spear phishing aimed at a CEO, CFO, General Counsel or board member. A compromised real CFO mailbox often launches BEC that passes lookalike and SPF/DKIM/DMARC checks.

T1189, Drive-by Compromise

Watering hole

Compromise a site the target community trusts, then exploit visitors' browsers. No email, no attachment, no decision beyond visiting.

Whaling is the same technique with a different payoff: board data, M&A details and the authority to approve a wire without a second signature.
HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering61 / 85

When the whale's own mailbox sends the fraud

A compromised executive account passes every sender check, so look at the account instead.

  • Authentication passesSPF, DKIM and DMARC pass because the real server sent it. Do not treat a pass as clearance.
  • Executive sign-insNew country, unmanaged device, or an MFA reset on the executive's account shortly before.
  • Mailbox changesNew inbox rules, forwarding to external addresses, or a new app consent with mail scopes.
  • Unusual recipientsIsFirstContact can still fire: an exec writing to a junior finance clerk for the first time.
Executives are high-value and often exempted from controls for convenience. Those exemptions are exactly what whaling exploits.
HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering62 / 85

Watering hole: the first signal is the exploit itself

  • A browser parent spawning PowerShell is the tell
  • Hidden window plus encoded command, moments after browsing
  • Ask what site they were on: a legitimate site, quietly compromised
This alert gets dismissed as a false positive unless the analyst asks what the user was browsing right before. The browser is telling the truth; the site it visited was lying.

The four levers map onto a rigorously studied model, plus three more.

ReciprocityA small favour first, to prime a bigger one laterOpening psychology

HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering67 / 85

Spotting the three new levers in a reported message

Social proof

Claims about what colleagues already did that the recipient cannot check, such as a percentage of the team or a fake reply thread.

Liking

A new outside contact building rapport over several harmless messages before the real request arrives.

Commitment

A trivial confirmation first, then a bigger related request a few messages later in the same thread.

Liking and commitment only show up across a thread, not in one message. Ask the reporter for the whole conversation, not just the last email.
HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering68 / 85
Notice when a message is working unusually hard to make you feel something.

Strong pretexts stack levers: authority + urgency + scarcity is the classic wire-fraud combination. Routine business rarely needs more than one.

That is better advice than 'look for one red flag'. Rapport-building over several benign messages is the attacker investing in liking before spending it.

Consent phishing: access without a password

An illicit OAuth consent grant survives password resets and MFA.

HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering70 / 85

How an illicit consent grant works

  1. 1Register an appThe attacker registers an application with the identity provider, e.g. Entra ID.
  2. 2Request plausible scopesRead mail, access files, read calendar, like a productivity add-in.
  3. 3Send the consent linkThe victim sees the real Microsoft or Google login page. Padlock, domain and MFA all check out.
  4. 4User clicks consentThe app gets an OAuth token scoped to the approval, independent of the user's password.
The only thing the victim actually evaluates is a blandly worded permissions screen, which most users click through. A live Mail.Read grant lets the attacker watch for BEC opportunities.
HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering71 / 85

Hunting consent grants in Entra ID audit logs

Hunt for an unfamiliar or newly registered app, outside your approved set, granted high-privilege scopes by a user with no reason to install an add-in that day.
HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering72 / 85

What separates a risky consent from a routine one

CheckRoutineRisky
Who consentedAdmin, as part of approved onboardingOrdinary user, IsAdminConsent false
ApplicationKnown, verified publisher, on the approved listUnfamiliar name, unverified, newly seen
ScopesNarrow: sign-in and basic profileMail.Read, Files.ReadWrite.All, offline_access

offline_access is the persistence scope: it lets the app keep refreshing tokens without the user present.
HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering73 / 85

The grant is the compromise

Flagged record

grantedBy a.desai@corp.example, an ordinary user. appName "Quick Notes Sync", never IT-approved. scopes "Mail.Read offline_access": reads mail indefinitely.

Wrong fix

Reset the password and re-register MFA. The attacker never authenticated as the user, so the app's standing grant is untouched.

Right fix

Find and revoke the application's consent grant. Prevent with admin-only consent, and train that a consent screen deserves password-page scrutiny.

This is the common remediation mistake: closing the ticket after a password reset leaves the attacker's access fully intact.
HACKTHESOCLesson 30Social engineering74 / 85

Removing an illicit consent grant, step by step

  1. 1Identify the appNote the application and service principal ID from the consent event.
  2. 2Revoke the grantRemove the delegated permission grants and disable or delete the enterprise application.
  3. 3Revoke tokensRevoke the user's sessions and refresh tokens so already-issued tokens stop working.
  4. 4Scope the accessReview what the app read: mail access and file activity by that app ID.
  5. 5Close the doorRestrict user consent to admin-approved apps, and search for other users who granted it.
If other users consented to the same app, each grant must be removed. Search by app ID, not by user.

Make the recipient the sensor, not the point of failure

Continuous, scenario-based

Regular simulated phishing (SANS: monthly at minimum) improves click rates and reporting rates. A once-a-year slideshow measurably fails.

Normalised Reporting Score

SANS NRS: percentage who correctly reported a simulated phish, minus the percentage who fell for it. Every report becomes SOC intelligence.

Reinforce, weight by role

Celebrate reporters, pair a failed simulation with a blame-free micro-lesson. Train finance, HR, executive assistants and executives more often.

Punishment teaches people to hide mistakes, the opposite of what a SOC needs mid-incident. A silently deleted phish leaves the SOC blind to the campaign.

From one user report to the whole campaign

  1. 1User reportsThe report button sends the message, with headers, to the SOC queue.
  2. 2Triage the sampleHeaders, auth results, levers, payload or none. Confirm it is malicious.
  3. 3Find every copySearch EmailEvents by sender, subject, URL or attachment hash across all mailboxes.
  4. 4RemediateRemove the copies, block sender and domain, check who clicked, replied or signed in.
  5. 5Close the loopTell the reporter it was real and thank them. That feedback drives the next report.
One report can protect hundreds of mailboxes, but only if the SOC searches beyond the reporter's inbox.

Key takeaways

  1. 01Every pretext pulls the same levers: authority, urgency, reciprocity and fear, extended by Cialdini's social proof, liking and commitment.
  2. 02T1598 has no payload to detect; help-desk MFA resets look legitimate. Callback on a number on file is the control.
  3. 03Smishing and quishing move the payload to a personal phone or an image that email security cannot read.
  4. 04MFA fatigue (T1621) shows as many 500121 denials then one success; number matching and rate limiting counter it.
  5. 05BEC uses no malware: read From, Reply-To, Received and SPF/DKIM/DMARC, and verify any payment change out of band.
  6. 06Consent phishing survives password resets: hunt Consent to application events and revoke the grant.
